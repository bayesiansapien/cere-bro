1. Monolithic 3D integration of ALD oxide semiconductors on 200-mm wafers
Publication: Peer-reviewed research article, Nature Nanotechnology, 15 September 2026
Authors: Chang Niu et al.
Process area: ALD, monolithic 3D integration and manufacturing scale-up
Key finding: Three tiers of ALD indium-oxide transistors were monolithically integrated on 200-mm silicon wafers. More than 100,000 devices were fabricated, spanning ferroelectric, enhancement-mode and depletion-mode FETs. Reported threshold-voltage standard deviation reached 0.04 V, while average mobility was as high as 91.6 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. Cross-tier circuits were functional. A four-tier computing-in-memory accelerator designed with a custom InOₓ PDK delivered modeled 1.4–2.9× speed improvements, with comparable energy-delay-product gains over 2D baselines.
Why it matters: This moves oxide-semiconductor M3D beyond isolated low-temperature devices toward wafer-scale statistical evidence, process-design-kit support and multilayer circuit operation.
Engineering implications: The combination of ALD uniformity, low thermal budget and tight \(V_T\) distribution is relevant to sequential integration above silicon logic. Remaining HVM questions include cumulative thermal exposure, inter-tier alignment, contact resistance, device drift, bias-stress reliability, particle adders and yield loss compounded across tiers. Full text may require subscription access.

2. Low-GWP carbonyl fluorocarbons for SiO₂ plasma etching
Publication: Peer-reviewed research article, ECS Journal of Solid State Science and Technology, 15 September 2026
Authors: Minuk Kim, Inkyoung Cho, Dongjun Jeon and Chang-Koo Kim
Process area: Plasma etch chemistry and emissions reduction
Key finding: C₂F₄O, C₄F₈O and C₅F₁₀O—reported with GWPs near or below unity—were compared with CHF₃, whose stated GWP was 14,600, in inductively coupled SiO₂ etching. C₂F₄O/Ar produced the lowest etch rate, whereas C₄F₈O/Ar and C₅F₁₀O/Ar approached CHF₃/Ar performance. XPS showed steady-state fluorocarbon films thinner than 4 Å for all gases. The rate differences therefore correlated primarily with radical populations rather than large differences in passivation-film thickness.
Why it matters: It demonstrates that very-low-GWP feed gases can approach incumbent dielectric-etch productivity without relying on a thick polymer layer.
Engineering implications: C₄F₈O and C₅F₁₀O merit patterned-wafer evaluation for selectivity, profile control, ARDE, residue and chamber-wall conditioning. Matching blanket etch rate is insufficient: dissociation products, abatement compatibility, gas delivery stability and polymer memory will determine manufacturing viability.

3. Tone-switchable Sn-based MLD dry resist with 7-nm e-beam patterning
Publication: Peer-reviewed research article, ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces, 18 September 2026
Authors: Xingkun Wang et al.
Process area: EUV resist deposition, development and reaction mechanisms
Key finding: A molecular-layer-deposited Sn hybrid resist based on tetrakis(dimethylamino)tin and 2-butene-1,4-diol switched tone according to development chemistry. Ammonia wet development produced positive tone; fluorine-based ICP dry development produced negative tone. With 50-keV electron-beam exposure, wet development resolved a 7-nm single line. EUV testing resolved an 18-nm single line. In-situ XPS, TEM, EDS, irradiated-species mass spectrometry and DFT were used to connect exposure-induced bond breaking and network reorganization to the tone change.
Why it matters: A single vapor-deposited material supporting wet-positive and dry-negative operation offers an additional integration variable for balancing sensitivity, swelling, roughness and pattern-transfer resistance.
Engineering implications: Dry development could reduce liquid-induced collapse, while MLD offers composition and thickness control. Required next evidence includes dense-line resolution, dose-to-size, LER/LWR, stochastic failures, outgassing, substrate selectivity and across-wafer defectivity. Despite the title’s “sub-7 nm” language, the accessible abstract reports 7 nm under e-beam and 18 nm under EUV.

4. Dilute-HF pretreatment exposes otherwise invisible silicon-wafer defects
Publication: Peer-reviewed open-access research article, Semiconductor Science and Technology, 14 September 2026
Authors: Kaisla Sutinen et al.
Process area: Incoming-wafer inspection and yield control
Key finding: A brief ex-situ dilute-HF treatment before photoluminescence imaging substantially increased defect contrast. Scratches shallower than 1 µm became visible, whereas untreated-wafer imaging could miss microcracks extending to approximately 150 µm depth. Controlled timing also allowed defect-depth estimation and, with illumination from both sides, identification of the nearer wafer surface.
Why it matters: Subsurface cracks and shallow handling damage can seed breakage, stacking faults or downstream yield loss but remain difficult to screen nondestructively.
Engineering implications: The method could augment incoming inspection without the material removal and contamination concerns of Wright etching. Manufacturing qualification should examine HF concentration and timing windows, native-oxide regrowth, metallic contamination, surface termination, sensitivity across resistivity ranges and compatibility with automated handling. The accompanying dataset is publicly available.

## 2. Where I stand on RSI

**From:** Interconnects by Nathan Lambert <robotic@substack.com>  
**Date:** Sat, 19 Sep 2026 15:42:20 +0000  
**Link:** [https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/1a0ba55a79c99518](https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/1a0ba55a79c99518)

View this post on the web at https://www.interconnects.ai/p/where-i-stand-on-rsi

We’re in an era where a few organizations are using thousands of concurrent agents to improve their processes and output. These organizations happen to be just the frontier AI labs, in particular OpenAI and Anthropic. In the last few weeks, I’ve been pondering what it means for so many employees across these organizations to rapidly update their expectations for the pace of AI progress and associated risks.
A core perspective I have is that the frontier labs and broader frenetic, competitive culture in the San Francisco AI scene set up an environment that amplifies any AI concern. This has some benefits in causing more general audience awareness of AI, as fear sells, but exaggerating risk timelines or severity will have negative second-order effects. I remember many loud AI safety debates, and their associated clouds over the viability of open-source AI, in 2023 and 2024 — the primary risks then did not arrive in the forecasted timelines.
The general populace of these two key labs was very anxious about AI risks and the rate of progress even a year ago, and especially as agents got stronger product-market fit at the start of 2026. This cultural precondition, when exposed to the reality that thousands of agents will constantly be working fairly productively in your business, will only increase this anxiety. The step from this anxiety, and incidents like OpenAI-HuggingFace, to extinction risks feels very religious.
Richard Ngo had an apt summary [ https://substack.com/redirect/53d9c857-97c9-4162-af7e-d8f69871148d?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] of the situation:
Now a large proportion of the AI safety community is implicitly or explicitly orienting to futures where an intelligence explosion occurs within a few years. My default expectation (absent an extensive pause) is that a similar thing will happen: they’ll turn out to be directionally correct (relative to the expectations of almost anyone not linked to the community) but factually wrong. Specifically, we won’t have superintelligence within the next 8 years, but things will still be moving so fast that it’ll *feel* like the people who argued for short timelines were right.
… I wanted to say something now because it feels like the level of bandwagoning towards “singularity soon” is getting pretty wild.
Personally, I think this view aligns closely to what I outlined in my alternate scenario to true recursive self-improvement (RSI), which I called lossy self-improvement [ https://substack.com/redirect/fdb34358-1e09-421f-8ebd-0c9752b275f9?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]. A summary of this view is that:
Automatable research is too narrow to achieve a massive net acceleration in progress, in the face of scaling laws’ exponential costs,
Diminishing returns of more AI agents in parallel are real, &
Resource bottlenecks and politics are a major factor in building strong LLMs (and AI can do much less to accelerate this).
So, I’m left balancing the above, latent increase in the cultural temperature with the potential that the labs have seen genuinely scary, specific breakthroughs that are not public yet. My expectation is that more of the current AI safety concern is on the former – scaled agents working – but I hold high levels of uncertainty here. Foundational, imagination-based AI breakthroughs are the sort of thing that would make me update my RSI timelines from closer to a tool to sustain progress in the face of exponential costs (scaling laws), to something more unpredictable and/or unstable.

First, the podcast with Noam Brown made me internalize how big of a short-term acceleration mass inference capacity is. These labs will throw thousands of agents at important, measurable problems. It is important to not confuse massive steps in inference-time scaling, a dynamic which should be fairly predictable, with being the outputs of RSI, which is highly uncertain.

A TLDR would be that our current techniques work and let us solve problems we know how to state, but they don’t result in a magical level of generalization to unknown, harder problems in most partially verifiable domains (i.e. progress in math is an exception, rather than a rule).
