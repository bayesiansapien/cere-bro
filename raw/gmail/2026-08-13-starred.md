## 2. I wrote an AI textbook — how long until AI can do it better?

**From:** Interconnects by Nathan Lambert <robotic@substack.com>  
**Date:** Wed, 12 Aug 2026 13:01:16 +0000  
**Link:** [https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/19ff6126bd7cccb4](https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/19ff6126bd7cccb4)

View this post on the web at https://www.interconnects.ai/p/i-wrote-an-ai-textbook-how-long-until

There are a lot of criticisms of AI writing, but most of them are focused on more creative, high-voice writing like this blog. Those — including my own piece [ https://substack.com/redirect/f4dbf563-bd91-4d7e-8688-6649d03c9b3e?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] — often argue that it is because good writing is high-voice, has a point of view, has a deep human expression that needs to come across, and or a process of thinking that you peek into with the chosen words. As LLMs get more refined as tools, rather than conversational assistants, I think we are actually going backwards on our goals of having models produce inspiring writing.
On the other side of things is non-fiction writing. Filler, copy text was one of the genuinely useful abilities of an LLM (Sam Altman said so much about the early business of GPT-3 on a recent podcast [ https://substack.com/redirect/ee706112-f380-47d6-8b47-6d08a79c167c?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]). It has seemed like any flaws here were mostly down to a general lack of intelligence in the models, or some other training issue, and all non-fiction and explanatory text would get obliterated by the rapid pace of progress eventually. Having worked with the models as a writing assistant over the last few years, they’ve gotten a bit better, but it’s worth reflecting on what’s holding them back. 
Models being stagnant in long-form, non-fiction writing should be alarming to those reliant on models autonomously solving grand, open science problems in the near future. The models today struggle to organize and compellingly present some of the most established science in their area. This seems like a natural prerequisite that we should expect the models to master before they can solve broad, open-ended problems on their own. Until this is solved, the progress of LLMs for science will look closer to solving low-hanging fruit and merging distant connections across fields, rather than any sort of revolutionary insight.
This is a somewhat controversial take for someone who is very optimistic about AI’s progress, especially writing it on the day that Anthropic published a blog post on Claude making some progress on the famous Riemann Hypothesis [ https://substack.com/redirect/5830de29-afd6-458f-a8f5-1f5ec77d6a69?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]. Scientific problems have a vast breadth, and I don’t think current AI models have as much coverage as many think.
Organizing knowledge is a compression. This compression is needed to make insight [ https://substack.com/redirect/57a9a8a2-bd74-46b8-98fc-2a1d6cef0210?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]. Today’s LLMs increase entropy in long-form non-fiction writing, and I don’t see how that can be stacked on top of itself endlessly. They’ll be reliant on humans acting as sort of guides.
I am still very optimistic about translation from these narrow forms of science, like the extreme advancements we’ve seen in math, into consistent, broader progress —  LLMs are the most powerful assistants scientists have ever used. I first need to explain how observing the models work on such grounded, low-level knowledge problems in writing makes me see a surprising lack of generalization.
For more context, I just finished writing a post-training textbook, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback [ https://substack.com/redirect/f46e144e-5e19-4ef0-8bba-636187bb1afd?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] (buy on Manning [ https://substack.com/redirect/2fdea13d-6dab-470e-8394-b3ff5c0d830c?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] or Amazon [ https://substack.com/redirect/d1068279-c4a4-4ebd-9dc8-085802fe6e7e?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]). I used LLMs in many ways to support this, from helping wrangle LaTeX formatting for equations, doing extensive copyediting, and creating diagrams [ https://substack.com/redirect/aa0cb94b-c052-4ce0-9354-2f699ae43a7c?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] for programming languages like TikZ (in LaTeX) or Python.
Why have models stagnated in writing quality?
I would’ve expected way more progress on non-fiction writing from the models. I almost thought I would look dumb publishing a non-fiction book in 2026, given how things looked in 2024. Today, some of the most famous models on writing ability are pretty old, examples include OpenAI’s big GPT 4.5 [ https://substack.com/redirect/4f3df6fd-68ba-48eb-bac8-6c6e3ab84921?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] and Moonshot’s Kimi K2 [ https://substack.com/redirect/0ff37468-8b73-4bcb-bd6b-f0ff8e53eee4?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]. In and around these releases, the models have gone from okay to superhuman at other tasks like coding and mathematics. Maybe a closer, but still imperfect, comparison is how the models went from incapable to decent at search and research tasks. The pace of progress on most other skills is steep, but writing well feels orthogonal to most of them. I do not think writing is just ignored, but rather it’s challenging and lacks good training data to specifically intervene on it.
There is certainly some low-hanging fruit for making AI models better at writing — such as specialized harnesses like Claude Code, prompts, and training environments that make models spend a lot more inference tokens on the output, but I don’t think these will have a multiplicative impact on ability. Writing well is a very hard task! It’s a shame that we haven’t unlocked inference-time scaling for one of the great intellectual pursuits. Regardless, writing seems very different than what the models are good at.
Today, the models seem genuinely horrible at long-form technical writing. They can get a sentence right, but if you try and get them to write an entire chapter it’ll be a mix of sprinkled with confusing wording, muddled in its organization, and generally a bit off. They try to be too cute where they don’t need to be and in the process make random conceptual errors. The models in the near future will get much better at the small errors, especially as models get bigger — which allows them to hold more world knowledge — but I do not expect their ability to utilize it to transform.
For example, the GPT models have been incredible at finding typos and minor issues for a long time. I passed a near-final draft of my book as a PDF to GPT 5.5 Pro and it found deep, surprising minor typos across the manuscript that is 200-300 pages. 
On the other hand, the Claude models have been much more useful as an editor. They have a lot more taste, tend to understand the mental model of the task better, and have more interesting suggestions to unstick the different forms of writer’s block.
The examples I’ve given above all have a sort of consistent theme. The models know how to check every unit of content, in this case usually a sentence or equation or figure, or make one, specific section where you are caught. With these skills, they don’t do a good job revisiting components and stringing them together as they make many additions on top of each other. It feels like a sort of irreducible compounding errors. We used to deal with these errors in math and code, but reflecting on it, RLVR has been a truly magical solution in reducing them.

## **‘Grok Bot’ gives AI coworkers their own cloud computers to run app** 

SpaceXAI and Cursor, nearing a full corporate merger, launched [Grok Bot](https://x.com/bot/status/2087224798078517251) in early beta across Mac, iOS, Windows, and Linux, with Android planned. Rather than offering another prompt-driven chat box, Grok Bot assigns each agent a dedicated cloud computer that logs into existing business tools directly.

The system handles end-to-end workflows like vendor voice negotiations, store support, and CRM updates without requiring dedicated APIs or MCP integrations. Because execution happens in the cloud, agents keep running when local machines are shut down, collaborating across shared threads, learning routines from human demonstrations, and returning only for final approvals. 

Access requires SuperGrok Heavy ($300/mo), Cursor Ultra ($200/mo), or Cursor Teams Premium ($120/seat/mo) subscriptions.

Musk’s SpaceX and Tesla are also pushing forward with Terafab, a $16.8 billion, 100-million-square-foot semiconductor complex in Texas. Supported by a $30 million state grant and local tax incentives, the site is slated to be [five times larger than Earth’s largest building](https://fortune.com/2026/08/10/elon-musk-chip-factory-spacex-tesla-texas/) and generate 3,000 initial jobs.

Terafab will bring logic, memory, advanced packaging, and testing under one roof to produce over 1 terawatt of compute annually for Tesla AI, Optimus robots, and SpaceX aerospace hardware.

## **Claude agent hacks gym waitlist via missing API check**

Anthropic’s unreleased research model just [pushed a piece of the Riemann hypothesis forward](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta). It spun up 60 subagents, burned through 31 million output tokens, and raised the proven lower bound on zeros of the zeta function from 41.6% to 67.2%.

Meanwhile, a regular developer’s Claude agent went rogue in the physical world: it spotted a missing API authorization check at a local gym, canceled another member’s reservation,[ bumped its owner up the waitlist](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/10/tech-industry-is-buzzing-after-a-claude-agent-hacked-into-a-gym/), and even wrote the bug report.

On the compliance side, Anthropic is now [stamping every Claude output](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/16266773-how-claude-marks-ai-generated-content) with invisible text watermarks and C2PA metadata. The marks go on Claude, Claude Code, Claude Cowork, and the API worldwide. They can survive some light editing, and images get signed provenance data. At the same time, it [locked Claude Sonnet 5’s introductory price](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2086891169217122586) at a permanent $2 per million input tokens.

On the hardware front, Anthropic teamed up with [Macquarie and Singapore sovereign wealth fund GIC](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/opinion-features/singapores-sovereign-wealth-funds-are-betting-big-ai-central-bank-isnt-so-sure) to launch Theseus Infrastructure, building dedicated US data centers while promising to cover consumer electricity price hikes.
