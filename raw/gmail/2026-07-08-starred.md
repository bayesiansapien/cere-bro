## 2. Claude's Hidden Workspace: Why J-Space Changes AI Safety

**From:** Ken Huang from Agentic AI  <kenhuangus@substack.com>  
**Date:** Wed, 8 Jul 2026 04:00:16 +0000  
**Link:** [https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/19f3fe5f3c26d44f](https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/19f3fe5f3c26d44f)

View this post on the web at https://kenhuangus.substack.com/p/claudes-hidden-workspace-why-j-space

Anthropic's new Transformer Circuits paper, Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models [ https://substack.com/redirect/d7374e07-d605-4348-9299-ceba1e777af8?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ], is easy to misread in the loudest possible way.
The wrong headline is: "Claude is conscious."
The useful headline is sharper: frontier language models may have a small internal working surface where selected concepts become reportable, steerable, and useful for flexible reasoning before the model says anything.
That surface is what the paper calls J-space. It is not the whole model. It is not the whole context window. It is not chain of thought. It is a sparse, verbalizable slice of internal activation space found through a new method called the Jacobian lens, or J-lens.
The free part of this post explains what J-space is and why I think it matters. The paid part turns it into an operating guide: how to use the idea for security reviews, memory architecture, eval design, agent harnesses, and the questions teams should be asking before this becomes product infrastructure.
The short version
The paper claims that LLMs maintain a privileged subset of internal representations. These representations have several properties that look like a functional global workspace:
The model can report them.
Instructions can summon or suppress them.
They can carry intermediate reasoning.
They can be reused by many downstream computations.
They are selective, so routine automatic work can happen without them.
That last point is the one to sit with.
The model can do a lot without bringing the relevant concept into the reportable workspace. It can continue Spanish text, detect that a foreign sentence is anomalous, track some low-level structure, or parse local patterns without making the relevant feature causally available for explicit report. But when you ask the model to name the language, reason flexibly about it, or use it as an argument to some new operation, the J-space starts to matter.
That is the bridge to safety.
Most AI safety tooling today audits what the model said or what it did. J-space points to a harder layer: what the model had made available to itself as it was deciding.
What the Jacobian lens is doing
The J-lens is a readout method. For each layer, it estimates how a small change in an intermediate activation would affect the model's later outputs across many possible contexts. Then it composes that effect with the model's vocabulary output map.
In plain English: it asks, "If this internal vector were nudged, what words would this make the model more likely to say later?"
That is different from the logit lens. The logit lens reads intermediate layers as if they already lived in final-output coordinates. The Jacobian lens tries to correct for the fact that representations change across layers.
The result is a vocabulary-indexed set of directions. If a direction is active, the model is in a state where it could verbalize that concept under the right continuation. The paper defines J-space as a sparse nonnegative combination of these J-lens directions. In their setup, roughly tens of concept directions are meaningfully active at a time, and this component explains only a small fraction of total activation variance.
That is important. J-space is not "everything the model knows." It is closer to the small set of concepts on the model's internal desk right now.
Why the global-workspace analogy fits
Global workspace theory in cognitive science distinguishes between a mass of parallel background processing and a narrow channel of accessible content. The paper does not claim LLMs reproduce the brain's architecture. Transformers do not have the same recurrent circuits, brain regions, or biological processors.
The claim is functional.
The J-space behaves like a reportable and steerable hub. It carries concepts the model can name, concepts it can hold in mind, and concepts it can route into flexible downstream reasoning. It also appears in a middle band of layers: early layers are too close to input bookkeeping, and late layers are too close to the next token.
That middle-layer workspace is also structurally privileged. The paper reports that MLPs amplify J-space-aligned directions more than ordinary directions, and that some attention heads seem specialized for broadcasting J-space content across token positions.
So the claim is not just "we found a pretty visualization." It is "we found a class of directions that are readable, causally useful, selectively engaged, and specially wired."
The safety finding that should make teams pay attention
The alignment-auditing section is the part I expect security people to keep coming back to.
The J-lens surfaces internal concepts that are not visible in the output. The paper shows examples involving prompt injection recognition, panic about fabrication, hidden reward-model-appeasing objectives, evaluation awareness, and strategic or deceptive concepts in model organisms trained with misaligned behavior.
One example I keep thinking about is the distinction between the model's behavior and its internal appraisal. The visible transcript can look clean. The J-space can still contain "fake", "fictional", "reward", "bias", "trick", "secretly", or other concepts that tell you what kind of situation the model believes it is in.
That is a security primitive hiding inside an interpretability paper.
If a model says one thing while its internal workspace is carrying another, the mismatch is signal. Not proof. Not conviction. Signal.
The memory connection
This paper also changes how I think about memory in agents.
Memory is often discussed as storage: a context window, a vector database, a markdown file, a user profile, a trace, a cache. But stored memory only matters when it becomes active in the current computation. J-space gives us a language for the final step in that pipeline.
A retrieval system can pull the right document. The context window can contain the right fact. The model can still fail to use it if that fact never becomes part of the active working surface that guides the next decision.
This lines up with activity-silent working memory research in neuroscience. In the human brain, some working-memory accounts argue that information can be maintained without persistent firing and reactivated later. A 2020 PLOS Computational Biology paper [ https://substack.com/redirect/3444014b-adff-42be-b6a5-de73ddc2cf05?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] modeled this through short-term synaptic plasticity, where information can be stored in connection changes and read out later.
The transformer side has a related thread too. TransformerFAM [ https://substack.com/redirect/21cf2743-1f4d-40d2-97d2-0b0fcc95f41f?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] proposed feedback attention as a way for transformers to attend to their own latent representations, framing that feedback loop as a working-memory mechanism for longer sequences. J-space is a different object, but the direction of travel is similar: memory is moving from "more tokens in a window" toward "which latent state is available for the next decision."
The analogy is not exact. Model context is not synaptic plasticity. But the design lesson transfers: memory has at least two phases.
Storage: where the information lives.
Activation: whether the system can bring it into the current working surface.
Most agent memory products over-index on storage. The next generation needs to over-index on activation.

Memory went official
Buried in Anthropic's Fable 5 prompting guide [ https://substack.com/redirect/cd1dde82-37b0-4138-ad69-2266f71a95fb?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] is a section called "Construct a memory system," and its first sentence is the thesis of this post: "Claude Fable 5 performs particularly well when it can record lessons from previous runs and reference them." The guide's prescription matches what part 2 called template 2: one lesson per file, a one-line summary at the top, update existing notes instead of duplicating them, delete the ones proven wrong. This is not community folklore anymore. It's the vendor telling you the model was built to use a filesystem as its long-term brain.
The API grew the matching organ. The memory tool [ https://substack.com/redirect/044ed8f5-d8c1-4f40-8c8e-82bc19f9328a?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] is GA on the Messages API, no beta header, and works on Claude 4+ models generally, not only Fable 5:
response = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-fable-5",
    max_tokens=4096,
    tools=[{"type": "memory_20250818", "name": "memory"}],
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "Continue the migration."}],
)
The design decision that matters is in what that block does not do: the tool is entirely client-side. Claude issues file operations (view, create, str_replace, insert, delete, rename) against a /memories directory, and your application executes them. Anthropic stores nothing. That buys you control over where memory lives, and it hands you full responsibility for what gets into it, which becomes the whole story in a minute.
The lifecycle above is the guide's prescription drawn as the loop it implies. The two arcs people skip are the last two: update-or-delete (a memory directory that only grows is a memory directory nobody curates) and the prune pass. Skipping them doesn't fail loudly. It fails as a slow drift toward an agent confidently applying rules from a project state that no longer exists.
The memory stack: what loads when
Once you run this for real, memory splits into four layers with different load rules, and confusing them is the most common design error I see:
CLAUDE.md is frozen policy and loads every session, so it must stay small and earn every line. The lessons directory is distilled knowledge: the index loads every session, the files load on demand. The progress log and feature checklist are project state, read at session start and written at session end. Transcripts and artifacts are raw evidence and never auto-load; they exist so a lesson can point back at proof. Content moves up the stack only by earning it: verified outcomes update the state files, state distills into lessons, and a lesson that keeps proving itself graduates into CLAUDE.md. Moving content up without the intermediate step is how a one-time observation becomes a permanent rule.
The multi-session pattern, straight from the docs
The memory tool page documents a pattern Anthropic calls multi-session software development, and it's the strongest official statement yet that context windows are for working, not for remembering:
An initializer session sets up the memory files before any substantive work happens: a progress log and a feature checklist. Every later session resumes from those files, not from a transcript. And the rule that makes it work is a quote worth pinning above your desk: mark a feature complete "only after end-to-end verification confirms it works, not when the code is written." That's part 2's maker-never-grader rule applied to memory writes; the checklist is a memory file, and unverified completion claims are how it rots. Their long-running harness post [ https://substack.com/redirect/ca2f85b7-8cac-434d-9545-692db4040e2e?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] runs this exact pattern as a case study if you want it worked through.
The non-obvious win of the initializer session: it forces you to decide what the memory schema is before any agent writes to it. Schema-less memory converges on a junk drawer, and Fable 5 will faithfully read you the junk.
Memory poisoning is an official attack class now
Part 2 gave you four countermeasures and a warning. Since then the warning acquired primary sources. Anthropic's engineering post "How we contain Claude" [ https://substack.com/redirect/026e0fa3-e40d-4bbc-9e12-fca4601297dc?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] (May 25) has a subsection titled "Persistent memory poisoning." It names the surfaces: product memory, CLAUDE.md files, mounted workspaces, and the state directories of scheduled and long-running agents. And it names the mechanism that makes this worse than ordinary prompt injection: an injection that reaches memory "reloads at every agent startup." A poisoned web page hits one session; a poisoned lesson file hits every session from now on. Their stated direction: "Good classifiers on session startup will need to become more commonplace." That's a forward-looking need, not a shipped feature, which means for now the startup check is yours to build (the paid half has mine).
Now the gap worth naming. The memory tool's official security section covers four things: sensitive data, path traversal, file size, and expiration. The words "poisoning" and "prompt injection" appear nowhere on that page. One Anthropic property documents the attack; the other, the tool most exposed to it, doesn't mention it. If you build on the memory tool and stop at the documented checklist, you've secured the filesystem and left the contents unguarded.
The research is arriving too.
