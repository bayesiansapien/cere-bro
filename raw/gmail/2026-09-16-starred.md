The debate on US datacenters has never been so politically charged. Four states have acted in under two months. New York has stopped issuing environmental permits for datacenters, Texas has paused the next step in its massive ERCOT interconnection queue, Pennsylvania has pulled datacenters out of fast-track permitting and made state permits conditional on new guardrails, and Oregon has frozen datacenter deals on state-owned land.
Beyond the state level, more than 300 towns, cities and counties have voted to halt datacenters over the past year and a half. If you read the news, the US datacenter buildout looks like it is being legislated to a halt, one government board at a time. As AI drives an unprecedented wave of datacenter construction over the last year, concerns over electricity costs, water consumption, land use, and community impacts are increasingly influencing policy.
But once again, the narrative is driven by widely spread misinformation. In January, we debunked the “Datacenters are taking all of our water” myth [ https://substack.com/redirect/7e79e915-3561-42bd-b992-4a16c0676fa5?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] (TLDR: they don’t, they’re actually remarkably water-efficient). Then in June, in Stop Saying Half of 2026 US Datacenter Capacity is Cancelled [ https://substack.com/redirect/db0fb3b9-601c-40b2-833f-5a06fd32b927?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ], we explained that the widespread narrative of datacenters not showing up was grossly off, driven by uniformed claude-coded forecasts. 
Today, we dive deep into the widespread narrative of moratoriums killing the US datacenter buildout. Once again, it’s highly inaccurate. Our Datacenter Industry Model [ https://substack.com/redirect/b7491234-fd6a-4da8-a0aa-aa2925a32cc9?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] forecasts +38GW of Datacenter IT capacity delivered in the US in 2027, more than double that of 2026. Our methodology tracks real-time and historical satellite imagery of 6,000+ datacenters [ https://substack.com/redirect/b7491234-fd6a-4da8-a0aa-aa2925a32cc9?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]. Contact sales@semianalysis.com to get access to the full granularity. Today, we estimate that roughly 2.3 GW of planned capacity is genuinely delayed because of local moratoriums and New York’s executive order, the two categories of policy intervention for which we can establish a direct project-level impact.
At the local level (i.e., cities, townships, counties), of the roughly 20 GW sitting inside a restricted boundary, only 1,525 MW is delayed. Three projects account for the delayed MW: an AWS campus in Ohio, a powered land developer campus in Pennsylvania, and a site in Colorado.

Most analysis of datacenter moratoriums focuses on counting the number of restrictions, because that is relatively straightforward. But the number of moratoriums is a poor proxy for the amount of MW capacity they actually affect. A moratorium only matters to our forecast if it applies to a specific project, covers the project’s parcel, and blocks an approval that the developer still needs. Therefore, determining the actual MW impact requires a parcel-level, project-by-project analysis.
