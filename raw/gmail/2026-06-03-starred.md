Your Enterprise Data Deserves Better Than a Chatbot
Large language models and their multimodal variants remain the foundation models most people encounter first. That makes sense. Text, images, audio, and video cover a huge range of knowledge-work tasks, and today’s chatbots are far more capable than the text-only systems many people first tried. But enterprise AI does not run on chat alone. It runs on tables, time series, transactions, telemetry, product catalogs, customer histories, service graphs, and messy operational data that rarely fits neatly into a prompt. Using current coding agents for data science  [ https://substack.com/redirect/b0409109-c6cc-4f9b-8fd9-54c54d32dc8d?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]makes this gap concrete. Generating Python and SQL is easy for an agent or an LLM, but data science requires a uniquely human skepticism to contextualize messy data and recognize when a result is simply too good to be true. We may finally be moving past the TINA [ https://substack.com/redirect/d59508d9-118a-4792-9dfa-404c30b76be8?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] phase of foundation models for enterprise AI, where the answer to every problem has been “just use an LLM.” A new wave of frontier models look more specialized and useful for the prediction and decision problems that actually run businesses.
If you’re a regular reader, consider becoming a paid supporter 🙏
Structured and Semi-structured Data Get Their Foundation Model Moment
Kumo’s [ https://substack.com/redirect/bcd0ca7b-9264-46e1-9fbf-8bef5f83787d?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] foundation mode is aimed at one of the most valuable categories of enterprise data: structured relational data. Think customer records, orders, transactions, risk signals, and event histories spread across warehouses and lakehouses. The argument is straightforward: businesses still rely on slow, hand-built ML pipelines, while LLMs lose too much structure when tables are flattened into text. AutoML tried to automate this work, but mostly by tuning the old workflow of joins, features, and model selection. Kumo’s relational foundation model [ https://substack.com/redirect/d9b4f54d-9c85-4a60-9916-47899469f067?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]  takes a different route: it treats a database as a graph, where rows and tables become connected entities. Instead of manually building features, the model attends over raw relational context and generates predictions such as churn risk, recommendations, fraud likelihood, or customer lifetime value. The appeal is workflow compression: fewer bespoke pipelines, faster iteration, and a prediction layer that agents or applications can call on demand.
The newer KumoRFM-2  [ https://substack.com/redirect/3b4f08d2-1704-42e7-8c9d-e6c0dcb531ec?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ]release sharpens that pitch. It is described as a pre-trained model that can make predictions directly over multi-table databases using only a small number of labeled examples at query time. In other words, teams can ask predictive questions across connected tables without first flattening the data, building features, training a task-specific model, and maintaining a separate production pipeline. The reported benchmark results are notable because KumoRFM-2 claims to beat both supervised baselines and other foundation-model approaches on relational tasks while using a tiny fraction of the available labels.
That said, I would not treat this class of foundation models as a blanket replacement for every predictive system. For lower-stakes routing, forecasts, personalization, fraud triage, and operational scoring, the value could be immediate. For quantitative finance [ https://substack.com/redirect/f6434075-7b8f-493a-823d-3f8bf0bb5f76?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ], regulated credit decisions, medical risk scoring, or underwriting, I would expect a more cautious path: careful validation, calibration checks, monitoring, challenger models, explainability review, and human governance before deploying to production.
Kumo isn’t the only active effort here. Prior Labs [ https://substack.com/redirect/70de6e13-6397-43e4-829d-2317fa6d5bd5?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] is pursuing foundation models for tabular data with TabPFN [ https://substack.com/redirect/53f3f93a-d436-40c0-8362-7ca391d5ad1d?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ], a system aimed at row-and-column prediction problems such as churn, fraud, pricing, demand forecasting, credit scoring, predictive maintenance, and clinical risk. Its pitch is similar in spirit: reduce the amount of preprocessing, feature engineering, tuning, and model selection needed before teams get useful predictions. This momentum extends to temporal data, where an expanding roster of time-series foundation models [ https://substack.com/redirect/0da228b8-b996-414c-85f7-5be8711d41a3?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] can now generate reliable forecasts without prior, domain-specific fine-tuning. The important lesson is that structured enterprise data is becoming a first-class target for foundation-model development, with architectures that target tabular, relational, and temporal data.
Datadog is moving in a related direction, though it uses different language. At the AI Agent Conference [ https://substack.com/redirect/7d40cc65-bf33-443f-8618-c50cac5f49ff?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ], Ameet Talwalker described [ https://substack.com/redirect/5ead3bbc-eb54-45d2-adfb-3b15a2e0d39c?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] work on what Datadog calls a “world model” for observability. I think it belongs in this discussion because the inputs are largely structured and semi-structured operational data: metrics, logs, traces, service topology, code, events, alerts, and incident history. The goal appears to be a foundation model that learns how production software systems behave over time, not just a dashboard that summarizes what already happened.
The Toto 2.0 [ https://substack.com/redirect/65daf24b-26e8-45ed-a94b-f89faeec10de?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] release strengthens that story. Datadog’s results suggest that larger Toto models keep getting better, run much faster than the first generation, and generalize beyond observability despite being trained largely on observability and synthetic data. For enterprise teams, the practical implication is that telemetry may become a richer prediction layer for incident detection, root-cause analysis, simulation, and agentic remediation. The world model effort is the next step in that direction, moving beyond forecasting individual streams toward a learned model of how distributed systems fail, recover, and respond to change.

## 7. Virginia Woolf's Night and Day

**From:** Quantum Zeitgeist Newsletter <quantumzeitgeist@substack.com>  
**Date:** Tue, 2 Jun 2026 09:40:24 +0000  
**Link:** [https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/19e87b504f73c096](https://mail.google.com/mail/u/0/#inbox/19e87b504f73c096)

View this post on the web at https://quantumzeitgeist.substack.com/p/virginia-woolfs-night-and-day

Virginia Woolf’s Night and Day is a serious piece of literature given a modern twist. Tina Gharavi’s adaptation of the dense 1919 novel, which had its world premiere at SXSW London 2026, takes Woolf’s story of a young woman who would rather chart the stars than marry well and brings a contemporary wit to it without softening its weight.  It might not be quantum, but we thought it might be an interesting cultural reference to how science did used to happen and the parallels of today. 
What carries the film is the struggle, a woman in 1910 fighting to break from the status quo against almost everyone around her, and beneath that fight runs a quieter charge, the hope that science can finally shake off the shackles of a dusty, hidebound past.
The novel, with a modern twist
Despite the lightness suggested by its billing, this is a serious literary adaptation rather than a comedy, and it is the stronger for it. Woolf’s novel is a dense affair of competing engagements and divided loyalties, and Gharavi and screenwriter Justine Waddell keep that weight while threading a modern wit through it. Set in London in 1910, against the suffragette movement and a visibly cracking Edwardian order, it follows Katharine Hilbery, played by Haley Bennett, who resists her parents’ plans for marriage to pursue astronomy. When her controlling father, played by Timothy Spall, pushes her toward an engagement with family friend William, played by Jack Whitehall, her independence comes under threat, and her world widens when she befriends the suffragette Mary Datchet, played by Lily Allen, and crosses paths with working-class editor Ralph Denham, played by Elyas M’Barek.
The look and the modern touch
The film is good to look at, with camera work, settings and individual scenes that earn the word cinematic rather than televisual. Sebas”. Sebastian Edschmid’s photography makes the most of the Edwardian period, and the world of the 1910s is staged with such confidence that it frames Katharine’s struggle rather than merely decorating it. The modern twist is largely a matter of tone, since Jack Whitehall and Timothy Spall bring a lightness and wit rather than playing the period entirely straight, and Whitehall is a stand-up by trade, so the casting tilts knowingly toward humour. The script has been updated to land for a modern audience too, with contemporary references that a stricter adaptation would never permit, and that wit lifts the material rather than undercutting its seriousness.
The struggle at the centre
Strip away the period trappings, and the film is about one thing: Katharine’s refusal to accept the place she has been assigned. She is pushed toward marriage, steered away from her telescope and told that ambition is not hers to have, and the drama comes from watching her resist all of it at once. The fight is against almost everyone, the father, the suitor and the expectations of her class, and the film is at its strongest when it lets that conflict stay sharp. Bennett carries the role with enough force that you stop counting the corsets and start caring whether she gets out, and the players around her, Allen’s suffragette among them, give her a world worth fighting to join.
The women who did get out
The reason the film reaches beyond its period is that the question it asks did not stay in 1910, and the encouraging part of the answer is how far the scene has moved. Quantum computing, one of the newest sciences going, has female founders and has had them for years. Dr Ilana Wisby served as the founding CEO of Oxford Quantum Circuits [ https://substack.com/redirect/60b94c34-b6b0-4920-a691-943d5add3826?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] and built it into one of the UK’s leading quantum companies. Michelle Simmons founded Silicon Quantum Computing [ https://substack.com/redirect/2a603a1e-ad96-4da3-9b7a-f2aa23f6f97b?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] in Australia and leads it as chief executive. Carmen Palacios-Berraquero co-founded Cambridge’s Nu Quantum [ https://substack.com/redirect/fd3b6384-3af3-4d56-87d0-ddf07502d65d?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] and runs it as CEO Pascale Senellart helped found the French photonic company Quandela. [ https://substack.com/redirect/c42954b9-45c8-4f4e-9902-fdc2330045c2?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ] Eleni Diamanti co-founded the quantum-networking firm Welinq, and Rebecca Krauthamer co-founded and leads the post-quantum security firm QuSecure [ https://substack.com/redirect/7ca8f02f-ac6c-4c74-a58d-85ad0ee2020e?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ].
Those names are still the exception, and the scale is worth stating plainly. Surveys put women at under a fifth of the quantum workforce, with a 2023 Quantum Economic Development Consortium estimate of around 17% in the United States, and the founder tier is thinner again, with one recruitment study finding that four in five quantum companies have no senior woman at all. A rough sum makes the point. Take the roughly 1,100 quantum companies tracked worldwide, allow a generous 10% female founders and two founders apiece, and you reach only a few hundred women founding quantum companies anywhere on earth, a figure you could nearly seat in a single lecture theatre.
One woman makes the point more neatly than any founder, because she has lived both halves of Katharine’s divided life at once. Dr Libby Heaney trained as a quantum physicist, took a PhD in quantum information science and published across the field then retrained in art and is now widely regarded as the first artist to use quantum computing as a working medium, with pieces shown at Tate Modern and the V&A. She is the subject of an earlier profile on how she bridges science and art [ https://substack.com/redirect/014a0bf6-86e5-4bac-bd2f-7de51ed9d0ac?j=eyJ1IjoiOGs4ZDAifQ.VI2uzD6zEHQ-HoC2J2iX6FwxM11lZ3HP_Zidnio1pMQ ], and she is the clearest reply to the film’s central tension, because the choice between the stars and the world of art that Katharine is told she must make is the very choice Heaney refused.
Verdict
It is genuinely watchable and captivating. The seriousness of the source has been carried over rather than embalmed, and the modern wit is largely the reason it holds the attention rather than serving as a respectful trip to the museum. What carries it is Katharine’s fight to break away against almost everyone, and Bennett gives that fight the force it needs. The result is a serious adaptation with a contemporary pulse, and a reminder that the choice it dramatises, between ambition and the life you are told to want, is one plenty of women in science are still being asked to make.
