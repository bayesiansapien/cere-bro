# How Do You Diffuse AI Into the Real World? (Varun Shenoy, Long Lake)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B0fjR3yaZFU

## TL;DR
Long Lake does not sell software. It buys the businesses and deploys AI inside them, which means when the AI fails it is their own P&L, not a customer's complaint. Shenoy's framing: capability is real and diffusion is the actual bottleneck, and diffusion takes a generation, as it did for electricity, where the demo was 1882 and Ford's electrified moving assembly line was 1924. Three lessons from two and a half years of owning the outcome: climb the autonomy ladder rather than jumping to "AI coworker," build a flywheel that turns real work into traces into evals into better agents, and treat continual learning and user enablement as one loop rather than two teams. The closing argument is that you cannot co-design software with a services business over Zoom or a support ticket. You have to show up.

## Key Takeaways
- **The diffusion framing is the intellectual core.** A 200-person property management firm where nothing has changed is not a failure of AI, it is the expected state. Electricity required ripping out existing motors, installing new equipment, and retraining everyone. Shenoy's claim is that AI diffusion is the single most important problem of the next twenty years, and that model capability will not be the binding constraint.
- **The autonomy ladder, six rungs.** Copilot (a RAG chatbot, instant answers), synchronous agent (Claude Code, Codex, runs 1 to 5 minutes with tools and skills, you stay in the loop), asynchronous agent (goes away and returns, and critically can be triggered by external events rather than by the user), long-running agent (hours to weeks, an open problem the labs are working on), and AI coworker (proactive partner). Everyone wants to sell you the last rung. His hard-won position: you have to earn the right to do more, both because models are not there for some tasks and because the field organisation needs to be brought along incrementally.
- **The jagged frontier applied to form factors.** For code, the synchronous agent is a coding agent on your desktop and the async agent is that same agent in a sandbox producing a PR, which he calls largely solved. Engineers are unusually good at async agents because they already parallelise: launching ten jobs and being comfortable that job seven finishes before job three is normal for them. For services, the synchronous version (a co-working agent with deep enterprise context, MCP, custom tools) exists. The asynchronous version for services is the genuine frontier, because services work is traditionally serial. People clear an inbox one email at a time, not ten at once.
- **The sharpest idea in the talk: represent knowledge work as code.** Models are trained on code, want to write code, and are excellent at it. Rather than waiting for models to catch up on services knowledge work, encode that work in a representation the model is already strong at. That is a capability-arbitrage move, not a workaround.
- **The valuable tasks are not on the internet.** How to close the books when receipts are missing, how to scope a building from a blueprint collaboratively, how to coordinate vendors to fix a roof. This knowledge lives in people's heads and in twenty-year-old software. Frontier models have read everything humanity wrote down, and this was never written down.
- **The trace flywheel, with three distinct payoffs.** Agents collaborate with employees on real work, producing rich traces including tool calls, hiccups, and papercuts. Payoff one: automatically built and scored evals, capturing both explicit feedback (thumbs, notes) and implicit feedback, notably the diff between what the AI generated and what the human actually submitted. Payoff two: internal post-training on business data that is entirely out of distribution for frontier labs. Payoff three: agent customisation per company, per user, and per client.
- **The ratchet is the part most teams miss.** Every week's hill-climbing benchmark becomes next week's regression test. Improvements are locked in rather than traded away.
- **Ground truth is available and unambiguous in services.** Did the roof get repaired. Did the books get closed. No LLM judge required, and no proxy metric to be gamed. This is a genuine structural advantage over most AI evaluation settings.
- **"The exceptions are the job."** His illustration contrasts the smooth slope we imagine for LLM tasks against the actual terrain of hills, ravines, and death by a thousand papercuts. The demo is the slope. The exceptions are the work.
- **Continual learning and enablement are one loop.** Usually owned by separate siloed teams (research or platform versus growth or deployment). The agent only improves if people use it, and people only use it if it is worth adopting. Which surfaces the real question: how do you get the *initial* usage? Everyone assumes usage shows up. It never does. A 100-year-old firm does not change its processes because a tool was provisioned, and the person who has closed the books the same way for twenty years will keep doing that.
- **Extreme software-service co-design, borrowed from Jensen Huang's hardware-software co-design.** Meet people metaphorically, by embedding into Excel, the ERP, the 3D design software, Outlook, so enablement energy stays low. And meet them physically: fly out, do lunch-and-learns, run a cotton candy stand at their conference, go mountain biking and ask what actually breaks in their day. Sit two-on-one and watch them use it.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **Post-training on proprietary out-of-distribution data is a defensible moat, and it is the most interesting technical claim here.** Frontier labs cannot access the operational data of an HOA management firm's back office. Long Lake owns it. That means small specialised models fine-tuned on this data can plausibly beat frontier models on these specific tasks at a fraction of the serving cost, which is exactly the compression-and-specialisation thesis. The economics also work in their favour: they capture the full margin improvement rather than a software licence fee, so investment in efficiency accrues directly to them.
- **Implicit feedback via diffs is a nearly free preference signal.** The delta between the AI's draft and what the human actually submitted is a paired comparison generated by normal work, at zero labelling cost, with no annotator disagreement. That is a better DPO-style training signal than most deliberately collected preference data, and almost nobody has it because almost nobody sits inside the workflow.
- **Autonomy rungs are latency-tolerance tiers, and they have different cost profiles.** Synchronous agents are latency-bound, so they need fast models and aggressive KV-cache reuse. Asynchronous agents are throughput-bound, so batching, cheaper quantized models, and off-peak scheduling all become available. Moving a workload from synchronous to asynchronous is not just a UX change, it changes which optimizations are permissible and typically cuts serving cost substantially.
- **Knowledge-work-as-code is a representation-alignment argument.** If you can express a business process in a form near the training distribution's strongest region, you get better performance without touching the model. Cheaper than fine-tuning and cheaper than waiting for the next release.
- **The regression-ratchet pattern is directly reusable.** Promote each week's benchmark into the permanent suite. Without this, hill climbing wanders: you improve on the current target while silently regressing on last month's.
- **Per-company, per-user, per-client customisation is a routing and adaptation problem at scale.** Thousands of variants cannot each be a full fine-tune. This is where LoRA-style adapters plus a routing layer that selects the right adapter per request is the obvious architecture, and it is a serving-efficiency problem before it is a modelling problem.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The claims check out and the scale is real. Long Lake, roughly three years old and backed by General Catalyst, has acquired more than 30 businesses across HOA management, construction, and now corporate travel. Its property management arm alone raised $670 million, acquired 18 businesses, and reportedly reached $100 million in EBITDA in under two years, with AI agents handling resident inquiries and drafting board materials. In May 2026 Long Lake Management agreed to take American Express Global Business Travel private for $6.3 billion, off the NYSE, at a reported 65% premium, with GBT's CEO framing it as combining travel expertise with Long Lake's AI capabilities.

The strategic context Shenoy omits is that this is not an idiosyncratic experiment, it is a category. General Catalyst's Creation Fund, over $1.5 billion and launched in February 2025, is built on an explicitly Elad Gil-adjacent thesis of AI-margin-expansion buyouts across accounting firms, law firms, call centres, MSPs, IT services, property management, HVAC, roofing, and insurance brokerage. Financial press through 2026 has covered the VC roll-up craze extensively, including as a Silicon Valley buyout playbook now landing on Wall Street. So the honest framing is that Long Lake is the most visible instance of a well-funded bet, not a lone pioneer.

Two caveats worth holding. First, a 65% takeover premium on a public company is a very large wager on AI-driven margin expansion, and it has to be paid for out of realised efficiency. The thesis is unproven at that scale, and the $100 million EBITDA figure in property management is company-reported without disclosed attribution between AI-driven gains and ordinary roll-up effects like procurement leverage, overhead consolidation, and multiple arbitrage. Roll-ups produced margin expansion long before LLMs existed, and disentangling the two is exactly what nobody has published.

Second, and to Shenoy's credit, he does not claim to have solved the hard part. Async agents for services remain the stated frontier, long-running agents are described as an open problem at the labs, and the enablement answer is genuinely labour-intensive: get on a plane, run a cotton candy stand. There is no clever trick offered, and the absence of one is the talk's most credible feature. The "you have to touch some grass" closing line is a real finding, and it is also the least scalable part of the strategy, which is the tension the business has to resolve.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Harvest implicit feedback from diffs, starting immediately.** This is the single most transferable idea and it costs almost nothing. Anywhere an agent produces a draft that a human then edits before use, log both versions. For Amit's work: agent-proposed quantization configs versus what was actually shipped, agent-drafted benchmark scripts versus the corrected version, suggested routing rules versus deployed rules. That paired data is a preference dataset generated by normal work, and it is exactly the input a distillation or preference-tuning run needs.
- **Ratchet your evals.** Promote each iteration's target metric into a permanent regression suite rather than replacing it. Optimization work is especially vulnerable to this failure: a new quantization scheme improves the current benchmark while silently degrading a model shape or query class nobody re-tested. A growing append-only suite is the fix, and it is a process change rather than an engineering project.
- **Reclassify workloads by latency tolerance, then optimize each tier differently.** Audit which internal inference workloads are genuinely synchronous. Anything that can tolerate minutes should move to a batched asynchronous path with a smaller quantized model and off-peak scheduling. In most organisations this is the largest untapped cost reduction available, and it requires no model work at all, only a queue.
- **Look for out-of-distribution proprietary data inside your own organisation.** Long Lake's moat is data frontier labs cannot see. The equivalent question: what does your team have that no lab has? Kernel profiling traces across your specific hardware mix, failure modes of quantization on your model family, historical routing decisions with outcomes. Small models post-trained on that beat frontier models on those narrow tasks and cost a fraction to serve. This is the most direct application of the talk to Amit's actual job.
- **Prefer ground-truth outcome metrics over judge scores wherever a real outcome exists.** "Did the roof get repaired" has no ambiguity. In optimization work the equivalent already exists and is often bypassed in favour of proxies: did the deployed model hold accuracy at target latency under production traffic, not did an LLM judge rate the output favourably.
- **Represent the task in the model's strongest modality.** If a process is currently expressed as prose instructions and the model handles it poorly, try expressing it as code, a schema, or a typed configuration. This is free capability, and the same insight underlies why typed languages help coding agents.
- **Do not skip rungs on the autonomy ladder.** Deploying a proactive autonomous agent into a workflow that has not yet run a synchronous one successfully is how internal AI projects lose trust permanently. The Snowflake talk in this same batch makes the identical point from a different angle: trust is earned slowly and lost overnight.
