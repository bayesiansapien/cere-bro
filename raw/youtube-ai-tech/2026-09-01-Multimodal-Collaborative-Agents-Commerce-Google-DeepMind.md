# Multimodal Collaborative Agents for Next-Gen Commerce (Nidhi Kaushik Vyas, Google DeepMind)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AhQpRalYlyg

## TL;DR
The most substantive talk in this track on evaluation, and the framing is sharper than the commerce examples suggest. Vyas's diagnosis: most agents are search-bar wrappers that assume the user arrives with a well-formed intent and the right vocabulary. Real users arrive with a vibe. She calls the gap between the feeling and the query the articulation gap, and the agent's job is to close it through proactive elicitation rather than to wait for better keywords. Her loop is discovery (build a working state from conversation history, personal context, and reference images, including soft constraints with confidence scores), research (choose the *modality* of elicitation, then do comparison work in the background), and adaptive response (choose bullets, comparison table, or visual board based on what the query needs). The genuinely reusable material is the auto-rater suite she attaches to each stage, particularly counterfactual sensitivity and over-asking detection. Her closing point that response structure is itself part of model intelligence is correct and underrated.

## Key Takeaways
- **The articulation gap is the right problem statement.** Users rarely have well-formed intent, so an agent that only answers well-specified queries fails on the majority of real traffic. "Prepare to accept vibes" is her own summary and it is a design requirement, not a joke.
- **Working state, with soft constraints treated as first-class.** From a query like "redo my living room under a budget," the agent builds session history, user context, extracted hard constraints, and then the interesting part: soft constraints inferred from a reference image, each carrying a **confidence score** rather than being asserted flatly.
- **Real-time variables are separated out deliberately.** Some working-state fields, inventory being the obvious one, must be refreshed at response time because stale values make the whole answer moot. Distinguishing cacheable state from must-be-fresh state is a real architectural decision that most agent designs blur.
- **Intent-gap prioritisation by information gain.** Given several unknown variables, the agent does not resolve all of them. It compares candidate moves and picks the single question with maximal information gain. Her example is asking room width before style confidence, because a recommendation that does not physically fit invalidates everything downstream. That is decision-theoretic question selection, not a scripted intake form.
- **Modality selection as a distinct decision, and this is the talk's most original idea.** For a subjective constraint like style, text elicitation is the wrong instrument. The agent instead generates a visual preference board of candidate styles derived from the user's reference image, establishing a shared vocabulary neither party could express in words. Show and ask, rather than always ask.
- **Micro-signals feed the confidence model.** Hovers and clicks on the preference board update the agent's style-confidence estimate. Implicit preference data, collected as a side effect of normal interaction, at zero labelling cost.
- **Constraint-to-ontology bridging happens at runtime.** The agent forms a temporary mapping between the constraint it is exploring and the merchant's product catalog ontology, so retrieval can actually act on it. In Q&A she confirms this is where merchant domain expertise flows in, and that UCP is the mechanism for merchants to speak a common language with agents.
- **Adaptive response format.** Bulleted summary for policy or review questions, trade-off table for comparisons across axes the user cares about, visual references for inspiration queries. "The way you have the model response structured is also very much part of the intelligence."
- **The auto-rater suite, stage by stage, which is the part worth copying.** Working state: all facts retained, confidence calibration within an error bound, and **counterfactual sensitivity** (flip parts of the query and verify that the extracted constraints change where they should and stay fixed where they should not, measured in both directions). Collaborative strategy: are all blockers identified, is the agent **over-asking**, and question utility. Elicitation: efficiency at discovering hidden preferences using a **user simulator** seeded with known constraints, turn efficiency, and format selection accuracy. Response: format accuracy, data fidelity (no hallucination), and **user actionability** (does the format leave the user confident enough to commit).
- **Her candid answer on agent-to-agent commerce.** They have not reached the point of agents interacting with their agents, and their user studies find that users prefer to be involved personally in upper-funnel discovery and inspiration, delegating to agents mainly at the lower funnel for comparison and price negotiation. She expects MCP to be the interface eventually. That is a more honest read of consumer appetite than the rest of this conference track offers.
- **Merchants do not control presentation.** Response format is part of the agent's intelligence and is deliberately not a merchant decision, because the goal is one horizontal layer across all merchants with a consistent user experience. Worth noting as a power question, not just a design one.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **Information-gain question selection is the optimization worth stealing.** Each clarifying turn costs latency, tokens, and user patience, so the objective is to minimise turns to sufficient confidence. Selecting the question that maximally reduces posterior uncertainty is the correct formulation, and it is the same reasoning as active learning: query the point that most reduces model uncertainty rather than the next point in a list. Her room-width example also captures a dependency structure worth encoding explicitly, since some unknowns invalidate downstream work and should be resolved first regardless of raw information content.
- **Confidence scores on inferred constraints are what make the loop closeable.** Without a calibrated confidence, the agent cannot know whether to ask or to proceed. This turns "should I clarify" from a heuristic into a threshold test, and it is why the calibration auto-rater matters as much as the accuracy one.
- **Counterfactual sensitivity is the single most transferable eval idea in this batch.** Perturb one aspect of the input and assert that the corresponding output field changes while unrelated fields stay fixed. It tests causal attribution rather than surface correctness, and it catches the specific failure where a model produces the right answer for the wrong reasons. Cheap to implement, and almost nobody does it.
- **Over-asking as a first-class failure mode.** Most eval suites penalise wrong answers and ignore excessive interrogation. An agent that reaches a correct recommendation in fifteen questions has failed even though every question was individually defensible. Turn efficiency is a real objective with a real cost, and this is the same insight as counting wasted turns in tool use from [the Sourcegraph talk](./2026-08-26-Death-of-Developer-Advocates-Agent-Advocacy-Stephanie-Jarmak.md).
- **User simulators seeded with known constraints are the right harness for multi-turn evaluation.** You cannot measure elicitation efficiency without a ground truth about what the user actually wanted, and a simulator gives you exactly that: hidden preferences you can check the agent discovered, plus reproducibility and the ability to run thousands of trials.
- **Modality selection is a routing decision in a different dimension.** Text versus visual versus structured comparison is a choice about output channel, driven by the type of the constraint being resolved. The same principle applies to any generation task: match the representation to what the model and the consumer each handle best, which is the same argument as [representing knowledge work as code](./2026-08-28-Diffusing-AI-Into-The-Real-World-Varun-Shenoy-Long-Lake.md).
- **Cacheable versus must-be-fresh state is a concrete performance lever.** Precompute and cache everything stable, fetch only the volatile fields at response time. This is the compute/serve split from [Notion's GTM architecture](./2026-08-26-Notion-GTM-Systems-Flora-Liu.md) applied to a single conversational turn.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The techniques map onto a real and active research literature. Selecting follow-up questions to maximise expected information gain is the standard formulation for handling ambiguous queries, with published work on training LLMs to ask sequential preference-revealing questions, including approaches that treat clarification as a forward-and-reverse denoising process. Google Research has published specifically on asking clarifying questions for preference elicitation with LLMs, so this talk is the productisation of in-house research rather than a one-off product intuition. There is also recent work arguing agents should help users *construct* preferences rather than merely elicit them, which is a stronger version of Vyas's articulation-gap point: for genuinely fuzzy intents the preference does not exist prior to the interaction, so the visual preference board is not extracting a hidden answer, it is helping form one.

On the merchant side, her UCP reference is accurate. UCP launched in January 2026, co-developed by Google with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair, Target, and Walmart, covering catalog search through order management, which is exactly the constraint-to-ontology bridge she describes needing.

**The important caveat concerns the auto-raters themselves.** Her stack depends on LLM-as-judge at every stage, and the literature is clear that this requires calibration against human raters rather than being trusted by default. Most agent work does not calibrate. Reported human-alignment rates for the best judge models sit around 75%, which is useful and is not ground truth. More pointedly, recent work titled "Catching One in Five: LLM-as-Judge Blind Spots in Production Multi-Turn Transaction Agents" finds systematic judge failures in precisely this setting, multi-turn agents conducting transactions, which is the exact configuration Vyas is evaluating. So the auto-rater suite is the right *design* and its outputs should be treated as a noisy proxy with known blind spots, particularly on the subjective dimensions like question utility and user actionability where there is no verifiable answer to check against.

To her credit, she says the auto-raters are an evolving system that starts simple and grows alongside the product, which is the honest framing. And her Q&A answer on consumer appetite, that users want to stay personally involved in discovery and delegate only at the comparison and negotiation stage, is the most useful piece of demand-side evidence in the entire agentic-commerce track, given how thin actual [transaction volume](./2026-09-01-x402-Isnt-Good-Yet-Jan-Curn-Apify.md) turns out to be.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Add counterfactual sensitivity tests to your existing eval suites this quarter.** This is the highest-value idea here for Amit's work and it transfers directly. For a quantization pipeline: change one property of the input (sequence length, batch composition, a specific layer's sensitivity) and assert that the corresponding output metric moves while unrelated metrics hold. For a router: perturb the query class and assert the routing decision changes, then perturb something irrelevant and assert it does not. This catches the case where a config is right by accident, which accuracy-only evals cannot distinguish from being right for the right reason.
- **Measure turns and questions, not just final correctness.** Over-asking is a real cost that standard evals ignore. Any agent-assisted workflow should be scored on how many interactions it took to reach a correct outcome, because a system that gets there in fifteen turns has failed at the thing that matters.
- **Attach calibrated confidence to inferred state, and use a threshold to decide whether to ask.** Systems that silently proceed on low-confidence inferences produce confidently wrong outputs. Systems that ask when uncertain and proceed when confident need a calibration eval, which is why her confidence-within-error-bound rater is not optional.
- **Build a simulator with hidden ground truth for any multi-turn evaluation.** Seed it with the answers, then measure how efficiently the agent recovers them. This is the only way to evaluate an interactive process reproducibly, and it is the same construction as generating synthetic workloads with known optimal behaviour for a scheduler or router.
- **Calibrate your auto-raters against human judgement on a sample, and report the alignment rate.** Given roughly 75% best-case alignment and documented blind spots in multi-turn transaction settings, an uncalibrated LLM judge is an unmeasured instrument. Sample a hundred cases, have a human grade them, publish the agreement number alongside every auto-rater result.
- **Separate cacheable state from must-be-fresh state explicitly in any agent's context assembly.** Precompute the stable parts, fetch the volatile parts at request time. Straight latency and cost win with no accuracy cost.
- **Note the demand signal and let it inform expectations.** Users want to drive discovery themselves and delegate comparison. That argues for building agents that assist decisions rather than agents that make purchases, which is close to the opposite of what most of this conference track assumed.
