# Your Agent Just Authorized What?! | Jay Mok & Ben Coumes, PayPal

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vGn6N4-bxBY

## TL;DR
The most useful conceptual contribution in the agentic-commerce track, and it fits on one slide. Every agent authorization reduces to three questions: did the human authorize this, is it allowed right now in this scope, and can we prove it later. The right answer depends on two context variables, stakes and whether the counterparty ecosystem is open or closed, which produces a three-tier matrix. Low stakes in a closed ecosystem (Claude Code with connectors) needs only OAuth plus tool permissions plus system logs, because actions are reversible. Medium stakes in a closed ecosystem (a vaulted commercial card shared with a travel buyer agent via OAuth) needs scoped mandates but can rely on ordinary transaction logs, because both parties borrow trust from a shared intermediary. High stakes among unknown counterparties, which they say they have not seen in production yet, needs cryptographic proof, and their recommendation is that the industry converge on FIDO Verifiable Intent plus AP2 mandates. Their closing observation is the important one: this model is not payments-specific, it applies to any hard-to-reverse agent action.

## Key Takeaways
- **The three questions are the framework, and they map cleanly onto mechanisms.** Did the human authorize this becomes a passkey or equivalent. Is this allowed now in this scope becomes a time-bounded token with an amount and possibly a specific merchant or product intent. Can we prove it later becomes the dispute-resolution requirement, which is what determines whether logs suffice or cryptographic evidence is needed.
- **Stakes and counterparty openness are the two axes, and the second one is the underappreciated variable.** Their badge analogy carries it well: once you have badged into the building you do not re-badge for every room, because you are inside a trusted boundary and can extend some trust to anyone else you meet there. High stakes with unknown parties is meeting a stranger on the street, where showing a badge proves nothing.
- **Low stakes, tier one: Claude Code.** Human consent via OAuth to GitHub, Jira, or Linear. Scope via Claude's allow, deny, and ask tool permissions. Evidence via system logs, and crucially, the ability to revert. No cryptographic proof needed because the actions are reversible and it is a closed ecosystem.
- **Medium stakes, tier two: shared vault plus OAuth scopes.** Their example is a merchant such as a travel content provider wanting to monetise occupancy data and reviews to buyer agents. Two Braintree and PayPal enterprise primitives combine: a vault storing payment credentials on behalf of buyer agents, which alone does little, plus OAuth-mediated access to those credentials for merchants. Together they manufacture a closed ecosystem of buyer and seller agents that can transact. A commercial card is shared with the buyer agent under a scoped mandate, and dispute handling falls back to existing transaction logs.
- **The trust-borrowing mechanism is the actual insight in tier two.** Neither the buying agent nor the selling agent trusts the other. Both trust the intermediary (their partner, Nevermind) to enforce the payment mandate, so the intermediary's enforcement substitutes for bilateral trust. That is how closed ecosystems are constructed rather than discovered.
- **High stakes, tier three: the multi-layer selective-disclosure token.** Layer one is issued by a trustworthy credential provider. Layer two encapsulates the user's instructions to the agent, signed with the user's private key. Layer three, present only for autonomous payments, is signed by the agent. The power is selective verification: a merchant verifies that the checkout is correct, a payment processor verifies that the payment mandate is correct, and no party needs any prior relationship with any other.
- **The PayPal approval token is a new primitive shipping to production.** Historically PayPal orders were synchronous: the user checks out, opens the PayPal app, approves, done. Here the user is redirected to PayPal to confirm the instructions given to the agent, and PayPal returns a JSON payload carrying amount, expiry, and the intended merchant. It is an opaque string only PayPal can approve, so it is a proprietary analogue of verifiable intent rather than the standard itself.
- **The generalisation is the most valuable line in the talk.** The same three-tier model should govern any hard-to-reverse agent action: medical orders, e-signatures, securities trading. Payments are simply the domain where the infrastructure got built first.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The layered selective-disclosure design is the technically substantial content, and it is worth understanding precisely because it solves a problem that recurs across multi-party agent systems.

The problem is that in an open ecosystem each participant needs to verify a different claim, and none of them should see the others. A merchant needs to know the checkout is authorised and does not need the payment credential. A processor needs the payment mandate and does not need the user's shopping instructions. A layered token where each party can verify only its own layer solves this without a trusted broker holding everything. That is the same architectural property as capability-based security: hand out the narrowest verifiable claim rather than a general credential plus a policy engine.

The layer-three detail deserves emphasis because it is doing something subtle. The user signs the instruction layer and the *agent* signs the autonomous-action layer. That creates a two-signature chain establishing both that the human delegated and that the agent acted within the delegation, and it is what makes after-the-fact attribution possible in a dispute. Without the agent's signature you can prove the human authorised a class of action, but not that this specific action came from the authorised agent rather than from an impostor. This is the cryptographic answer to the accountability question that the Edge & Node and Kiduna talks at this conference tried to answer with registries and blockchain audit trails, and it is a cleaner one, because it requires no shared registry and no chain.

For Amit's work the transferable idea is the two-axis matrix rather than the crypto. The general rule it encodes: the strength of the evidence you need scales with the irreversibility of the action and with the social distance to the counterparty. Reversible action plus known counterparty needs only a log. Irreversible action plus unknown counterparty needs a signature. Most agent-permission designs pick one level and apply it uniformly, which either over-secures cheap reversible actions (adding latency and friction to everything) or under-secures expensive ones. Tiering by these two variables is the correct design, and it composes with the enforce-outside-the-agent principle that six talks at this conference independently reached: the tier determines *what* you enforce, and the enforcement always sits outside the agent.

One thing the talk does not address, and it is the gap that matters. The three questions are all about authorization, and none of them is about correctness. A properly signed, in-scope, provable transaction can still be the wrong transaction, because the agent misread the user's intent or was manipulated into forming a bad one. Verifiable intent proves the human authorised an envelope. It does not prove the purchase inside that envelope was what the human wanted. That distinction is exactly the prevention-versus-accountability split, and this talk, like the others, delivers accountability.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The standards recommendation is well-founded and the institutional consolidation happened four months before this talk.

On April 28, 2026 the FIDO Alliance announced initiatives to develop interoperable standards for agentic interactions and commerce, forming two technical bodies: an Agentic Authentication Technical Working Group and a Payments Technical Working Group, tasked with defining how agents authenticate users, verify intent, and execute transactions. Google donated the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) to the Alliance and Mastercard contributed its Verifiable Intent framework. Visa, American Express, PayPal, Stripe, Adyen, Cloudflare, Microsoft, and Okta joined, with roughly 60 organisations associated with the donation.

The division of labour between the two contributions is worth stating precisely, because the talk blurs it. AP2 defines how the participants in an agentic payment flow (agents, merchants, wallets, payment providers) coordinate around user authorization, with mandates as its core primitive: structured, cryptographically signed Verifiable Digital Credentials capturing what a user authorised an agent to do. Verifiable Intent defines how that authorization is *proven*, as a cryptographic credential framework turning user authorization into portable verifiable evidence. So AP2 creates and shares intent, Verifiable Intent proves it, which is why Mok and Coumes recommend both together rather than either alone.

The underlying token format is a real IETF standard, not a proposal. Selective Disclosure for JSON Web Tokens (SD-JWT) was published as RFC 9901 in November 2025, allowing a holder to reveal specific claims from a credential while keeping others hidden, layered on the existing JWT ecosystem. That is what makes the layered-verification design implementable with standard libraries rather than bespoke cryptography, and it is a stronger foundation than the talk conveys.

AP2 has also advanced beyond what they describe. AP2 v0.2 introduces "Human Not Present" payments, allowing agents to execute payments autonomously against pre-authorised instructions, with the canonical example being buying limited-run tickets the moment they go on sale. Google updated AP2 and launched Universal Cart at I/O 2026. So tier three is further along in specification than "we haven't seen it in production yet" suggests, though production deployment is indeed the gap.

**The critical caveat, and it is exactly the gap identified above.** Fortune reported on August 24, 2026, a week before this talk, under the headline that Google can track precisely how your agent spends your money but it is no help when the agent buys something you did not approve. That is the authorization-versus-correctness distinction stated as journalism. The entire AP2 and Verifiable Intent stack produces excellent evidence that a transaction was authorised and in scope, and provides no mechanism for the case where the agent stayed inside its envelope and still bought the wrong thing. For a user, "we can prove you authorised an agent to spend up to $200 at this merchant and it did" is not a remedy when the purchase was a mistake. Dispute resolution in agentic commerce is therefore an unsolved problem sitting directly behind a well-solved authorization problem, and no talk at this conference addresses it.

Note also that the PayPal approval token is explicitly not the standard. Mok and Coumes say it is an opaque string only PayPal can approve, similar in concept to verifiable intent but not the same. So PayPal is recommending industry convergence on an open standard while shipping a proprietary primitive first, which is the normal commercial pattern and worth reading as such.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Adopt the two-axis matrix as the default design for agent permissions in any system Amit builds.** Irreversibility on one axis, counterparty familiarity on the other. Reversible plus known needs a log. Irreversible plus unknown needs a signature. Uniform permission levels either over-secure cheap actions or under-secure expensive ones, and both failures are common.
- **Apply the three questions as a checklist, and then add a fourth.** Did the human authorize, is it in scope now, can we prove it later, and, missing from the talk, was it the right action. The first three are authorization and are largely solved. The fourth is correctness and is not, which is where the real residual risk lives.
- **Use SD-JWT (RFC 9901) rather than inventing layered credentials.** It is a published IETF standard with library support and it gives exactly the per-party selective verification property that makes open-ecosystem transactions possible without a trusted broker holding everything.
- **Note the two-signature chain as the answer to agent attribution.** User signs the delegation, agent signs the action. That establishes both that the human delegated and that this specific agent acted within the delegation, which is what a dispute requires. It is a cleaner solution than the registry and blockchain approaches proposed elsewhere at this conference and it needs no shared infrastructure.
- **Copy the trust-borrowing pattern when you need two mutually-unknown parties to transact.** Neither trusts the other, both trust an intermediary to enforce the mandate, and the intermediary's enforcement substitutes for bilateral trust. This is how you manufacture a closed ecosystem, and it is far cheaper than building open-ecosystem cryptography for a bounded set of participants.
- **Apply the framework outside payments, as they suggest.** Any irreversible agent action deserves the tier-three treatment: deleting production data, submitting a paper, sending an external communication, provisioning expensive compute. For Amit specifically, an agent authorised to launch training runs is making irreversible spending decisions, and the same envelope-plus-signature logic applies.
- **Track the dispute-resolution gap as the open problem.** Authorization is nearly solved and correctness is not. Whoever answers "the agent stayed in scope and still got it wrong" builds something the current standards stack cannot, and the Fortune reporting suggests users are already hitting it.
