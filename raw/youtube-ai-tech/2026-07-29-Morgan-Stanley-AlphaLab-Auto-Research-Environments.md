# Morgan Stanley's AlphaLab: Multi-Agent Research Across Optimization Domains

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-07-29
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kiqubc5b5Yo
**Speaker:** Brendan Rappazzo, Morgan Stanley Machine Learning Research (~30 PhD AI researchers)

## TL;DR
Morgan Stanley built an auto-research agent to automate quant research, open-sourced it with a 40-page tech report, and then discovered its own architecture was the least valuable part. The 1.0 design is three phases: a research phase where the agent builds context and reads arXiv, an eval-building phase guarded by an adversarial Builder/Critic/Tester loop, and a mass-experimentation phase run as a Jira-style Kanban board with a Strategist agent proposing experiments and Worker agents implementing, submitting Slurm jobs, and writing postmortems back. It reached the top 12% of an NVIDIA-hosted Kaggle competition on 10 iterations and produced real production model improvements. Then it started failing on the hardest problems, and the team concluded that every design choice they had hand-made (how many strategists, whether they debate) is itself a verifiable loop an LLM should optimize. The 2.0 thesis is the headline: **general auto-research is becoming a commodity, so all enterprise value lives in building environments and evals.** They are now doing meta-harness optimization where the LLM reads traces and improves the harness, plus GRPO and on-policy distillation on open-source models against those environments.

## Key Takeaways
- **The problem shape that makes this work is Kaggle-like.** Input time series, predict future values, possibly with calibration constraints. Rappazzo notes that being on the sell side means less adversarial selection, which is what makes the auto-research frame viable at all. This is the honest scoping caveat most auto-research pitches omit.
- **Three motivating opportunities, all mundane and all real.** More compute cycles on existing production algorithms (hyperparameter search, ensembling). Transfer of a working system across desks (credit bonds to muni bonds) as an automatable translation task. Greenfield desks with no ML at all, where even a reasonably trained model has large impact.
- **The capability threshold was December 2025.** He is specific: it did not feel possible until Opus 4.5 plus harnesses like Claude Code and Codex, and calls this a common sentiment. Two things crossed at once, model long-horizon ability and the harness pattern.
- **They wrote their own harness rather than using a framework, and Claude wrote most of it.** The argued benefit is max freedom to tweak anything and provider agnosticism through plain functional tool calling. This is now a defensible default rather than a contrarian choice.
- **Three tools carry the whole system.** Full shell access (write bash, set up Python, edit and run code), web search (read arXiv and technical blogs to reach public state of the art), and a Slurm abstraction where the model declares "I need 4 H100s and this many CPUs" and submits a config without doing hardware orchestration.
- **The research phase is a super-CLAUDE.md builder.** It is prompted to build a to-do list such that completing every item would let it start experimenting, which lets them re-prompt whenever it tries to exit early. Each item produces a markdown note file, which later agents query to manage context dynamically. Runs 3 to 4 hours.
- **The eval-building loop is the most transferable piece of engineering.** One builder agent writes the eval code. Two critics review: one high-level (conceptual errors, forward leakage of information) and one programmatic (writes unit and integration tests). Issues route back to the builder, and the loop does not terminate until all three are satisfied. His framing of the risk is precise: LLMs are not malicious but make very silly mistakes, and optimizing against a bad eval collapses the whole system.
- **The experimentation phase is a Kanban board on purpose, for steerability.** A Strategist queries all prior context and pushes experiment cards to a to-implement column. Workers write the code, write the Slurm config, submit, wait, then inspect the ML training curves (underfit or overfit) *and* the eval results, and write a postmortem back to the Strategist. The Strategist can then self-evolve: three transformer variants failed, XGBoost is working, explore more tree methods. Because it is a board, humans can cancel cards, add their own, and chat with the Strategist to inject intuition. The UI choice is a control-surface choice.
- **Reported results, stated without inflation.** Better LLM training config than a Karpathy-style single-agent-in-a-loop baseline on speedrunning. Top 12% on an NVIDIA-hosted Kaggle competition to fine-tune NeMoTron into a reasoning model, on only 10 iterations because they joined late, with the caveat that AlphaLab works better the more iterations it gets. Internally, several cases of taking an already-decent model and finding meaningful improvements now moving through risk into production.
- **The three failures they admit, and the single answer.** (1) It fails on the hardest problems, so how real is any of this? (2) Are the design choices (strategist plus workers) arbitrary? (3) Where is Morgan Stanley's enterprise knowledge and the team's quant expertise actually encoded? The answer to all three is **build your own evals and environments.**
- **The lesson he says he relearns monthly:** start with a good eval. They were overeager and wanted to treat the agent like a human researcher. Now they are opinionated: strict Kaggle-like format, data and description in, harness in the middle, harness's only job is to submit containerized models, it sees a public leaderboard score, the human sees a private held-out validation score.
- **The identity claim that reframes everything: evals and environments are the same thing, you just train in environments.** Once the format is strict, 10 to 20 carefully built environments become a reinforcement learning signal, and you can point AlphaLab at AlphaLab.
- **Meta-harness optimization is now the actual work.** Rather than humans hill-climbing on "maybe there should be two strategists, maybe they should debate," the LLM reads traces and results and improves the harness itself. Separately, they are collecting traces from open-source models and touching weights via GRPO and on-policy distillation, and expect the best configuration to be an orchestration of open and closed models optimized jointly.
- **Expertise is encoded through qualitative rubrics, not just metrics.** Designing verifiable metrics is the easy part. They also grade rollouts against rubrics derived from looking at traces and asking what makes a good researcher and what the thought process should be. Those rubrics are how human expertise becomes a learning signal.
- **The closing thesis, stated as a prediction:** general auto-research will become a commodity, and he cites GLM 5.2 as evidence it is already happening. Therefore all value as an enterprise or human expert comes from building environments. Their plan is to keep open-sourcing the harness precisely because the environments hold the value.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The mechanically important claim for optimization work is the **inversion of where the moat sits**. AlphaLab 1.0 was an architecture: a specific multi-agent topology with specific roles. AlphaLab 2.0 asserts that the architecture is disposable ("whatever lives in the middle, in the limit we almost don't care about"), initializable from 1.0, and destined to be optimized away by the model itself. What is not disposable is the environment: the data, the verifiable metric, the held-out private validation set, and the qualitative rubric.

This maps directly onto compression and routing work. Consider what an environment for a pruning or quantization workflow would have to contain to be a real RL signal rather than a benchmark:

- **Data and task description in, containerized artifact out.** For quantization, the submission is a quantized checkpoint plus its serving config. The harness never sees the private eval.
- **A public leaderboard score the agent optimizes against, and a private held-out score only the human sees.** This is the specific defence against the failure mode Rappazzo names, where the system optimizes a subtly broken eval. In compression this failure is endemic: perplexity on a held-in corpus is the classic bad eval that a pruning search will happily exploit while destroying downstream behaviour.
- **The Builder/Critic/Tester loop applied to the eval itself.** The high-level critic looking for "forward leakage of information" is exactly the calibration-set contamination problem in post-training quantization, where the calibration data overlaps the evaluation distribution and the reported accuracy is fictitious. Having a dedicated adversarial agent whose only job is to hunt leakage is a cheap and obvious safeguard that compression pipelines generally lack.
- **The Slurm abstraction as the correct level of hardware indirection.** The agent declares resource requirements and submits, without orchestrating hardware. For kernel and inference optimization work this matters more than it does for quant research, because the eval *is* hardware-dependent. An environment for kernel optimization must pin the hardware, or the leaderboard measures machine variance.

The Strategist postmortem loop is the second transferable mechanism. Its value is that failed experiments become context rather than being discarded, which produces the self-evolution behaviour he describes (abandon transformer variants, pivot to tree methods). In a compression search, the equivalent is that a failed sparsity pattern should update the search prior, not just be scored and dropped. Most hyperparameter search infrastructure throws away exactly this information.

The third and most consequential mechanic is the **open-plus-closed orchestration being optimized jointly** via GRPO and on-policy distillation against their own environments. This is a routing problem stated as a training problem: rather than choosing a model per query at inference, they are training the open model to fill specific roles in a heterogeneous ensemble, with the environment as the reward. That is a strictly more powerful formulation than post-hoc routing, and it is only available to someone who owns the environment.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The talk's claims check out against the published record, with useful specifics the talk elides.

**The paper and code are real and more detailed than the talk suggests.** AlphaLab is published as *AlphaLab: Autonomous Multi-Agent Research Across Optimization Domains with Frontier LLMs* (arXiv 2604.08590), with a project page and code in Morgan Stanley's MSML open-source repository. The published description gives **four** phases rather than the three in the talk: it configures domain-specific prompts and metrics, explores the dataset and researches prior work via web search, constructs an evaluation framework through the adversarial Builder/Critic/Tester loop, and runs large-scale GPU experiments via the Strategist/Worker loop. The prompt-and-metric configuration step is the one he compressed away.

**The evaluated domains are more optimization-relevant than the talk conveys.** The paper evaluates with GPT-5.2 and Claude Opus 4.6 across three domains, and one of them is **KernelBench, where AlphaLab writes custom GPU kernels and benchmarks them against PyTorch's optimized compiler**. He mentions CUDA kernels in passing. The paper treats kernel generation as a first-class evaluation domain, which makes this directly relevant to inference optimization work rather than tangentially so. Anyone in kernel or serving optimization should read the KernelBench section of the paper rather than relying on the talk.

**The commoditization claim about GLM 5.2 is well supported.** GLM-5.2 was released by Z.ai on 13 June 2026 as an MIT-licensed open-weight model with 744B parameters (40B active) and a 1M-token context. It leads open-weight models on every public agentic and coding benchmark and holds the top open-weight position on SWE-bench Pro at 62.1%, ahead of GPT-5.5 at 58.6%, while remaining competitive with but behind the closed frontier on broader intelligence indices. Independent commentary calls it the first open-weight model that "feels right in coding harnesses as a general agent." Kimi K3, published by Moonshot on 27 July 2026 as a 2.8T MoE model, now leads open weights on LiveBench Coding (81.45) and Agentic Coding (57.58), ahead of GLM 5.2's 79.65 and 51.92. So his commoditization thesis is not speculative, and the open-weight frontier moved again two days before he gave the talk.

**The implication he does not state.** If open-weight agentic capability is improving on a roughly six-week cadence, then the depreciation schedule on harness engineering is brutal and the appreciation schedule on environments is the mirror image. Environments built against a strict submission format survive model turnover. Harnesses tuned to a specific model's quirks do not, which is precisely the failure the FactSet talk documents from the other direction.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **Build one environment before building any more harness.** Pick a single well-posed compression or routing task and construct it in AlphaLab's strict format: data and natural-language objective in, containerized artifact out, public leaderboard score visible to the agent, private held-out score visible only to you. The discipline that matters is that the agent can never see the private set. This is the concrete, transferable output of the entire talk.
2. **Steal the Builder/Critic/Tester eval loop verbatim, and give the high-level critic one specific job: hunt for leakage.** In post-training quantization and pruning, calibration-set contamination is the direct analogue of the forward-leakage problem he describes, and it silently inflates reported results. A dedicated adversarial critic that only looks for overlap between calibration and evaluation distributions is cheap to build and catches a class of error that no amount of downstream tuning recovers from.
3. **Instrument failed experiments as context, not as discarded scores.** The Strategist postmortem loop is what produces the pivot behaviour (three transformer variants failed, explore tree methods). In a sparsity or quantization search, write the postmortem: what was tried, what the training curves showed, what the eval showed, and what that implies about the next hypothesis. Feed it back into the search prior. Most hyperparameter infrastructure keeps the scores and throws away exactly this.
4. **Read the KernelBench section of arXiv 2604.08590 rather than the talk summary.** It is the part of the published work closest to inference-optimization practice, it is not covered in the talk, and it is the strongest available evidence about whether agentic search can beat a compiler on kernel generation.
5. **Reframe the routing problem as an environment problem.** Their most sophisticated move is training open models into specific roles inside a heterogeneous open-plus-closed orchestration via GRPO and on-policy distillation, using their environments as the reward. Post-hoc inference-time routing is the weaker version of this. If the goal is dynamically choosing the cheapest adequate model, an environment that scores quality-per-unit-cost lets you *train* the routing policy rather than hand-tuning a threshold, and the environment is the thing that makes it possible.
6. **Assume the harness depreciates and the environment appreciates.** Given GLM 5.2 in June and Kimi K3 in July, treat any harness-specific tuning as a six-week-half-life asset and any environment as a durable one. Budget engineering effort accordingly.
