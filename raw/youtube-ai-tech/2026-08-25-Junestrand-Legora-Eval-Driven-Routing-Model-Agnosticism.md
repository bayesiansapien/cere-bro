# Max Junestrand: You Need The Willingness To Learn Faster Than Anyone Else

**Channel:** Y Combinator
**Published:** 2026-08-25
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o0ORPbSEgd8

## TL;DR
Legora's founder tells the origin story, and buried in the Q&A is the most concrete statement of eval-driven model routing from a production system anyone has published this year. The narrative arc: three non-lawyers in Sweden, a YC rejection in May 2023 after failing to answer what kind of lawyers they served, acceptance two months later under a new name, GA in October 2024, and $1 million to $100 million ARR since, with over 3% of the world's lawyers now active users. The operational decisions are more interesting than the growth. They refused to fine-tune, betting instead that base models would keep improving and that the work was delivering model capability into a market, which was contrarian in 2022-23 when Bloomberg was spending heavily on a domain-specific legal model. They froze sales for six months to fix reliability, on the reasoning that in law you get one chance and are punished when things go wrong rather than paid when they go right. And the routing claim, stated flatly: the core IP and muscle to build is the ability to evaluate use cases, because that is what allows you to route effectively. They hired lawyers whose job was half customer-facing and half constructing use cases to run evals against, built an internal benchmark over three years, kept it private while only OpenAI and Anthropic models were worth measuring, and released it once the model board got wide enough to matter. Their surprise finding was that Grok performed among the best given cost, and they could not yet offer it because it was not on their data processing agreement.

## Key Takeaways
- The anti-fine-tuning bet is the most consequential technical decision and the reasoning is clean: models will keep improving, so the durable work is in delivering their capability to a market, not in specialising them. Stated partly as conviction and partly as capital constraint, which is honest.
- The concrete counter-example he names is Bloomberg spending heavily on a domain-specific legal model, which by implication did not pay off against general models plus good harness and product.
- "Build for the world today and maybe one step ahead" is the operating rule that followed. His first sale was, in his own words, we are like ChatGPT but compliant in Europe, which he correctly identifies as a weak pitch that was nonetheless good enough at the time.
- The eval-then-route formulation is the line to remember: the ability to evaluate use cases is the superpower that allows you to route effectively. Evaluation capability is upstream of routing capability, not parallel to it.
- How they built that capability is the practical part. They hired lawyers with split roles, customer-facing plus use-case construction for eval suites. Domain experts producing evaluation cases, not just annotating them.
- Two distinct customer segments with opposite cost preferences, which is the real argument for routing rather than model selection. One group wants to loop expensive reasoning models on hard problems for long periods and accepts large LLM bills. Another wants open-weight models to cut consumption cost. His answer is that the right policy is somewhere in between, which is what a router is.
- The domain-specific reason law tolerates expensive inference: token spend is tiny relative to the human expertise cost it displaces, so for complex litigation you throw the most capable model available at it. That inverts the usual cost sensitivity and is a clean example of why routing policy has to be per-vertical.
- Non-technical constraints bind model choice as hard as capability does. Grok was among the best performers per unit cost on their benchmark and was unavailable because it was not on their data processing agreement. Banks and large law firms refuse Chinese models outright.
- Capacity is a real engineering constraint at their scale, with locked contracts with labs and compute providers, because the system cannot go down when a measurable fraction of the world's lawyers depend on it.
- The unglamorous cost centre he names without prompting: millions of dollars on OCR and document parsing. At scale the preprocessing pipeline is a first-order expense, not a detail.
- The current hard problem is reactive to proactive agents. Rather than a prompt triggering work, context sources trigger it: an incoming contract routes to an agent that works it in parallel and escalates to a lawyer only if needed, or a data room is connected and the agent organises it and produces the diligence report unprompted.
- The reliability decision is the strongest founder judgment in the talk. Freezing sales for six months at roughly $1.3 million ARR, while competitors were doing ten times that, because the failure mode in law is unrecoverable. The $1M to $100M curve starts after the freeze.
- The hiring heuristic, stated as y-intercept versus slope: they stopped optimising for impressive prior logos and started selecting for trajectory, on the reasoning that a high starting point with a flat curve fails inside an exponentially scaling company.
- The Jantelagen observation is a genuinely interesting cultural-engineering problem. Swedish norms against thinking yourself exceptional support a meritocracy of ideas and are incompatible with building a winner-take-most enterprise company, so they deliberately blended cultures rather than picking one.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The sentence worth extracting is that evaluation capability is the prerequisite for routing capability, and it deserves to be taken more seriously than a passing remark in a Q&A usually gets. Most routing work treats the router as the hard part and the eval as instrumentation. Junestrand's claim inverts that: without a use-case-level evaluation suite you cannot know which model is better for which class of request, so you have no basis for a routing policy and the router is guessing. The practical corollary is the staffing decision he describes, which is the non-obvious part. He put domain experts on eval construction as half their job, permanently, not as a one-off labelling exercise. That produces an eval suite that tracks the actual distribution of customer requests and evolves with it, which is the only kind of eval a router can be trusted against. A static benchmark built once cannot support a routing policy for long, because the request distribution drifts and the policy silently decays.

The two-segment observation is the cleanest real-world justification for routing over model selection that I have seen stated by an operator. One customer group wants maximum capability with long-running loops and accepts the bill. Another wants open weights to cut consumption cost. These are not different levels of the same preference, they are different objective functions, and no single model choice satisfies both. That means the routing policy has to be parameterised by customer, not just by query, and the router's objective is a per-tenant cost-quality tradeoff rather than a global one. That is a materially harder problem than the standard formulation and it is what production routing actually looks like.

His point about law's cost structure is the piece worth generalising. Token spend is negligible relative to the human expertise it displaces, so the optimal policy is to spend maximally on capability. That is a statement about the ratio of inference cost to displaced labour cost, and it gives you a clean way to classify verticals. Where that ratio is very small, routing should bias hard toward capability and cost optimisation is close to worthless. Where the ratio approaches one, as in customer support at roughly a dollar per resolved ticket, routing must bias toward cost and latency and the entire optimisation stack earns its keep. The same router architecture, the same evals, and opposite policies. Anyone building a general router should be computing this ratio explicitly rather than assuming cost matters.

The constraint that actually binds their model choice is the one nobody models: Grok was among the best performers per unit cost on their own benchmark, and they could not use it because it was not on their data processing agreement. Large law firms and banks refuse Chinese models regardless of measured quality. So the feasible model set is determined by contracts and customer policy, and the optimisation runs inside that set. Any routing framework that treats the model pool as fixed and known is missing the variable that changes most often in an enterprise deployment. The right design has the eligible model set as a per-tenant input to the policy, refreshed from contract state, and the router must degrade gracefully when its preferred model becomes ineligible mid-flight.

The reactive-to-proactive transition is the architecturally significant shift and it changes the cost profile more than it changes the capability story. A reactive agent's compute is bounded by user requests. A trigger-driven agent's compute is bounded by event volume, which is not something the user is throttling, and every incoming contract or document arrival spawns work whether or not anyone needed it. That makes cost control a scheduling and admission-control problem rather than a per-request routing problem, and it introduces a question routing does not usually face: whether to do the work at all. Escalation to a human is itself a routing decision with a very different cost basis, since a lawyer's hour dwarfs any model call, so the policy has to trade model spend against the probability of avoiding escalation. That is a genuinely new objective and it is the interesting problem in their stack.

Finally, the millions spent on OCR and document parsing is the reminder that preprocessing is not free. In a document-heavy pipeline the parse layer can rival or exceed inference cost, it is embarrassingly parallel and therefore easy to over-provision, and it is almost always excluded from the cost models people build for LLM systems. If you are modelling end-to-end cost for any retrieval-heavy or document-heavy deployment, the parse and extraction stage belongs in the model as a first-class term.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The growth claims check out and are, if anything, understated in the talk. Legora crossed $100 million ARR in April 2026, eighteen months from general availability, reported as the fastest enterprise software company to that milestone on record, ahead of OpenAI, Anthropic, Cursor and Wiz. Funding has moved well past the $9.51 million Benchmark cheque he describes negotiating on a desk: a $150 million Series C in October 2025, then a $550 million Series D at a $5.55 billion valuation led by Accel with Benchmark, Bessemer, General Catalyst, ICONIQ, Redpoint and Y Combinator participating alongside new investors including Alkeon, Bain Capital, Firstmark, Menlo, Sands, Starwood and Salesforce Ventures, extended by a further $50 million to $600 million at a $5.6 billion post-money valuation. Total raised exceeds $800 million, and reporting in August 2026 has them exploring a round at around $10 billion while Harvey approaches $15.5 billion. Headcount figures in the talk drift between 750 and 1,300, consistent with him quoting different points in a very fast ramp.

The benchmark is real and its formal name is the Legora Benchmark for Agentic Reasoning, Legora BAR, published at legora.com/bar and released roughly a month before this talk. Its stated design is worth noting because it matches his description of the internal version: end-to-end legal tasks drawn from real cases, purpose-built to track how the product performs as both models and harness advance. The published results support his surprise about Grok specifically. On the longest-running tasks, Fable 5, Grok 4.5, Opus 4.8 and Sonnet 5 all significantly outperform the suite average, and the reported pattern is that models look similar on short legal work and diverge as tasks get longer and more complex, where sustaining judgment across a full matter is the differentiator. That divergence-with-task-length finding is the most useful published datapoint in this entire item, and it is a strong argument that short-task benchmarks systematically fail to rank models for agentic use. The evaluated model families are from Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI, which is what his garbled "SpaceX and Grock" refers to.

The Jude Law campaign is confirmed and the details are better than the anecdote. Created by NoA Åkestam Holst, directed by Rhys Thomas, under the platform "Law just got more attractive," running across digital, social and out-of-home in New York, London and Scandinavia, targeting the US, UK, Canada and EU. His account of Law insisting on bringing his own scriptwriter and cinematographer, and of nagging as an underappreciated negotiation technique, is not independently verifiable but is consistent with reported coverage of how the campaign came together. Legora has since extended the celebrity and sports partnership approach beyond Law.

One claim to treat as unverified: over 3% of all the world's lawyers as active users. It is a company figure with no stated definition of active or of the denominator, and given global lawyer counts run into the millions it implies a large absolute number. It may well be right; it is not checkable from the outside.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Reorder your own routing work to put the eval first, because Junestrand's claim is that this ordering is the whole game and his production system is the evidence. Before writing a routing policy, build a use-case-level eval suite that reflects your actual request distribution, and staff it continuously rather than once. Without it the router is optimising against a proxy you have not validated.
2. Compute the inference-cost-to-displaced-labour-cost ratio for every workload you route, and let it set the policy's bias. Where the ratio is tiny, as in complex legal work, bias hard toward capability and stop optimising cost. Where it approaches one, as in high-volume support, cost and latency dominate. Same router, opposite policies, and getting this backwards wastes the optimisation entirely.
3. Make the eligible model set a per-tenant input to your routing policy rather than a global constant. Legora's best cost-adjusted model was unusable because of a missing data processing agreement, and enterprise customers veto whole model families on provenance grounds. Design the router so a model becoming ineligible is a configuration change, not an incident.
4. Take the divergence-with-task-length finding from Legora BAR seriously in your own evaluation design. Models rank similarly on short tasks and separate on long multi-step ones, which means short-task benchmarks cannot rank models for agentic deployment, and a compressed model that looks fine on short evals may fail specifically on long-horizon coherence. Add a long-horizon axis to any compression evaluation you run.
5. Put document parsing and OCR into your cost models as a first-class term. Legora spends millions on it and it is routinely omitted from LLM cost analyses. For any retrieval or document-heavy pipeline, measure parse cost alongside inference cost before concluding where the money goes.
6. Recognise that trigger-driven agents change the cost problem from routing to admission control. When work is initiated by events rather than users, the volume is not user-throttled, so the first decision is whether to do the work at all. The interesting objective is trading model spend against the probability of avoiding a human escalation, since the human hour dominates every other term.
7. Note the anti-fine-tuning bet as a live consideration for your own compression roadmap, because it cuts against specialisation. Their thesis was that base capability improvement outruns the value of domain adaptation, and it worked. The version of that worth holding is that any specialisation with a long build time is racing a rising baseline, which argues for compression and adaptation techniques that are cheap to redo rather than expensive to get right once.
