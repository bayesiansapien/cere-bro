# Just-in-Time Ontologies: What Cheap Intelligence Means for Data (Hrishi Olickel, Southbridge AI)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-10-12
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa3hQVIKShw

## TL;DR
Olickel argues that data infrastructure was built on the assumption that transformation (judgment) is expensive, and cheap intelligence along the full Pareto frontier breaks that assumption. Universal ontologies fail not mainly from lack of buy-in but because there is no universal schema: source, storage, decision, and the model's own prior are four distinct schemas, and they only align at the moment a decision is made. So build schemas "just in time," keep them with their receipts, and reuse them. His proof point: an Ohio campaign-finance entity-resolution job projected at ~$10,000 was done for ~$795 (on an $800 pre-set budget) using TypeSafe's Jev decision model, with an Opus/Astra-class agent self-optimizing the pipeline over 10 days. The routing and cross-model-checking lessons are directly relevant to Amit. The cost figure is self-reported and the transcript is garbled on it.

## Key Takeaways
- **Four schemas, not one.** Source (what the recorder can access), storage (what can be written down), decision (what the question needs), and the model's prior (what the model assumes the world looks like). A "customer" means payer to billing, caller to support, liability to compliance. Only the decision fixes the definition.
- **Three model roles that coding agents wrongly collapse into one context window:**
  1. **Designers:** slow, "thick-token" frontier models (Opus or above) that spec the pipeline and authorize spend.
  2. **Workers:** cheap, fast, specialized, instruction-following models with known defects, inside the pipeline. Decision models (Jev) slot here.
  3. **Hill climbers ("Pareto surfers"):** models that take a working baseline and iteratively swap in cheaper/faster components under a fixed budget and failure surface.
- **Ohio case study:** 23,317 donor cases. On 200 held-out families, a cheap reasoning model alone matched Fable at ~2% of the cost. Jev confidently said "no" to all household-link questions until the question was rewritten to define sufficient evidence, then flipped to correct. Fix was prompt definition, not model or threshold changes. Over a dozen such "qualification" rounds each pinned one definition (committee, filer, co-residence).
- **Checking costs ~4x doing.** About $545 went to second opinions, disagreement resolution, and audit versus ~$128 for first answers. Verification, not generation, is the dominant cost of reliable data work.
- **Agent run stats:** 10 days, a dozen-plus architectures, 4,000+ harness runs, ~90,000 Jev calls, ~6B tokens across inner models.
- **Prior-driven errors are the largest emerging error class:** a schema expected `in_kind` but the file used `In-Kind`, silently reclassifying 15,658 in-kind contributions as cash. A date parser tried four formats while the file used a fifth, emptying 99.4% of dates while row counts and totals still matched. Models "helpfully" took absolute values of negative loan amounts, turning a net $784K into $5.3M of debt, and multiple models validated it.
- **Cross-model prior intersection:** Jev and GPT-6 Luna agreed on 194 of 200 families with zero errors in the agreed set. All errors sat in the six disagreements. Models from different lineages disagree more usefully. The exception he hints at: models distilled from another lab's outputs inherit that lab's priors.
- **Species-level errors survive ensembling:** four model families produced an identical total because they shared a sign convention the answer key did not. These have to be enumerated and handled explicitly.
- **What self-optimization needs:** a stable deployment surface (their open-source Hankweave harness: checkpointing, rollback, budget enforcement), bounded experiments (cost, time, access), encoded methodology (pattern library), and, most important, a failure surface defined before starting.
- **Six-stage loop:** spec and split (800K rows → 200K cases → typed questions), define failure surface, bounded pilots, move checks into rigs between work segments ("codons"), compose and validate, deploy and let it mature.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **This is a routing paper in disguise.** Decomposing a job into typed, small questions lets each sub-question move to its own point on the cost/accuracy frontier. That is per-subtask routing with a hill-climbing search over (model, prompt definition, verifier) configurations, scored against a fixed failure surface.
- **Agreement-gated escalation.** Two cheap, diverse decision models that agree are treated as resolved; disagreement routes to a more expensive judge or human. This is a cascade where the escalation signal is inter-model disagreement instead of a confidence score. It works only if errors are decorrelated, which favors lineage diversity over raw accuracy.
- **Prompt definitions beat model upgrades** for decision models. The household-link flip shows a constrained-output classifier's calibration is dominated by how the decision boundary is described. Treat the question text as a hyperparameter in the search.
- **Distillation warning for Amit's work:** his aside that distilled models share their teacher's priors means a distilled student is a poor independent verifier of its teacher. Diversity for checking has to come from a different pretraining lineage, not from a compressed copy.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
"Jeff/Jev" in the transcript is TypeSafe AI's Jev, launched Tuesday September 15, 2026, which fits his "launched on a Tuesday" and "10 days" timeline. Jev classifies, scores, and routes against plain-English criteria and returns probabilities, priced at $0.042 per million input tokens with free output and 70 to 500 ms latency. It is still early access, and there are no independent benchmarks. TypeSafe's own 711-case dashboard put Jev at 67.8% versus 74.1% for the best comparator, winning on cost and latency but losing on accuracy. That supports the talk's real lesson: decision models are cheap enough that you can afford to spend 4x on checking, and you need to. "Luna" is OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna behind the Decisions API (DevDay, Sept 29), and Microsoft Decision-1 and Amazon Strands Decider 2B have since joined the category. The DuckDB framing comes from Hannes Mühleisen's May 2025 "The Lost Decade of Small Data?" post.

The cross-model agreement claim is directionally right but rosier than the literature. Kim et al. (ICML 2025, "Correlated Errors in Large Language Models," 350+ models) found that when two models both err they pick the same wrong answer ~60% of the time, that shared provider and architecture raise correlation, and that the most accurate models have highly correlated errors even across providers. A 2026 preprint estimates nine frontier judges from seven families carry only about two independent votes. So "194 agreements, zero errors" on 200 examples is a small sample. His own sign-convention anecdote is the counterexample. Hankweave and the $795 cost could not be independently verified, and the transcript's "$7.95" is almost certainly a transcription error for $795 given the $545 + $128 breakdown.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Add disagreement-gated cascades to your router benchmark.** Compare (a) confidence-threshold escalation versus (b) two cheap models from different lineages with escalation on disagreement. Measure the error rate inside the agreed set and how error correlation moves with model pairing. Kim et al. gives a ready baseline for expected correlation.
- **Measure verifier independence after compression.** When you prune, quantize, or distill a model, compute its error correlation with the parent on a held-out set. High correlation means the compressed model can serve as a cheap substitute but not as a cross-check. That is a useful, under-reported metric to include in compression evals.
- **Budget verification explicitly.** In any pipeline cost model, assume checking may cost several times generation. Routing savings on first answers matter less than routing on the verifier tier.
- **Before trusting any LLM-built data pipeline**, write invariant checks the model cannot rationalize away: null-rate per column, sign distributions, category counts against source. Totals matching is not evidence of correctness.
