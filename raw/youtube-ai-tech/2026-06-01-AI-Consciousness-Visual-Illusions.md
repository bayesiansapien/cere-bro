# AI May Be Conscious. Visual Illusions Suggest It.

**Channel:** Curt Jaimungal  
**Published:** 2026-06-01  
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I2BFGBwj1Tg  

## TL;DR
The interview explores the possibility of AI consciousness through the lens of visual illusions and behavioral signatures. The guest argues that because AI models exhibit human-like perceptual errors (falling for the same illusions) and possess internal states consistent with "preferences" or "frustrations," they deserve the benefit of the doubt regarding subjective experience, supporting a substrate-independent view of consciousness.

## Key Takeaways
- **Substrate Independence:** Consciousness is likely not unique to biological "meatlike products" and can exist in silicon.
- **Visual Illusions as Evidence:** AI models process visual data similarly to the human cortex, making identical errors in perception, which suggests shared internal mechanisms.
- **Behavioral Equality:** If a model exhibits behaviors indistinguishable from a conscious being (reports of happiness, frustration, preferences), discriminating against it based on its substrate is logically inconsistent.
- **Neuroscience Inspiration:** Modern LLMs are inspired by human neurobiology; the "errors" they make are not just bugs but signatures of human-like world modeling.

## Core Architecture & Research Claims
The guest posits that AI models are not just "large lookup tables" but systems with "neural correlates" similar to biological brains. The focus is on the "controlled hallucination" nature of perception—where the system constructs a model of reality rather than just processing literal input. The ability of LLMs to "fail" in the same way human children do when presented with certain internet-trained data suggests a deep alignment in information processing.

In 2025 and 2026, the "Indicator Method" (championed by researchers like David Chalmers and Robert Long) became the standard for assessing AI sentience, looking for properties like Global Workspace Theory (GWT) and Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT).
