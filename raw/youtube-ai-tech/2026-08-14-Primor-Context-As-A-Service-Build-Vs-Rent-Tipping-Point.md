# The Rise of CaaS: Context-as-a-Service for Agentic AI — Omer Primor, Bright Data

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-14
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ot4OPrPH4xY

## TL;DR
Primor runs product marketing at Bright Data and this is a vendor talk that arrives at a conclusion conveniently served by his employer's products, which is worth saying up front because the analysis underneath is still the most useful build-versus-rent framing of the retrieval layer available right now. His argument runs in three moves. First, the web has stopped being a source of data for humans and become a source of context for agents, and it decays fast: social content within a day, news, finance and retail largely stale within 30 days, which makes retrieval a continuous process rather than a snapshot. Second, a new category he calls context-as-a-service has emerged alongside AI search, vertical providers in e-commerce, travel, finance, HR and real estate that do not just crawl and index but build knowledge graphs, deduplicate entities and merge sources, effectively acting as very good search engines for one narrow thing. Third, and this is the payload, he ran a test rather than a benchmark: enrich 100 companies across 25 fields using an Opus 4.8 agent loop, comparing AI search providers, CaaS vendors, plain Google, and the model's native search. Coverage converged across almost everything, with the CaaS providers slightly behind because a fixed index cannot answer a question about data it never collected while a searcher can keep exploring. Cost clustered too, except for one native option that was extreme and one CaaS vendor that was both cheapest and worst, which he reads as a long-tail niche play. Then the real point: every repeated query costs the same as the first even when nothing changed, so frequency is the cost killer, and teams respond by cutting corners, researching weekly instead of daily and taking 10 results instead of all of them. His alternative was to go straight to the known sources, LinkedIn companies, LinkedIn jobs, Crunchbase, with off-the-shelf and quickly built self-healing scrapers, merge with basic conflict heuristics, and pay zero tokens. Coverage was decent, cost was dramatically lower, and pricing a week of setup at $5,000 put the break-even at just over 15,000 entities. His closing line is the one to keep: owned context compounds while rented context decays.

## Key Takeaways
- Bright Data's scale, offered as credentials: over 20,000 customers including a claimed 70% of the largest AI labs, more than 50 billion HTML pages a day and over 20 petabytes of video, audio and other media.
- The data decay chart is the analytical foundation of the talk and it is the part most worth internalising. Social content is stale in well under a day. News, finance and retail are largely irrelevant 30 days out. The consequence he draws is correct: web context is not a snapshot, not a monthly refresh, but a continuous process, and any cost model that prices a one-time collection is answering the wrong question.
- The search market restructuring in three years, from near-total Google dominance for humans, to search appearing inside chatbots which blurred the human and agent channels, to agent-native search companies indexing the web with no human user in the picture at all.
- Incumbents piling in: Amazon shipped its own index and web retrieval for agents on AgentCore, and Microsoft repackaged its search as part of its agentic development suite weeks earlier. His read is that the space is crowding fast because Google's synonymy with web search is genuinely shaking.
- The limit of search as a context primitive, and his examples are sharp. You can search for a product's price this morning. You cannot search for how that price moved over six months, what discounts it carried, or how a company's headcount changed over time, even though all of that existed on the web when it happened. Search retrieves the present. Context often means the history, and the history is only available to whoever was recording.
- What distinguishes CaaS from crawling: entity extraction, deduplication, enrichment and merging across sources into a knowledge graph. He characterises them as vertical search engines that are very good at one narrow domain, and situates them as the agent-facing evolution of data-as-a-service, with ZoomInfo's launch of a separate agent-facing brand as the incumbent's version of the same move.
- Test design, stated honestly as a test and not a benchmark: one entity type (company), 25 fields ranging from trivial (domain, name, headquarters) to hard (hiring and people data), an Opus 4.8 agent looping field by field with budget guardrails, run 100 times over the event's sponsors.
- Coverage result and the reason behind it, which is the most transferable finding: search-based approaches and plain Google converged well, and the two CaaS providers came in behind. The mechanism is closed-world versus open-world. A CaaS provider knows what it has collected and can never answer beyond it, while a searcher can keep exploring. He is careful to note the flip side, that the CaaS providers surely hold many fields his test never asked for, so coverage is only meaningful relative to a specific job.
- Cost result: most search and CaaS options and Google clustered together, but with a composition difference that matters. CaaS cost is what you pay the vendor. Search cost is vendor plus substantial token burn to structure raw results into something retrievable and actionable. Same output, different cost anatomy.
- The cheapest CaaS provider was also the least accurate, which he reads as a long-tail niche forming inside the category, lower quality data at much lower price for small shops that do not need more.
- Frequency, not volume, is the cost killer, and his framing of a million records is the useful one: not a million distinct entities in one run, but the same entities revisited repeatedly as new questions arise and as facts change. Private equity due diligence is his example, and the pattern is that you keep asking about the same entities forever.
- Every repeated query costs the same as the first even when the answer has not changed, and no false positive is free. Token cost does not shrink with repetition and volume discounts do not flatten the curve.
- The behavioural consequence is the real damage, and it is easy to miss because it never shows up as a failure. Teams silently degrade their own systems to control cost: research once a week instead of daily, take 10 results instead of all, do not ask the marginal question. The capability exists and goes unused.
- The build alternative: skip the middleman entirely because for company enrichment everyone knows the sources, which are the same sources the CaaS vendors and ZoomInfo pull from. Use existing scrapers for LinkedIn companies, LinkedIn jobs and Crunchbase, build two more with an AI scraper builder in minutes, merge with simple conflict-resolution heuristics, and incur zero token cost.
- The ontology observation is genuinely good and underdeveloped in the talk: LinkedIn already models companies, people and jobs as linked entities, so for many domains the knowledge graph you were going to construct already exists in the source's own schema. You are often paying a vendor to rebuild structure that was never lost.
- The tipping point: price a week of setup at $5,000, and break-even against renting arrives at just over 15,000 entities or queries in his scenario. He is appropriately hedged that the real number could be 10,000 or 100,000 depending on the case, and his substantive claim is that it is lower than most teams assume.
- Why owning changes behaviour rather than just cost: retrieval after the upfront investment is effectively free, so you can re-ask, re-query, and stop rationing. He rates the removal of corner-cutting as possibly the most important effect, plus the ability to attach custom business logic and join with proprietary data.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
Strip the vendor framing and this is a memoisation problem with a staleness constraint, which is a shape you already know from caching, and stating it that way makes the missing analysis obvious. Renting context means recomputing an answer on every request at constant marginal cost. Owning context means paying once to materialise a store and then serving from it at near-zero marginal cost. The break-even is the standard amortisation crossover, and his $5,000 over 15,000 queries is that calculation done honestly. What he leaves out is that a cache has two parameters, not one. He models hit cost and build cost and never models the refresh rate, which is strange because his own opening slide is a decay curve. If retail and finance data is stale in 30 days, then an owned store is not a one-time build, it is a build plus a recurring recrawl whose frequency is set by the decay rate of the fields you care about. The correct model has a periodic invalidation term, and once you include it the owned option's cost curve is not flat, it is a shallow staircase. His slogan that owned context compounds while rented decays is contradicted by his own first chart: owned context decays too, you just get to choose when to pay for the refresh.

That correction makes the decision better rather than worse, and it changes what you optimise. The right variable is not build versus rent, it is refresh rate per field, because his own 25-field task mixes fields with wildly different decay constants. Company domain, name and headquarters are effectively static and should be crawled once and never again. Job postings and headcount move weekly. News moves hourly. Renting treats all 25 fields at the same cost and the same freshness, which means you overpay enormously for the static fields and underpay in staleness for the volatile ones. The dominant architecture is therefore neither of his two options: materialise the slow-moving majority of fields once, and rent live retrieval only for the fast-decaying minority. That is a per-field policy over a mixed store, and it beats both endpoints of his comparison. The fact that a vendor talk arrives at a binary when the answer is a mixture is the clearest sign of where the framing is doing sales work.

His coverage result is the most interesting empirical finding and it has a clean structural explanation that he gets right and then does not exploit. CaaS underperformed because it is a closed world, capable only of answering from what it collected, while search is open world and can keep exploring. That is precisely the recall-versus-cost tradeoff between a small specialised model and a large general one, and the standard answer in that setting is a cascade rather than a choice. Query the owned or vertical store first at near-zero cost, detect a miss, and fall back to open search only for the residual. His own data says the residual is small, since coverage converged across methods, meaning the expensive open-world path is needed for a minority of fields. He never proposes this, and it is the design that dominates everything he tested. The engineering difficulty is entirely in miss detection, because a knowledge store that returns a stale or partial answer with no signal of incompleteness is worse than one that returns nothing, and this is the same abstention-calibration problem that determines whether any cascade works. Coverage metadata, per-field timestamps and an explicit not-collected response are the primitives that make it possible, and a CaaS provider that shipped them would be far more useful in a cascade than one that optimises its own standalone coverage number.

The cost-composition point deserves to be lifted out because it is the sort of thing that gets lost in a total-cost bar chart. CaaS cost is vendor fees. Search cost is vendor fees plus token burn to structure unstructured results. Those are the same total and completely different things, because the token component is the one you can attack with the techniques you actually work on. Structuring search results into 25 typed fields is a bounded extraction task with a fixed schema, which is close to the ideal case for a small distilled model or a quantised local one, and running it on Opus-class inference is the single most obviously wasteful choice in his entire setup. He gestures at this once, noting there is a lot of optimisation left and that lighter models could be used, and then moves past it. Ballpark it: if extraction is a meaningful fraction of the search-path cost and a distilled extractor cuts that fraction by an order of magnitude at comparable field accuracy, the tipping point he computed moves substantially to the right, because he is comparing a cost-optimised build against a deliberately unoptimised rent. His conclusion may still hold, but his crossover number is not defensible as stated.

The behavioural argument is the strongest thing in the talk and it is the one with the least quantitative support, which is a shame because it is measurable. Metered retrieval causes teams to ration, and the rationing is invisible because it appears as a design decision rather than a failure. Weekly instead of daily, 10 results instead of all, skip the marginal question. This is exactly the dynamic that governs whether any expensive capability actually gets used, and it is why fixed-cost infrastructure produces different behaviour than metered infrastructure at identical total spend. It is the same reason a local model gets used for experiments that an API-metered model does not, even when the API is cheaper in aggregate. If you want the argument to land with a budget owner, the measurable version is query volume before and after the pricing model changes, holding capability constant. Nobody appears to have published that, and it would be a genuinely useful result.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The competitive facts check out and are more specific than he gives them. Web Search on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore went generally available on 19 June 2026 as a managed connector for AgentCore Gateway, running over an Amazon-operated index spanning tens of billions of documents refreshed within minutes, with semantic snippet extraction tuned for model context, Amazon Knowledge Graph integration for verified facts, and a query path contained inside the customer's AWS environment with no data egress. Pricing is $7 per 1,000 queries. Two details matter for his argument. First, Amazon is combining a web index with a knowledge graph, which is the CaaS pattern absorbed into a hyperscaler platform rather than a separate category, and platform absorption is usually what happens to a category before its independent vendors get squeezed. Second, the zero-egress design targets exactly the enterprise objection that keeps regulated buyers off third-party retrieval APIs, which is a distribution advantage no independent CaaS provider can match.

The funding and pricing picture around agent-native search confirms his read that the category is real and adds the part he omits, which is that it is consolidating. Exa raised an $85M Series B at roughly a $700M valuation, Parallel raised $100M, and Nebius acquired Tavily in February 2026. Raw search pricing has converged tightly, with Exa at $7 per 1,000 requests and Tavily around $7.50 to $8 per 1,000 basic searches, matching AgentCore's $7. When four independent providers and a hyperscaler all price within 15% of each other, the search hop is commoditising, which supports his thesis that the differentiation and the money have moved to structure, history and ownership rather than to retrieval itself.

The one dimension he never measures, and it is a serious omission for an agentic use case, is latency. Independent benchmarking across agent search APIs finds a 20-fold spread, from 669ms at the fast end to 13.6 seconds for Parallel's deep mode. His test looped field by field over 25 fields per entity, so per-hop latency multiplies by 25 and then by the number of entities, and a 20x spread across providers means his cost-converged options are not remotely time-converged. For an interactive agent that difference decides whether the product exists, which is the point the Oxylabs talk from the same event made explicitly. Cost parity with a 20x latency spread means the cheapest option on his chart may be unusable, and nothing in his analysis would reveal it.

The build-your-own case has an external headwind he does not mention and it is scheduled rather than speculative. Cloudflare began blocking AI crawlers by default in July 2025 with a Pay Per Crawl marketplace returning HTTP 402, and from 15 September 2026, one month after this talk, default settings block mixed-use crawlers from any ad-bearing page, with Pay Per Crawl becoming Pay Per Use. On the legal side, collecting public data is comparatively settled in the collector's favour after hiQ and the January 2024 summary judgment for Bright Data itself against Meta, but Reddit's pending DMCA Section 1201 suit against Perplexity and several data collection providers targets circumvention of rate limits and anti-bot measures specifically. Both facts bear directly on his tipping point. Self-hosted scraping of LinkedIn and Crunchbase carries access risk and potentially a metered access cost that a vendor absorbs on your behalf, and part of what a CaaS fee actually buys is that the vendor is the party in the litigation. His $5,000 setup estimate prices engineering time and nothing else. A more honest model prices setup, per-field refresh, access fees under emerging pay-per-crawl regimes, and the expected cost of being the named defendant, and every one of those moves the crossover to the right.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Rebuild his tipping-point calculation with a refresh term before using it for anything, because his own decay chart invalidates the flat-cost assumption underneath it. Owned context is a cache with an invalidation schedule, so the model is build cost plus refresh frequency times refresh cost, compared against per-query rental. His slogan about owned context compounding is only true for fields that do not decay.
2. Set the refresh rate per field rather than per dataset, which is the design his binary framing hides. In his own 25-field task, company name and headquarters are effectively static, headcount and job postings move weekly, and news moves hourly. Materialise the slow fields once and rent live retrieval only for the fast ones. This mixed policy beats both of the options he compared and is the actual answer.
3. Build the cascade he described but never proposed. Query the owned store first at near-zero marginal cost, detect misses, and fall back to open search only for the residual. His coverage data says the residual is small. The entire difficulty is miss detection, so require per-field timestamps, coverage metadata and an explicit not-collected response from any store you build or buy. This is the same abstention-calibration problem as any confidence-thresholded cascade, and it is exactly your area.
4. Do not run schema-constrained extraction on a frontier model, which is the largest unexamined inefficiency in his experiment. Structuring search results into 25 typed fields is a bounded task with a fixed output schema, which is the canonical case for a distilled or quantised extractor. He compares an optimised build against an unoptimised rent, so his crossover number is not usable until the extraction path is cost-optimised on both sides.
5. Add latency to any retrieval provider comparison you run, using a tail percentile, because he omitted it entirely and the external spread is 20x, from 669ms to 13.6 seconds. In a field-by-field loop, per-hop latency multiplies by field count and then by entity count. Cost parity across providers tells you almost nothing about whether the resulting agent is interactive.
6. Treat the rationing effect as a real design consideration and measure it. Metered retrieval makes teams silently degrade their own systems, weekly instead of daily, 10 results instead of all, and it never appears as a failure. If you argue for fixed-cost infrastructure internally, the measurable case is query volume before and after the pricing model changes at constant capability, which is a study nobody appears to have published and which would be genuinely useful.
7. Check whether the ontology you were about to build already exists in the source. His LinkedIn observation is the underrated engineering point of the talk: companies, people and jobs are already modelled as linked entities, so a large part of what knowledge-graph vendors charge for is reconstructing structure that was never actually lost. Look at the source schema before designing an entity resolution pipeline.
8. Price access risk and access fees into any build decision, not just engineering time. Cloudflare's default block on mixed-use crawlers for ad-bearing pages lands 15 September 2026 and Pay Per Crawl is becoming Pay Per Use, so scraped-data acquisition is becoming metered. Reddit's DMCA 1201 suit against Perplexity and its data suppliers is pending. Part of what a vendor fee buys is that the vendor, not you, is the named party.
