# Your LLM Stack Is a 2008 Database With Better Marketing

**Channel:** AI Engineer  
**Published:** 2026-07-20  
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XjI-AR4pt7Y  

## TL;DR
Lovina Dmello, senior software developer on NVIDIA's deep learning infrastructure team, argues that almost nothing breaking in production ML security is an exotic AI attack. It is the same boring infrastructure failure we supposedly solved a decade ago: over-privileged accounts, flat networks, and secrets sitting in the open. Her framing is that we replaced every assumption of the 2008 application stack (deterministic behavior, fixed parameters, single tenant) with probabilistic behavior, copyable weights, and shared multi-tenant GPUs, but we never replaced the security assumptions that went with them. Secure the LLM stack like a database, not like a model.

## Key Takeaways
- **Misconfiguration beats missing features.** An audit of 50 real production ML setups found at least one critical security mistake in 78% of them. The same three defects recur: wide-open access control, no segmentation between system components, and credentials or model weights in reachable storage.
- **Defenses are priced in latency, and that is why they never ship.** She tiers the cost honestly: basic controls (auth, input validation) run under 8% overhead and should be non-negotiable; heavier workload isolation runs 10 to 20% and should be applied selectively; real-time malicious-input detection runs 15 to 30% and is the tier researchers love most and production rejects. The useful question is not "should I do this" but "how do I do this without slowing everything down."
- **Four-pillar defense in depth:** infrastructure security at the base (containers, network, GPU virtualization, encryption), then access control, then runtime security, wrapped in practice and culture. Failures cascade upward. A perfect RBAC policy is decoration if the cluster underneath is open.
- **Maturity model mapped to NIST AI RMF, priced in overhead:** level 1 under 5% is test-environment only, level 2 at 5 to 10% is the real production baseline, level 3 adds controls regulated industries need. Most teams believe they are at level 3 and are actually at level 1 or 2.
- **Her four watch items, flagged as unsettled:** prompt injection (the model cannot reliably separate your instructions from someone else's input), RAG poisoning, GPU side channels from multi-tenant packing, and unverified model/add-on supply chain.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The talk is unusually direct about the security/performance tradeoff being an engineering problem rather than a research one, which is exactly the framing that matters for inference work. Two mechanics deserve attention.

First, the multi-tenant GPU packing that makes serving economics work is also the thing that creates the side-channel surface. Every packing decision that raises utilization (shared SMs, MPS, MIG partitions sized for density rather than isolation, KV-cache sharing across tenants) is simultaneously an isolation decision. The 10 to 20% isolation tax she quotes is essentially the cost of giving up some of that packing efficiency, and it lands on exactly the metric an inference team is optimizing.

Second, her claim that the same check "done sloppily can double response time, done well is fair" is the actual optimization target. Input validation and policy evaluation are usually implemented as synchronous Python in the request path. Moving them off the critical path (batched validation, evaluation overlapped with prefill, policy decisions cached per session rather than per token) is the difference between the green tier and the purple tier. The security overhead numbers in the talk are not laws of nature, they are the current state of unoptimized implementations.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The Ray story she opens with is real and has gotten materially worse since the 2023 disclosure. The vulnerability, [CVE-2023-48022](https://www.sentinelone.com/vulnerability-database/cve-2023-48022/), lets anyone with network access to the Ray Dashboard port (8265) submit arbitrary jobs, because the job submission API ships with no authentication. Oligo's original [ShadowRay](https://www.oligo.security/blog/shadowray-attack-ai-workloads-actively-exploited-in-the-wild) research found clusters under active exploitation for at least seven months with roughly a billion dollars of compromised compute, which matches her "over a billion dollars" figure. Anyscale classified it as expected behavior for trusted networks rather than a vulnerability, and only added token authentication in Ray 2.52.0. In November 2025 the campaign returned as [ShadowRay 2.0](https://thehackernews.com/2025/11/shadowray-20-exploits-unpatched-ray.html), a self-spreading GPU cryptomining botnet built on the same unpatched flaw. Her point stands harder than she made it: this was not just a misconfiguration, it was a default that stayed exploitable for over two years because nobody owned the boundary between the ML team and the security team.

Her prompt-injection and supply-chain flags were validated within days of this talk by two independent events. Snyk's [ToxicSkills audit](https://snyk.io/blog/toxicskills-malicious-ai-agent-skills-clawhub/) of 3,984 agent skills found 36.8% carrying at least one security flaw, 13.4% with a critical issue, and 76 confirmed malicious payloads, with 91% of confirmed malware combining a natural-language jailbreak with an executable shell payload. That is precisely her "supply chain plus prompt injection" pair, weaponized. Separately, the [Five Eyes joint statement](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ai-bypass-cybersecurity-systems-months-not-years-five-eyes/) of June 22, 2026 warned that frontier models will outpace prevailing cyber defenses in months rather than years. So her closing "treat it as a direction, not a gospel" is if anything too humble on timing.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
Audit your own inference and training infrastructure against her three misconfigurations before touching anything model-side, because that is where your actual risk is:

1. **Check every serving and orchestration dashboard for default-off auth.** Ray, vLLM's OpenAI-compatible server, Triton's metrics endpoint, Prometheus, and Jupyter/notebook ports on training boxes. If you run Ray anywhere, confirm you are on 2.52.0+ with token auth enabled, and run Anyscale's Open Ports Checker. This is a one-afternoon task with the highest risk-reduction-per-hour of anything in this list.
2. **Separate the routing tier's credentials from the model tier's.** LLM routing work concentrates exactly the kitchen-sink credential problem she describes: a router needs to reach every backend provider, so its key ring is the union of all provider access. Scope per-backend credentials and make the router hold short-lived, per-destination tokens rather than a permanent superset.
3. **Instrument the security overhead you are already paying, then optimize it as a latency problem.** Measure what your current input validation, auth check, and logging cost at p50 and p99 in your serving path. If they land in her purple tier (15%+), that is not a reason to remove them, it is a kernel and pipelining problem worth one sprint. Getting a control from 20% to 6% overhead is what makes it deployable everywhere, which is the actual frontier she identifies.
