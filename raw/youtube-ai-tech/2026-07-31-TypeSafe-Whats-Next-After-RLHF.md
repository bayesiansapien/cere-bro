# What's Next After RLHF? (Diogo Almeida, TypeSafe AI)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-07-31
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o

## TL;DR
Almeida co-authored InstructGPT, ChatGPT, and GPT-4, and uses that standing to argue his own work created a structural dead end. His resolution of the AI-is-amazing versus AI-is-a-bubble split is a single distinction: everything models excel at is intrinsically human-in-the-loop (the goal is to please a human), and everything they fail at is automation (the goal is to remove the human). This is not coincidence, it is the objective. RLHF optimizes human preference, so models are engineered to look right whether or not they are right, which is precisely disqualifying for unsupervised decision-making. His most contrarian claim: Claude Code is still the assistance era, not the next one, because it is still RLHF-shaped. The next paradigm is neither RLHF nor RLVR but a third objective, calibrated decision-making.

## Key Takeaways
- **The two cults.** One: every benchmark falls, autonomous operating time grows exponentially. Two: it is a bubble, circular financing, no value, everything is a chat app. Both cite real evidence. The split needs explaining, not picking a side.
- **The unifying explanation.** Left column (unsolved math problems, competitive coding) are tasks whose objective is to please the human in the loop. Right column (customer service decisions, back-office work) are tasks whose objective is to eliminate the human. The second category is simpler and models are worse at it. Assistance versus automation.
- **We literally put the human in the loop.** RLHF's objective is human preference. Asking why LLMs require a human in the loop answers itself.
- **Overpromising is a designed feature.** Because the reward model scores preference, there will always be a gap between preference scores and actual results. The fart-audio-as-music example: asked for an honest reaction to fart noises, ChatGPT called it "a very eerie vibe atmosphere piece." Under uncertainty, RLHF models default to what maximizes preference. The endgame of RLHF is engagement optimization.
- **What automation actually needs.** A model that does not care about the human at all and does the task correctly in a calibrated way.
- **The stakes heuristic every business has converged on.** Do not use AI for decisions with stakes to your business. The common pattern is pushing all cost onto the user (infinite doc-bot loops in customer service are fine) while keeping expensive decisions away from the model. Almeida calls this a horrible pattern and an accurate description of the present.
- **Claude Code is the same era.** Still RLHF. A purely RLVR system would look very different. The visible tension where models get better at agentic tasks and stop following instructions is two RLHF/RLVR objectives trading against each other, and neither one adds up to automation.
- **The software argument.** SaaS has not changed since 2019 except for bolted-on chatbots. This is exactly what you predict if AI is assistance-native: the only thing you can do with an assistant is put it beside the software. Gary Tan's "golden age of just-in-time software" is a double-edged compliment. Software got cheaper to write, not smarter. The building blocks of software have not become more expressive. Almeida calls that tragic.
- **His inversion of the bitter lesson.** Sutton says algorithms matter more than compute, true in games and false in reality. Almeida's stack: data matters more than compute, and doing the right task matters far more than data. Each post-training branch has a North Star. RLHF optimizes human preference. RLVR optimizes log error rate of pure correctness. TypeSafe optimizes calibrated decision-making.
- **On hallucination.** Pre-training is not the problem, it is phenomenal, and pre-trained models are highly intelligent. Hallucination is intrinsic to preference optimization, via a GAN-like reward model asymmetry that encourages mode dropping and overconfidence, because low confidence is easy for a reward model to detect and punish.
- **Teaser.** He claims the original scaling laws were incorrect and promised a post on it.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The mode-dropping mechanism is the technically substantive claim and it deserves unpacking because Almeida cut himself off mid-explanation. A reward model trained on human preference comparisons can detect hedging cheaply, since expressed uncertainty is a surface feature. It cannot cheaply detect factual wrongness, which requires knowledge. So the gradient reliably penalizes visible uncertainty and only unreliably penalizes error. Under that asymmetry, the policy's best move is to collapse onto a confident mode regardless of its posterior. The GAN analogy is apt: the discriminator's blind spots determine which modes survive.

For anyone doing calibration-sensitive work, this reframes what a post-RLHF model's logits mean. If preference optimization systematically compresses the confidence distribution toward high confidence, then confidence-thresholded routing (send to the big model when the small model is unsure) is built on a signal that RLHF specifically degrades. A base or mid-trained checkpoint may be better calibrated than its RLHF'd descendant, which is an empirically checkable and consequential claim.

The assistance-versus-automation frame also gives a clean way to segment routing traffic. Assistance-shaped queries tolerate a confident-but-wrong answer because a human catches it. Automation-shaped queries do not. These are different cost functions and should not share a routing threshold.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Almeida's credentials check out. He is an InstructGPT and GPT-4 co-author, and TypeSafe AI was founded in mid-2024 with Erik Gafni and Sasha Sheng, based in San Francisco. The product direction is more concrete than the talk lets on: they build TLMs (Typesafe Language Models) that do not generate text at all but emit structured outputs such as classifications, scores, and decisions with associated confidence, aimed at compliance pipelines, real-time decision systems, data reconciliation, and agents running without supervision. Reliability is built into the model rather than bolted on as guardrails. Read the talk as a well-argued thesis that also happens to be his company's positioning.

His hallucination mechanism is directionally supported by OpenAI's own "Why Language Models Hallucinate" (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, Zhang, arXiv 2509.04664), but with a meaningful difference in emphasis. That paper's argument is that training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty, and it puts significant blame on the hundreds of accuracy-based evals that penalize humility, noting that a single good hallucination eval cannot counteract them. It also finds that RLHF, RLAIF, and DPO have been shown to reduce hallucinations even while overconfident behavior persists. So the literature partially contradicts the strong form of Almeida's claim that RLHF causes hallucination. The more defensible version is that preference optimization plus accuracy-only evaluation jointly select for overconfidence, and RLHF alone is not the sole culprit. Separately, there is formal work establishing an inherent consistency-versus-breadth tradeoff: any model generalizing beyond its training data will either hallucinate invalid outputs or mode-collapse. If that result holds, the mode-dropping Almeida describes may not be purely an RLHF artifact.

The SaaS-has-not-changed observation is the weakest empirical claim in the talk and he offers no data for it. It is a rhetorically effective simplification, and the two-year-plus adoption lag typical of enterprise software makes it hard to falsify either way.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **Test whether RLHF degrades calibration on your own checkpoints.** This is the single most decision-relevant claim in the talk and it is directly testable. Take a base or mid-trained checkpoint and its RLHF'd descendant, run both on a labeled set, and plot reliability diagrams (confidence versus accuracy, expected calibration error). If the RLHF'd model is worse calibrated, every confidence-thresholded routing policy built on instruct models is standing on a degraded signal, and the fix might be as simple as routing on a base-model confidence score.
2. **Split routing thresholds by assistance versus automation.** Queries where a human reviews the answer and queries where the output feeds a downstream system have fundamentally different costs for confident errors. Tag traffic by which regime it is in and set separate confidence thresholds. Most routers use one threshold for both, which is a category error.
3. **Stop using preference-style LLM judges to evaluate compressed models.** If preference optimization rewards appearing right, then an LLM judge inherits that bias and will systematically prefer the more confident-sounding model, which is not the same as the more accurate one. For pruning and quantization evaluation, prefer verifiable graders or calibration metrics over pairwise preference judging.
4. **Add expected calibration error to the compression scorecard.** Accuracy-preserving quantization that destroys calibration is a real and under-measured failure mode, and it matters much more in the automation regime than the assistance regime. Report ECE alongside accuracy for every compression variant. Cheap to add, and nobody does it.
5. **Watch for the scaling-laws claim.** Almeida teased that the original scaling laws were incorrect. Given he is an InstructGPT author, that is worth following up on directly rather than through secondhand coverage.
