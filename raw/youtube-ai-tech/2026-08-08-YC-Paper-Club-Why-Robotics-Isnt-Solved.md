# Why Robotics Still Isn't Solved, But Could Be Soon (YC Paper Club)

**Channel:** Y Combinator
**Published:** 2026-08-08
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=myDCd0hNqQU

## TL;DR
The host opens by noting this is the tenth consecutive year he has been told robotics will be solved next year, and lays out four walls nobody has scaled: the sim-to-real gap in physics-respecting world models, action-conditioned dynamics and action-space representation, the sensorimotor deficit (humans have distributed tactile sensing everywhere, robots have one force-torque coin per fingertip), and embodiment drift, where actuators corrode and batteries fade until your teleop data is stale and the VLA needs retraining. Four talks then attack different walls. Physical Intelligence's MEM adds two-tier memory to VLAs so a robot stops washing dishes forever. R&B-EnCoRe treats embodied chain-of-thought as a latent variable and discovers per-embodiment reasoning rather than applying a fixed template, then drops the reasoning at inference to avoid the latency cost. SimToolReal abandons teleop entirely and trains one goal-conditioned RL policy in sim that transfers zero-shot to twelve unseen tools. Rerun's CEO argues the winning company shape is the "robotics application company" that starts with teleop and off-the-shelf hardware. General Instinct closes with the most directly transferable content: world action models are excellent and economically unusable at two GB200s per robot, and they compress them onto a Jetson.

## Key Takeaways

**The four walls (host's framing)**
- Sim-to-real: video world models do not respect physics. Drive a car into a Whole Foods in a learned world model and the store turns into a highway rather than a collision. Deformable objects are worse.
- Action conditioning is much harder than next-state prediction. Estimating the transition function from state alone is tractable; conditioning on action requires far more data, and the representation of the action space itself is an unsolved problem if you want fast learning.
- The sensorimotor wall, which he argues is under-discussed. Human skin detects normal force, tangential force, moisture, temperature, and vibration, and estimates the coefficient of friction, everywhere on the body. Robots get roughly one force-torque sensor per fingertip plus a wrist camera. His test: you can identify everything in your backpack by feel alone, with no vision, and no robot can do that because none has an epidermis. His illustration of the failure mode is trying to untie hockey skates with cold hands, where the policy degrades because the sensing degraded.
- Embodiment drift, which only people who have run robots for long periods notice. Actuators accumulate dust, corrode, and rust near the ocean. Force per commanded action shifts over time, and battery power delivery shifts too. Your teleop data goes stale and the whole VLA needs recollection and retraining.
- Teleop data collection is remarkably hard and finicky, and if scaling that is the plan, the plan is doomed.

**MEM, multi-scale embodied memory (Marcel Torne, Stanford, work done at Physical Intelligence)**
- The problem: most policies including Pi-0.5 and GR00T have no memory at all, so every timestep is context-free. Demonstrated failures: a robot washing dishes forever because it has no sense of elapsed time, and a grilled cheese burned black.
- Adding memory naively fails twice. Effectiveness drops from distribution shift and data scarcity, and efficiency collapses because longer context means longer training and slower inference.
- Their answer is compression plus decomposition. Split the policy into a high-level policy that emits the next step and a low-level VLA that executes. Split the memory to match: short dense visual context into the low-level policy, compressed language summary of the last few minutes into the high-level policy.
- Short-term memory implementation: a ViT variant with added temporal attention layers, dropping all tokens except the current image, on the argument that temporal attention has already folded the history into it. Benefits are easy ViT initialization (only temporal attention is new), heavy token reduction, and fast inference.
- Long-term memory is a recurrent text scratchpad. The high-level policy predicts its own memory string, which is fed back in. Text is far cheaper than images in tokens, it stays physically accurate because the policy edits it as events happen, and it is less prone to distribution shift.
- Tasks unlocked: grilled cheese with correct waiting, unloading groceries where items are only intermittently visible to the wrist camera so counts must be remembered, window cleaning that terminates, and a multi-tens-of-minutes recipe preparation task.
- The result he is most excited about is in-context adaptation. Memoryless policies repeat the identical mistake in an infinite loop (missing the same chopstick, pulling the wrong side of a fridge). With memory the policy notices its own failure and changes strategy, going lower for the chopstick or switching sides on the fridge.
- Honest limitations he volunteered: the high-level policy is trained with SFT, so annotators had to be told in advance what was worth remembering, which is a hand-designed prior. He wants RL over the memory space to discover it instead. And he concedes text is not how humans store memory, that a latent embedding would be more expressive, and that text wins today only on supervisability and debuggability.

**R&B-EnCoRe, refine and bootstrap embodiment-specific reasoning (Milan Ganai, Stanford, at Waymo)**
- Why embodied chain-of-thought at all: robotics is data-scarce, so any extra annotation signal is valuable, and because it is text a human can read why the robot decided something.
- The chicken-and-egg problem he identifies: there is no oracle source of reasoning data (no internet corpus of embodied reasoning traces) and no oracle model (you cannot pry open a human brain to get the mapping from image to fingertip motion).
- The second problem is verbosity, which is a latency problem. Should you replan at every step? Should you enumerate every object in the scene? Both may be redundant or actively distracting.
- Method: treat reasoning as an unobserved latent variable over context and action, then use variational inference. A reasoning proposer generates candidate trace types (visible objects, plans, gripper position, subtasks), and a validator scores them on three axes: concision, non-triviality (generalization), and most importantly action-predictiveness, which is what grounds the trace in the specific embodiment.
- Findings differ by embodiment, which is the point. Manipulation: move and gripper-position reasoning helps, perceptual reasoning does not because scenes are full of distractor objects. Legged locomotion: structural affordances and movements matter far more than terrain or counterfactuals. Self-driving: meta-action and visible-object reasoning are useful, and the method prunes hallucinated human annotations.
- Action forcing is the deployment trick worth stealing. Reasoning is used as co-training signal, then dropped at inference, so you get the annotation benefit without paying the token latency. This directly answers the objection that AVs cannot afford chain-of-thought.
- Headline conclusion: selective reasoning beats exhaustive reasoning. Valid reasoning is not necessarily useful reasoning. Reasoning is a budgeted resource to be discovered per embodiment, not a fixed template.

**SimToolReal (Tyler Lum, Stanford)**
- Premise: skip teleop. Highly dexterous actions cannot be demonstrated reliably at scale because of embodiment mismatch and missing force feedback, which forces humans into slow deliberate control.
- Train entirely in GPU-accelerated sim with tens of thousands of parallel robots, roughly 1000x real time, buying decades of interaction in days. Scale data with compute instead of human effort. Because it optimizes reward rather than imitating, it exceeds human dexterity rather than matching it.
- The unification insight: treat all dexterous tool manipulation as goal reaching. No task label. Move the object from current 6D pose to desired pose, through a sequence of goals. That single objective covers grasping, in-hand reorientation, spinning, and tool use, which prior work handled with one policy and one reward design per skill.
- Training uses procedurally generated primitive objects (cylinders and cuboids only) with random goals sampled as a position plus rotation, then deltas up to 10 cm and 90 degrees. Primitives were chosen partly because simulation runs two to three times faster on simple geometry.
- At inference, goal poses come from a human video via FoundationPose and SAM. Critically the video supplies no robot actions and does not train or fine-tune anything. The policy is frozen and the trajectory acts as a task prompt, so a new task takes minutes instead of a training run.
- Architecture: proprioception, current object pose, a bounding box of the graspable region, and current goal pose, through an LSTM, out to joint position targets for a 22-DoF hand and 7-DoF arm at 60 Hz.
- Train-test correlation was validated by evaluating checkpoints throughout training: as performance on random primitives and random goals rises, performance on unseen real tools with human-demonstrated trajectories rises sharply with it.
- Baselines: a fixed-grasp baseline must rotate with the arm alone and collides with the table, which is the evidence that in-hand reorientation is necessary rather than optional. Kinematic retargeting of human fingertip motion fails to even grasp because it never reasons about contact forces.
- Failure analysis is the honest part: roughly 60% of all failures come from pose tracking, not from the policy. Small and occludable objects like a marker are worst. Longer-handled and larger tools with more visual features track better.
- Emergent recovery was not trained for. It came from a domain randomization detail, random forces applied to the object in simulation that sometimes knocked it out of the hand, so the policy accumulated experience recovering. Without that it likely never drops anything in sim and never learns to recover.
- His answer on evaluation is worth quoting for anyone doing empirical work: real-world eval is expensive and rarely statistically significant, so you get a vibe check unless you run a hundred trials, and you cannot distinguish 90% from 87%. They lean on automated sim evaluation of the real tool set, while noting sim sometimes scores higher via magical tossing and catching that the real pose tracker would fail on.
- On the LSTM: chosen because the working RL codebase already had it and it performed better. RL is finicky enough that you do not rewrite what works. He believes transformers are better data sponges given unlimited data, and that this is not that regime. The host adds that in the Diffusion Policy paper the convolutional variant beats the transformer on about half the tasks, with the caveat that transformers are more tuning-sensitive.
- Follow-up work Play2Perfect: pretrain a shared dexterous prior through task-agnostic play in free space, then fine-tune on sparse-reward contact-rich assembly, deploy zero-shot. Motivation is that millimetre-precision assembly is too hard to learn from scratch.
- Everything is open sourced including policy weights, with an interactive demo that runs client-side in a phone browser.

**Rerun and the robotics application company (Nico, co-founder and CEO)**
- His thesis: the emerging winning shape is the "robotics application company," sometimes called the new integrator. Own a full business problem end to end, build as little custom hardware as possible, start with pure teleop, fine-tune open models rather than building foundation models. He predicts this category becomes the new SaaS.
- The playbook: find one customer problem someone will pay for, solve it with teleop and off-the-shelf hardware, deploy fast. The physical world is brutal, everything breaks, and you cannot enumerate failure modes in a lab.
- The asymmetry that makes teleop-first correct: if you can teleop it you can generally train a model to do it, and the converse does not hold.
- What deploying teaches you that planning cannot: the real viability threshold (a thousand good units a day), that failure may be acceptable if you can discriminate bad output cheaply, that customers actually care most about onboarding speed for new designs, that a physical fixture can halve failures because most failures were picking from a pile, how many hours of human practice teleop requires (which determines whether you must hire and train operators), and that your cheap research arms will break and you will change suppliers.
- Every robotics company he has visited that actually ships has a replica of the customer environment in its own office. Not one exception.
- Build a repeatable success metric by hand first, before automating it. This is where you encode what matters to this specific business, and it is the source of stickiness that generic model companies will not replicate.
- Good data is tautologically data that makes the model better, so you must train, evaluate, and debug data continuously. Researchers at well-funded labs told him the most common outcome of debugging a policy is realising you need to send different instructions to your data collectors. Collecting everything up front and training later is a large mistake.
- Why not Postgres: physical data is multimodal, multi-rate, episodic, and carries 3D semantics with deeply nested structure. Forcing it into table-based systems makes it very hard to query and inefficient to store and process, and teams that get the storage layer wrong accumulate workarounds.
- Where he sees traction now: data centre management, warehouse robotics, construction, tabletop and small-scale manufacturing, and food. Companies that can build a reliable robot are 100% supply constrained and have no trouble filling demand.
- His answer on why no billion-dollar revenue examples yet: robotics is death by a thousand cuts, and cheap experimentation only just arrived. There are many more arms on the market and base models are far better than two years ago, so previously everyone had to raise huge rounds and attack general problems.
- Contrast in resource allocation: frontier labs spend enormous compute per researcher, including large CPU spend purely to cut latency on searching and exploring data. Application startups instead minimize moving parts, prioritize fast turnaround on new data, and full-stack debuggability.

**General Instinct: making world action models economically viable (Bill and Guanming, YC)**
- The framing meme, from Jim Fan: VLAs are dead, world action models are next. The counter-meme they offer: VLAs are not dead, because they are small.
- Difference between the two: a VLA is a VLM predicting actions through an action head from the current observation only, with no explicit learning of future kinematics. A WAM uses a diffusion transformer to jointly predict future frames and future kinematics, so the pixel-level future supervises the model on physics. Their example: watching a dropped bottle teaches the correlation between physics and the action you produce.
- The economic problem, stated bluntly: even after all available optimizations, running DreamZero takes two GB200s at roughly $70K each. That is not a scalable unit economics for the robotics industry.
- Where the cost comes from: producing one 16-frame chunk requires running the DiT 32 times, because classifier-free guidance needs both a conditional and an unconditional flow-matching pass, and inference is autoregressive over 50 to 100 steps.
- Three published escape routes they survey. Predict only the future end state instead of the whole video (Image-RAM). Skip decoding entirely and use the DiT hidden state directly as the condition for the action head (FastOne), which removes the decoder from both training and inference. And the JEPA-style two-encoder approach with an MSE loss between current and future latents, versus treating the future as a distribution and using flow matching.
- Their own optimization stack, which is the most Amit-relevant list in the whole session: distill the VAE encoder-decoder, distill the DiT to a smaller one, split the single DiT into two (video transformer and action transformer) with cross-attention from video to action so future frames never need decoding, distill autoregressive flow-matching sampling from 50 to 100 steps down to one or two for a claimed 50x speedup with no performance drop, and explore alternative modalities (mask versus flow) for retaining world representation.
- They also describe asymmetrical denoising driven by measuring the energy of the keyframe cache, using that energy to decide per-frame whether to predict at full resolution, reduced resolution, or purely in latent space.
- Result: 500 ms per chunk of 16 actions on a Jetson.
- Their business rationale for building infrastructure rather than an application company: everyone is currently optimizing for generalization in research, but every one of these models eventually has to run in real time on the edge, and almost nobody is building that layer.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
Three of the five talks are, underneath, the same paper written for different audiences: the useful signal is dense and the representation is expensive, so find the cheapest representation that preserves the signal. MEM compresses history into a text scratchpad because text is orders of magnitude cheaper per token than images and because the compression is human-auditable. R&B-EnCoRe compresses reasoning by discovering which trace types are action-predictive for a given embodiment and pruning the rest, then compresses further by dropping reasoning entirely at inference via action forcing. General Instinct compresses the world model by removing the decoder, splitting the DiT, and collapsing the sampling schedule. Different levels of the stack, identical objective function.

The General Instinct numbers deserve the closest reading because they quantify where the waste actually is. Thirty-two DiT forward passes per 16-frame chunk decomposes into two independent multipliers: a factor of 2 from classifier-free guidance and a factor of 16 from the denoising schedule, compounded further by autoregression over 50 to 100 steps. Their 50x claim comes almost entirely from attacking the schedule (50 to 100 steps down to 1 to 2), which is the standard diffusion distillation result transplanted into a control loop. The structurally more interesting move is the DiT split with cross-attention, because it changes what is computed rather than how many times. Once the action transformer attends to the video transformer's hidden states, the pixel decoder becomes dead weight at inference, which is the same realization FastOne formalizes: the world representation was never in the decoded pixels, it was in the hidden state, and decoding was pure overhead you paid to supervise training. The asymmetrical denoising scheme, gating resolution on keyframe-cache energy, is essentially adaptive compute allocation driven by a cheap saliency proxy, which is the same family as early-exit and mixed-precision-by-layer.

SimToolReal is the cleanest illustration in the session that the bottleneck is often not the model. Sixty percent of failures came from FoundationPose, not from the LSTM policy. Any effort spent making the policy better would have moved the aggregate metric by at most 40% of the available headroom. This is the standard profiling discipline applied to a robot stack, and it is worth noting how rarely it is reported. The related point is his emergent-recovery finding: the capability that most impressed the audience came from one domain randomization detail (random perturbation forces knocking objects out of the hand), not from architecture or reward design. Coverage of the failure distribution in training produced robustness that no amount of policy capacity would have.

The unresolved thread running through the whole session is whether text is the right memory and reasoning substrate, and both presenters conceded it is not. Torne wants a latent embedding but keeps text for debuggability. Ganai points at continuous latent reasoning as the right long-term answer but argues text is how you exploit internet-scale VLM priors in a data-scarce regime. The host pushes on it twice with the same question: a dog does not reason in tokens. The honest answer given is that text is a strong prior plus supervisability, purchased at the cost of expressiveness and inference latency, and that action forcing is a partial way to stop paying the latency.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
All four papers are real, published, and the numbers presented were understated rather than inflated. MEM is arXiv 2603.03596, from a team spanning Stanford, UC Berkeley, and MIT, with Marcel Torne and Karl Pertsch as co-first authors alongside Homer Walke and Kyle Vedder. The published framing is that it gives Gemma 3-4B VLAs roughly 15 minutes of context while staying inside real-time inference limits, and the in-context adaptation results Torne demoed are quantified in the paper: +62% success on opening refrigerators with unknown hinge direction and +11% on picking up chopsticks at variable heights. The 62% figure is a large effect and he undersold it in the talk.

R&B-EnCoRe is arXiv 2602.08167, presented at RSS 2026, by Milan Ganai, Katie Luo, Jonas Frey, Clark Barrett, and Marco Pavone. The published gains across embodiments are 28% on manipulation success (Franka Panda in simulation and WidowX in hardware), 101% on legged navigation scores, and a 21% reduction in collision rate for driving, achieved without external rewards, verifiers, or human annotation. A related paper worth reading alongside it is "Do Vision-Language-Action Models Mean What They Say? On the Role of Faithfulness in Embodied Reasoning" (arXiv 2607.04681), which attacks the assumption that a readable chain-of-thought trace actually reflects the policy's computation, and therefore bears directly on Ganai's interpretability argument.

SimToolReal is arXiv 2602.16863 by Tyler Ga Wei Lum, Kushal Kedia, and Jeannette Bohg, with code and weights on GitHub. Play2Perfect is arXiv 2606.26428.

The DreamZero details need one correction. DreamZero is arXiv 2602.15922, a 14B world action model built by adapting Wan 2.1-I2V-14B-480P into a joint world-action model, and the published architecture explicitly has no separate inverse-dynamics module: action is simply another generated modality denoised inside the same monolithic DiT alongside video tokens. The slide Bill described with video prediction on the left and an IDM on the right is therefore a generic WAM diagram rather than DreamZero's actual architecture. The performance numbers justify the enthusiasm: 62.2% average task progress against 27.4% for the best pretrained VLA baseline, 49% task progress on unseen verbs on DROID against 25 to 32% for state-of-the-art VLAs, 42% relative improvement in cross-embodiment transfer from video-only demonstrations, and real-time control at 7 Hz already achieved through significant optimization. That 7 Hz figure is important context General Instinct did not mention: the published system is already real-time on adequate hardware, so their contribution is specifically about moving it to affordable edge hardware, not about making it real-time at all.

The efficiency problem they are attacking is now a crowded field, which is the strongest signal here. Efficient-WAM (arXiv 2606.10040) is a 1B-parameter world action model built around low-cost future imagination, a roughly 14x parameter reduction against DreamZero targeting the same capability. DSWAM (arXiv 2607.04927) proposes a dual-system world action foundation model for fine-grained manipulation, which is the same high-level and low-level decomposition MEM uses. OxyGen (arXiv 2603.14371) tackles unified KV cache management for VLA inference under multi-task parallelism, which is the serving-layer version of the same cost problem. NVIDIA has published its own overview of the world-action-model shift. So within roughly six months of DreamZero, the literature has split into a capability track and an efficiency track, exactly as happened with LLMs.

On General Instinct specifically, their public product is Instinct Edge: you supply a model, a target device, and a latency budget, and it returns an offline runtime that hits the budget on Jetson, mobile NPUs, ARM CPUs, Apple Neural Engine, and Snapdragon. The stated implementation is compression recipes plus custom CUDA, Metal, and ARM NEON kernels plus a continuous data-to-serving pipeline. That is a productized version of exactly the compression-and-kernels discipline, applied to robotics rather than language. For hardware context, Jetson Thor delivers roughly 7.5x the AI compute and 3.5x the energy efficiency of Jetson Orin, and community benchmarks put Pi-0.5 and GR00T N1.6/1.7 at around 23 Hz on Thor against 50 to 80 Hz on an RTX 5090, which frames how much headroom their 500 ms per 16-action chunk is actually working inside.

One claim to hold loosely: the two-GB200-at-$70K-each figure is Bill's, not a published benchmark, and the published DreamZero already runs at 7 Hz, so the hardware requirement depends heavily on which optimizations are assumed. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude argument about edge economics rather than a measured configuration.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Steal action forcing immediately for LLM routing work. It is the cleanest idea in the session and it generalizes directly. Train with chain-of-thought traces as auxiliary supervision, then drop them at inference. Applied to a router, this means training the small classifier on rich reasoning about why a query needs the expensive model, then serving a version that emits only the routing decision. You capture the annotation signal in the weights and pay none of the token latency, which is precisely the constraint that currently makes reasoning-based routers impractical.
2. Adopt the selective-reasoning result as a routing prior. Ganai's finding that valid reasoning is not necessarily useful reasoning, and that which reasoning helps is embodiment-specific and discoverable rather than designed, transfers to query classes. Run his variational setup over your own traffic: propose candidate reasoning types, score them on concision, non-triviality, and decision-predictiveness, prune what does not move the routing decision. That is a concrete research direction, not an analogy.
3. Copy the DiT-split trick as a general pattern for auxiliary-objective models. Whenever a model is trained with a heavy auxiliary decoder (pixel reconstruction, span reconstruction, any generative side-objective) that exists only to shape representations, check whether the decoder can be removed at inference and replaced with cross-attention into the hidden states. FastOne's insight, that the representation was in the hidden state and decoding was overhead paid for supervision, is a reusable audit question for your own architectures.
4. Take the two-track split (capability versus efficiency) as a market read. DreamZero landed in February 2026 and by mid-2026 there is a 1B Efficient-WAM, a dual-system variant, a KV cache manager, and a YC company selling latency-budget compilation for edge robotics. Physical AI is entering the phase where compression, distillation, and kernel work determine who can deploy, which is the phase where your existing skill set is the scarce input. Instinct Edge's product shape (model plus device plus latency budget, returns a runtime) is worth studying as the template for how this capability gets packaged.
5. Fix your evaluation before your architecture. Two independent lessons converge here. Lum's 60% pose-tracking failure share says profile the pipeline before optimizing the model, and his point that you cannot distinguish 90% from 87% without a hundred trials says most reported deltas in this literature are vibe checks. Apply both to quantization and pruning sweeps: report confidence intervals with a stated trial count, and attribute regressions to the component that actually caused them rather than to the compression by default.
6. Note the emergent-recovery mechanism for your own robustness work. Recovery behaviour appeared because training covered the failure distribution (random perturbation forces), not because of architecture or reward shaping. The equivalent for a compressed model is deliberately including degraded, adversarial, and out-of-distribution inputs in the calibration set rather than a clean representative sample. If your calibration data never contains the failure, the quantized model never learns to survive it.
7. If you ever want to build in this space, Nico's playbook is the cheapest available de-risking: one paying customer, teleop first, off-the-shelf arms, replica of the customer environment in your office, hand-built success metric before any automation. His asymmetry (teleoppable implies trainable, but not conversely) is a genuinely useful feasibility filter.
