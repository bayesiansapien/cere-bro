# How AI Agents Let GTM Teams Scale — Justin Joyce, Cloudflare

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qw_tC68KKes

## TL;DR
Joyce is a principal sales operations and strategy manager at Cloudflare with seven years on the machine learning side at Grainger before moving back into revenue operations six months ago. His framing of the problem is the clearest part of the talk: traditional GTM fails to scale because of two distinct gaps that get lumped together. The context gap is that a rep switching between a prospect call, a customer call and an adoption call must reassemble situational information between each one. The expert gap is the delta between how your strongest seller handles a situation and how a ramping seller does. Different problems, different fixes. His answer is three pillars: scale analysis so the ops team answers questions in five minutes rather than two hours, scale insight by pushing a weekly narrative rather than waiting for someone to open a dashboard, and self-service so reps pull expert-level context themselves. The load-bearing primitive underneath all three is curated skill files that encode business semantics and map them onto the data model, which is what lets a non-SQL user query the warehouse directly. The push pipeline is a three-agent chain, draft against MCP-sourced data, then a reviewer agent checking veracity, then a tone agent balancing risk against opportunity, tuned over two to three months of inspecting every run. Claimed outcome is 2x efficiency.

## Key Takeaways
- The context gap and the expert gap are worth keeping separate because they have different solutions. Context is a retrieval and assembly problem solved by self-service tooling. Expertise is a knowledge-encoding problem solved by curated skills. Conflating them produces a system that hands a ramping rep more data and no more judgement.
- Skill files carry both business semantics and the mapping to tables and columns, and serve technical and non-technical users from the same artifact. The SQL-writing analyst and the rep who has never written a query consume the same encoded logic, which is what keeps the two populations from drifting onto different definitions of the same metric.
- The skill files were tuned by seeding them with the questions the business actually asks, learned through testing. Joyce names closed-date changes and opportunity amount changes specifically. He claims this covers 80% or more of demand, leaving complex strategic questions as the remainder. This is the same intent-sampled eval discipline Snowflake's Izmit described in the adjacent talk, arrived at independently.
- Application building moved out of the IT backlog. Because the semantic mapping already exists in the skill files, the ops team builds internal applications directly rather than queueing for engineering, which is a second-order return on the same artifact.
- Push beats pull for a population that will never open a dashboard. Joyce's observation is that KPI adoption is bimodal in every company, some people live in dashboards and some never look, and a weekly narrative summary covering pacing to goal, trends, standouts and watches serves the second group without abandoning the first, since drill-down remains available.
- Data is deliberately pre-shaped before the agent sees it. They transform along time, a business slice of manager and theatre, and metric, reshape between wide and long depending on the consumer, and pre-compute the filters and aggregations the business cares about. The logic of how you would filter and aggregate is engineered up front rather than being rediscovered by the model per query.
- The push pipeline is a three-role agent chain with separation of concerns: a drafting agent that fetches through MCP and writes, a reviewer agent that checks the data claims, and a tone agent using a multi-shot prompt to present risks and opportunities in balance. Every LLM call is traced with inputs and outputs visible.
- Two to three months of inspecting every single run before settling the architecture. That is the unglamorous number in the talk and it is the one that explains why it works.
- Cloudflare OS is an internal agentic workspace where each GTM user gets their own compute and persistent environment on Workers and Durable Objects, composed of three parts, the curated skill layer, MCP connections, and an AI gateway. Named use cases are forecast briefs, QBR decks, purchase-history decks, account planning, ad hoc queries and renewal prep.
- Skills are centrally reviewed through a single alias, curated by both the GTM and operations teams, with the explicit goal of preventing skill proliferation. Somebody is deliberately saying no, which is the part most skill-library efforts skip.
- The honest forward-looking section names the hard unsolved parts: writing back to Salesforce, quoting and approvals, meeting-note capture, and embedding generated artifacts into calendared meetings, all of which are blocked on security and system integration rather than model capability.
- "Cambrian stage" is his own diagnosis of where they are, an explosion of skills and enthusiasm that will need consolidation so that sources of truth across teams stay aligned. Naming the coming consolidation while still in the expansion is unusually self-aware for a conference talk.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The pre-shaping decision is the technically substantive one and the talk understates how much work it is doing. Joyce transforms data along fixed dimensions and pre-computes the aggregations before an agent touches it, which means the model is not being asked to write analytical SQL against a raw warehouse schema. It is being asked to read and narrate an already-correct table. Those are different tasks with different error profiles. Text-to-SQL against raw tables fails silently: the model joins incorrectly, misreads a column's meaning, or emits a query that runs cleanly and returns the wrong number, and nothing downstream can detect it. Narration over a pre-aggregated result fails visibly, because the numbers are right and only the prose can be wrong. Moving work from the first regime to the second is the whole trick, and it is the same move as constraining generation with a grammar or a schema: you are shrinking the output space to one where invalid outputs are unrepresentable rather than merely unlikely.

The benchmark evidence for this is now strong enough to quote as a design rule rather than an intuition. dbt Labs' April 2026 benchmark measured Claude Sonnet 4.6 going from 90.0% to 98.2% with semantic-layer grounding, and GPT-5.3-Codex from 84.1% to 100% in a modeled-project configuration. Databricks AI/BI Genie documents the fuller spread, 53% baseline, 80% with metadata enrichment, and 100% with full metric definitions, which is the cleanest available decomposition of where the accuracy actually comes from: metadata gets you most of the way, formal metric definitions close the gap. AtScale reports 92.5% on TPC-DS with a semantic layer. Snowflake's Cortex Analyst reports above 90% on a 150-question internal suite, roughly double single-shot GPT-4 without one. The consistent picture across independent vendors is that frontier models land at 70 to 85% on clean raw data and 86 to 100% with proper semantics, and the reason is not that the model got smarter, it is that deterministic query generation from a modeled layer removes the class of subtly-wrong results entirely. Joyce's skill files are a hand-rolled semantic layer, and the 80%-of-questions coverage claim he makes is consistent with these numbers.

The reviewer agent is the piece most worth interrogating, because verification-by-second-model is widely deployed and frequently overrated. A separate agent checking the veracity of the first agent's data claims works well when the check is more constrained than the generation, and works poorly when it is a general "is this right?" prompt to the same model that just produced the text. The tell is whether the reviewer has access to something the drafter did not, or is performing a mechanically simpler task. Here it plausibly does: the numbers came from a pre-aggregated table via MCP, so the reviewer can check assertions in the prose against a small ground-truth table, which is a verification task, not a re-derivation. That is the configuration where it earns its cost. The cheaper and more reliable version of the same guarantee, worth trying before adding a model call, is to have the drafter emit numeric claims as structured references into the source table and validate them programmatically. Zero tokens, zero variance, and it catches exactly the failure the reviewer is there for. Reserve the model-based reviewer for claims that are not mechanically checkable, which in a weekly business summary means the trend interpretations rather than the figures.

The tone agent is a separation of concerns that is correct for a reason worth naming. Asking one model to fetch, reason, and hit a specific register simultaneously degrades all three, because tone constraints in a system prompt compete for attention with analytical instructions and the analytical instructions usually lose. Splitting the register into its own pass with a multi-shot prompt is also the pass where few-shot examples work best, since style is precisely the thing that is easier to demonstrate than to specify. There is a routing implication Joyce does not draw: the tone pass is a rewriting task over short text with the content already fixed, which is comfortably within reach of a much cheaper model than the analytical draft requires. In a three-stage chain, stages one and two need capability and stage three needs style-following, and paying frontier prices for the third is the easiest saving in the pipeline.

One correction. Joyce describes Durable Objects as "basically a storage sort of like S3," and that is wrong in a way that matters for anyone copying the architecture. A Durable Object is stateful compute with a unique global identity, single-threaded, pinned to one location, with its own transactional strongly-consistent storage up to 10 GB, now recommended on a SQLite backend. The guarantee it provides is that one key has exactly one live instance globally, so concurrent requests touching that state serialise automatically. That is the opposite of S3's model, which is stateless, eventually consistent in places, and has no serialisation guarantee at all. Cloudflare's actual S3 analogue is R2. The distinction is the entire reason Durable Objects are the right substrate for per-user agent sessions: each user's workspace is a single addressable instance with consistent state across requests, which is what makes a persistent environment possible without a separate session store or distributed locking. Describing it as storage undersells his own architecture. Worth noting alongside this that Durable Object Facets now let dynamically generated Workers instantiate their own isolated SQLite databases, which is the natural next step for exactly this pattern, giving each generated artifact or agent session its own database rather than sharing one.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The semantic-layer-beats-raw-SQL finding is the best-supported claim adjacent to this talk and it has consolidated fast during 2026. Beyond the vendor benchmarks above, there is peer-reviewable work: a paired benchmark on arXiv measuring accuracy and hallucination across three frontier models with and without semantic layers reaches the same conclusion, and Atlan's enterprise write-up frames the residual risk as metric drift, meaning the same business term resolving to different definitions across teams over time. That is precisely the failure Joyce is trying to prevent with central skill review through a single alias, and he is right that it is the thing that kills these systems slowly. It is also the reason his warning about the Cambrian phase needing consolidation is not just tidiness: an uncurated skill library is a metric-drift generator, because every team that writes its own skill file encodes its own definition of pipeline, of qualified, of churn.

Worth being clear-eyed about the vendor incentive in those numbers. Every benchmark cited above was published by a company selling a semantic layer, and the reported jumps to 100% are on modeled projects, meaning the questions fall inside what the model layer covers. Promethium's write-up on enterprise text-to-SQL accuracy exists specifically to argue that vendor benchmarks overstate real-world performance, and the honest reading is that the direction is unambiguous and the magnitude is optimistic. The uncovered tail is where the accuracy goes, which maps exactly onto Joyce's 20% of complex strategic questions that his skill files do not handle. Nobody's benchmark measures that 20%.

The Cloudflare platform details are accurate apart from the Durable Objects characterisation. Workers are stateless functions where each request may land on a different instance with no shared memory, Durable Objects are the stateful counterpart, and the combination is the documented pattern for per-session agent workspaces. Cloudflare shipped Durable Object Facets for exactly the AI-generated-app case during 2026.

The 2x efficiency claim is unaudited, self-reported, six months into the effort, and has no stated baseline or measurement method. Treat it the way you would treat any internal productivity number presented from a stage. The two-to-three months of reviewing every run before locking the architecture is the more credible signal that this works, because it is a cost nobody invents.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Move work out of open-ended generation into constrained narration wherever the target is a number. Pre-aggregate along the dimensions consumers actually slice by, then ask the model to read and explain rather than to derive. Silent wrong answers become visible wrong prose, which is a failure mode you can actually catch.
2. If any pipeline you own has a model writing SQL or analytical code against a raw schema, put a semantic layer in front of it and measure the delta. The published range is 70 to 85% raw versus 86 to 100% modeled, and the Databricks decomposition suggests metadata enrichment alone captures most of it, so the cheap intervention is worth trying before the expensive one.
3. Replace model-based verification with programmatic verification wherever the claim is mechanically checkable. Have the drafting stage emit numeric claims as structured references into the source table and validate them in code. Reserve a reviewer model for interpretive claims only. This removes a model call, removes variance, and strengthens the guarantee.
4. Split register from reasoning into separate passes, and route the register pass to a cheap model. Tone is a short rewriting task over fixed content, it is the pass where few-shot examples work best, and it does not need frontier capability. In a three-stage chain this is the single clearest cost saving.
5. Seed skill files, semantic layers and eval sets from the questions the business actually asks, not from what the system can currently answer. Joyce found closed-date changes and opportunity amount changes by testing against real demand, and got 80% coverage from a small number of encoded patterns because demand is heavily skewed.
6. Curate the skill library with an explicit gatekeeper and an explicit no. Uncurated skill libraries generate metric drift, where the same business term resolves differently per team, and that failure is slow, silent and extremely expensive to unwind. Central review through one alias is a low-tech control that works.
7. Use stateful compute primitives for per-user agent sessions rather than bolting a session store onto stateless functions. The property you want is exactly one live instance per key with strongly consistent local state, which is what Durable Objects provide and what a stateless-worker-plus-object-storage design does not.
8. Separate context problems from expertise problems before designing a fix. More retrieval does not close an expertise gap, and better encoded judgement does not close a context gap. Systems that conflate them deliver a ramping practitioner a larger pile of correct data and no improvement in what they do with it.
9. Budget for the unglamorous tuning period. Two to three months of reading every run is what stands between a three-agent pipeline that works and one that produces plausible weekly summaries nobody checks. There is no shortcut, and full input-output tracing per LLM call is the minimum instrumentation for doing it at all.
