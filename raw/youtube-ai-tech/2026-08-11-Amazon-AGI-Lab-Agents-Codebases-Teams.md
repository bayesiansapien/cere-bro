# Agents, Codebases, and Teams

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-11
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aeTb5BdmTTc

## TL;DR
Aditya Khandelwal (Amazon AGI Lab) makes the argument that almost all published advice on setting up a codebase for agents is written for a single repo and a single developer, and breaks the moment you apply it to a real team in production. His reframing is the load-bearing claim: making engineers work well with agents is not an individual contributor's problem, it is leadership's, because the highest-impact interventions require changing how the codebase is organised and no IC can do that unilaterally. His diagnostic list is the most immediately useful part, especially the observation that "the model is being really dumb today" almost always means the harness changed rather than the model, and that if your codebase cannot tolerate small harness changes then the codebase is the problem. The playbook from leading a team of ten is concrete: get progressive disclosure right and enforce it with hard limits, invest in one high-value skill that proves trust (theirs took code-done to PR-ready-for-review and often ran over an hour), wire issues, CI, agentic review, and a nightly code gardener into the repo to close the loop, and spend real effort winning over the skeptics by letting them edit the shared setup. The failure inventory is honest, including blowing up to 400 to 500 issues within a couple of weeks. And the measurement he gives in Q&A is the single most portable thing in the talk: baseline context load is around 20 to 25K tokens, and if your first prompt is pulling 40 to 50K, your progressive disclosure is not working.

## Key Takeaways
- The framing complaint that motivates the talk: there is abundant content on which skills to add and which setup is better, and it all breaks when applied to an actual team in an actual production setup. It works for individual repos and stops working at team scale.
- His recap of the industry arc, told without naming companies. Coding agents took off, a few people got outsized leverage and everyone asked whether this was AGI. Companies concluded that if one person can do this well, mandate it for everyone and token-max. Then the inevitable happened: AI slop shipped, a batch of sev-twos followed, and people retracted. Then model prices climbed, everyone discovered tokens have to be paid for, budgets got bolted on, and money is being lit on fire while having to come from somewhere.
- The two-axis model for where individuals sit: fear on one axis (is this coming for my job versus this is a handy tool) and confidence or utilisation on the other (using it a lot versus barely, because they do not know how). The trajectory he traces is high fear and low utilisation at the start, then early adopters raising confidence, then mandates pushing utilisation up while confidence stays flat, then the slop backlash producing low fear for the wrong reason, since "it just ships slop, I'm still needed." The target quadrant is low fear and high utilisation, and the job is moving people there from wherever they actually are.
- The failure signals, and this is the checklist worth stealing. Babysitting agents means the setup is wrong. "Insert latest model is being really dumb today" means the harness changed, not the model, and if the codebase cannot absorb small harness changes then the codebase is not set up well. Silently burning context and money, blowing through 500K then 750K then a million and hitting auto-compact on a task that is not complicated. Long sessions needing constant intervention. A constant slop factory. And asking how other companies ship so fast, or how model companies release on a month-and-a-half cadence, which means they have something you do not.
- The reframe: this is not an IC's job, it is leadership's and the company's, and making engineers work well with their agents is the most impactful thing an organisation can do.
- The distributional argument against figure-it-out-for-yourself, and it is the sharpest observation in the talk. Some people get outsized productivity and some do not. The people shipping 10 PRs a day look like gods next to those shipping one or two, and the one-or-two people get left with the review burden. So they cannot ship, they are reading bad code all day, they curse the agents, and they never make it to the 10-PR quadrant. The inequity is self-reinforcing.
- Harness engineering principle one, smart prompt injection: treat the entire codebase as the mechanism, so the agent gets the right context at the right time without you supplying it. His example is documentation living in the comments, so that if the agent greps into a file it reads the comment, follows it to the right file, and finds everything about that code. The framing to hold is that the agent should start with a map of how to find what it needs when it needs it.
- Harness engineering principle two, close the loop: slop is inevitable, some will seep in, so you need a pipeline that detects it, removes it, and lets the system self-heal.
- Harness engineering principle three, iterate continuously: you cannot do this for a month and be done, because things change underneath constantly, so it has to be a standing organisational activity.
- Harness engineering principle four, treat it as a human problem: fear is real, human emotions are real, and mandating your way through will not work.
- Playbook step one, do the basics and get progressive disclosure right. Find your best ICs, work out how they are making the codebase work for them, and propagate those practices. The hard part is human: it requires engineers to accept that their own setup is imperfect, and engineers do not like hearing that.
- Playbook step two, invest in one high-value skill. Theirs was called ship-it and it covered everything from code-done to PR-ready-for-review: opening the PR, the description, handling comments, merge comments, and CI failures, running through those loops. It often ran for over an hour, which initially scared people, and then became the thing that convinced them the AI could work for them without babysitting.
- Playbook step three, close the loop concretely. Issues and boards wired into the repo, CI/CD, agentic reviews, and a code gardener that runs nightly over the codebase checking whether things are organised correctly, where correctly is defined per codebase.
- Playbook step four, get people invested and win over the skeptics. Dismissing a skeptic as someone who is scared is easy and useless. The test of genuine buy-in is whether they will edit and play with the shared setup themselves.
- The budget he names explicitly: some percentage of IC time goes to iterating on the shared setup, producing no meaningful PRs up front, and it is worth it.
- Problem they hit: too many issues, blowing up to 400 or 500 within a couple of weeks, which is absurd for one repo, because many different agents were all creating issues without being wired correctly.
- Problem they hit: lack of agreement. The moment something did not work as expected, people reverted to babysitting. The correct response is taking that feedback into the skill rather than letting them opt out.
- Problem they hit, reframed as an expectation-setting issue: agents taking too long is good, because it means you can go do other things with confidence they are doing the right thing. His justification is the reasoning paradigm, where the longer the agent thinks the better the output, and he applies the same mindset to the codebase and the skills.
- Problems he accepts rather than solves: merge hell has to be dealt with, and experiment code will be slop. Their answer on the second is to treat prototypes as their own category and explicitly opt them out of the rigorous standards applied everywhere else.
- People move on the fear-confidence spectrum day to day, so the intervention is conversation. Ask why they are hitting this, check whether the model or harness changed, and go revisit whatever broke.
- The two mindset shifts he closes on. Replace "the model is so dumb" with "how can I make it smarter." And cross out "my setup," because it is the shared setup that gets invested in.
- The skiing analogy for commitment: pizza-braking guarantees a crash, and you have to commit to the speed to feel how turning actually works. Be okay with failing and falling, and the point is recovery.
- Q&A, the concrete progressive disclosure rules. A hard limit of 100 lines in SKILL.md, because the skill is really a folder. Runbooks reflected in code comments so an agent that greps into a file knows where to look for context. CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md as a thin index pointing to the right files rather than one big document, because that is what gets loaded on the first prompt.
- Q&A, the measurement. Roughly 20 to 25K tokens get taken anyway, so watch how much more gets added on the first prompt. If you are reaching 40 or 50K, that is not progressive disclosure and something is wrong.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The most transferable technical idea here is the attribution discipline hiding inside "the model is being dumb today," and it generalises far past coding agents. What he is describing is a system with several independently versioned components, where a change in any one of them surfaces as a quality regression that users attribute to the most visible component. The harness updated, the tool definitions shifted, the context assembly changed, and the felt experience is that the model got worse. The correct response is not reassurance, it is version pinning and variable isolation, and his stronger claim is the interesting one: if your codebase cannot tolerate small harness changes without visible degradation, the fragility is in your setup rather than in the harness. That is a robustness criterion, and it is testable. Deliberately perturb the harness and see whether output quality moves.

This maps directly onto compression work and it is worth carrying over verbatim. A quantised checkpoint regresses and the reflex is to blame the quantisation recipe. The actual causes are frequently elsewhere: a kernel version change, a tokeniser update, a different calibration split, a serving config that silently changed batch composition, an attention backend swap. Every one of those presents as "the compressed model got worse." His discipline says pin every layer, perturb one at a time, and treat a pipeline whose measured accuracy moves with an unrelated infrastructure change as broken independently of whatever accuracy it reports. The research equivalent of his robustness test is checking whether your reported accuracy delta survives a kernel or backend swap, and if it does not, you do not have a measurement.

The progressive disclosure numbers deserve to be treated as a budget discipline rather than trivia, and they line up exactly with the [Codex harness talk](2026-08-10-Codex-Behind-The-Harness-Context-Auto-Review.md) from the day before. OpenAI caps the skills manifest at 2% of the context window and degrades description fidelity to stay inside it. Khandelwal caps SKILL.md at 100 lines and treats 40 to 50K on the first prompt as a failure against a 20 to 25K baseline. Same intervention arrived at independently: express variable-size context components as a hard budget with a defined degradation path, and measure the actual first-turn load rather than assuming it. The mechanism underneath is also the same and it is the one to internalise, because it is a retrieval problem dressed as a context problem. A thin index plus on-demand file reads is sparse activation with the model acting as its own router, and the failure mode is identical to a mis-routed MoE expert: the file that was never opened is context the model cannot use, and nothing errors.

His claim that longer agent runtime is good because the reasoning paradigm rewards longer thinking is the weakest link in the talk, and it is worth separating into two claims because one is right and one is not. Test-time compute scaling has real returns, but the curve flattens and in several published settings turns negative, with models talking themselves out of correct answers, so "longer means better" is not a law. What is actually true about his ship-it skill is different and more useful: an hour of runtime is acceptable because the work is asynchronous, and asynchronous work has a fundamentally different latency-value curve from interactive work. That is a routing insight rather than a scaling one. The right allocation policy is not maximum depth everywhere, it is depth matched to interaction mode and to query difficulty, which means the interesting design question for a team like his is not how long to let agents run but how to classify work into interactive and async lanes with different depth budgets. He gets the right answer for the wrong reason.

The distributional argument is the part I would flag to any research lead, because the identical dynamic shows up in optimisation work and nobody frames it correctly. Whoever builds the sweep tooling gets an order of magnitude more experiments run, and everyone else inherits the analysis and review burden for results they did not generate. That person then cannot run their own experiments, spends their time reading someone else's output, forms a negative view of automated sweeps, and never builds their own tooling. The inequity is self-reinforcing in exactly the way he describes, and the fix is the same: treat the tooling as shared infrastructure that leadership funds and that everyone is expected to edit, not as an individual's clever setup.

Finally, his close-the-loop layer is the concrete implementation of what the [Velocity Sickness talk](2026-08-09-Velocity-Sickness-Team-10x-Faster.md) argued for but did not supply. That talk made the case for moving verification earlier and for a durable decision layer, and correctly identified that human review of confident AI output is unreliable. Khandelwal's answer is a deterministic and agentic verification layer that runs without a human in the path: CI, agentic reviews, and a nightly code gardener checking structural organisation. The nightly gardener is the piece I have not seen elsewhere and it is a good idea precisely because structural drift is invisible in any single diff and only detectable by looking at the whole repo periodically. The research analogue is a scheduled job that audits your experiment directory for configs that no longer match their recorded results, checkpoints with no provenance, and results tables built from mixed pipeline versions.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The industry arc he describes without naming companies is documented, and the specific detail is remarkable given who he works for. In March 2026 Amazon's retail website suffered multiple high-severity outages in a single week, with internal documents pointing to a trend of incidents tied to Gen-AI assisted changes, and Amazon's Kiro mandate has been linked to production outages including a 13-hour AWS Cost Explorer outage in mainland China. So when an Amazon AGI Lab engineer says AI slop shipped and a batch of sev-twos followed and declines to name companies, the most prominent public example is his own employer. That does not undermine the talk, it strengthens it: the playbook is a response to observed failure at scale rather than a theory.

The quality paradox he assumes his audience already knows is now well quantified and the numbers are worth carrying. New Relic's reporting captures the inversion: 94% of leaders rate AI-generated code as higher quality than human-authored code at the time of review, and once it ships 78% report more incidents, 86% report an increase in senior staff time spent fixing code, and 74% report at least 25% of AI code needing significant rework. That is the mechanism behind his review-burden argument stated as measurement. The code passes review and fails in production, which means review is not catching what matters, which is precisely why his answer is an automated closing loop rather than more human review. Apiiro's framing of 4x velocity against 10x vulnerabilities makes the same point from the security side, and the review-capacity claim is the blunt version: AI generates code faster than humans can review it, so complexity scales faster than oversight.

On progressive disclosure his specific rules match the emerging consensus and in one place he is more disciplined than the published guidance. The mechanism is that at startup only each skill's name and description are loaded, tens of tokens per skill, with the full SKILL.md body loading when the agent judges it relevant and large skills referencing external files read on demand. Anthropic's own context engineering guidance for the Claude 5 generation says to keep CLAUDE.md lightweight, describe briefly what the repo is for, and spend most of the token budget on gotchas inside the codebase, which is a slightly different emphasis from his thin-index framing and arguably complementary: index for navigation, spend the remaining budget on the non-obvious. The point most guidance stresses and he does not is that the description line is the highest-leverage text in a skill and should be written as a routing rule rather than a summary, since it is the only part the model sees at startup. His 100-line SKILL.md cap does not appear as a published norm anywhere I can find, and it is a good idea worth adopting precisely because it forces the folder structure rather than allowing gradual bloat.

Two things in the talk are unverifiable and should be held as one team's experience rather than as findings. The ship-it skill and the nightly code gardener are internal to his team and there is no published evaluation, so the claim that a single high-value skill is the right mechanism for winning trust is a plausible and appealing hypothesis rather than a demonstrated one. Similarly the 400-to-500-issue explosion is a useful war story about unwired agents duplicating work, and the underlying lesson, that multiple agents creating issues without coordination generates garbage at a rate no human can triage, generalises well beyond his specifics.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Measure your first-turn context load this week and compare it against his threshold. Baseline around 20 to 25K, and anything reaching 40 to 50K means the index is doing the work that on-demand file reads should be doing. This is a five-minute check that most teams have never run, and it is the cheapest single diagnostic in the talk.
2. Adopt the 100-line cap on any skill file you write, which forces the folder structure rather than allowing a single document to grow until it silently degrades every session. Pair it with writing the description as a routing rule rather than a summary, since that line is the only part loaded at startup.
3. Steal the attribution discipline for your compression pipeline and make it explicit. Pin the kernel, backend, tokeniser, and calibration split alongside the quantisation recipe, and run the perturbation test: swap one infrastructure component and check whether your reported accuracy delta survives. If it moves, the number you have been reporting is not measuring what you think it is measuring.
4. Run his robustness criterion against your own agent setup rather than waiting for a regression. Deliberately change something in the harness and see whether quality moves. A setup whose output depends on harness details is fragile in a way that will surface as "the model got worse" at the least convenient moment, and you would rather discover it on purpose.
5. Watch for the distributional trap in your own team, because it is the failure with the longest tail. If one person's tooling gives them ten times the experimental throughput while everyone else absorbs the analysis burden for results they did not produce, that is exactly his 10-PR versus 1-PR dynamic and it will end with the majority concluding automation does not work. Fund the tooling as shared infrastructure and require that others can edit it.
6. Build the nightly gardener for your experiment directory, since this is the idea in the talk with the least prior art and the clearest research analogue. A scheduled job that flags configs whose recorded results no longer match, checkpoints without provenance, and results tables assembled from mixed pipeline versions. Structural drift is invisible in any single change and only detectable by periodically auditing the whole thing.
7. Separate interactive from asynchronous work explicitly and give them different depth budgets, which is the correct version of his longer-is-better claim. An hour of agent runtime is fine for async work and unacceptable for interactive work, and the test-time compute literature does not support unbounded depth in either case. This is a routing policy, and it is the same allocation problem you already work on.
8. Copy the prototype opt-out, because it removes a recurring source of friction cheaply. Declare experiment and prototype code its own category, exempt from the standards applied to shipped code, and be explicit about where the boundary is. Applying production rigour to throwaway exploration is how teams end up either slowing down exploration or quietly abandoning the rigour everywhere.
