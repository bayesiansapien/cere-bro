# Is AI Just a Scam? (Ed Zitron)

**Channel:** The Diary Of A CEO
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CwLxp-C4nWY

## TL;DR
A second clip from the same Ed Zitron episode, and where [the August 27 cut](./2026-08-27-AI-Is-A-Massive-Scam-Ed-Zitron-DOAC.md) argued the financials, this one argues the mechanism of persuasion. Zitron's claim: generative AI was sold as magic that would replace all jobs and cure cancer, while actually being "boring cloud software that is extremely expensive and unprofitable, and also unreliable at its core." The labs overstate capability, trajectory, and financials simultaneously, and they do it by exploiting structural weaknesses in journalism, in the economy, and among sell-side analysts and governments. His phrase for the result is the largest non-consensual push of technology in history, and a "directionless egregore of capitalism" that works by frightening people into adoption. The media-dynamics critique is his strongest material. "Boring cloud software" is where the argument breaks.

## Key Takeaways
- **The adoption mechanism is coercion by narrative, not demonstrated value.** Three years of every outlet saying this will take your job, you must use this, you will fall behind. Zitron's point is that fear of obsolescence, not proven utility, is doing the work of a sales function. As a description of how enterprise AI mandates actually arrived in 2024 and 2025, this is hard to argue with.
- **"Exploiting the weaknesses in journalism."** The specific structural failure: tech journalism largely lacks the financial literacy to audit unit economics and the incentive to alienate its primary sources. Coverage therefore reproduces capability claims and ignores cost structure. This is a real and underdiscussed problem.
- **Sell-side analysts and governments as complicit rather than deceived.** Both have incentives aligned with the narrative continuing. Governments in particular have staked industrial policy on it.
- **"Non-consensual push."** The framing that most people did not choose to have generative AI inserted into their search results, their operating system, their documents, and their workplace tooling. As a description of distribution strategy, accurate. As a moral claim, contestable, since the same is true of every default in consumer software.
- **"Unreliable at its core."** The most defensible technical criticism in the clip. Nondeterminism and confabulation are properties of the architecture, not bugs awaiting a patch, and they are why so many deployments stall between demo and production.
- **The three overstated things.** What it can do, what it will do, and the underlying financials. Separating these is analytically useful, because they have different evidence bases and the third is the only one where Zitron has proprietary data.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **"Boring cloud software" is the load-bearing error.** Boring cloud software has gross margins of 70 to 80% and near-zero marginal cost per request. Generative inference has a large, irreducible marginal cost per request: every token costs GPU-seconds, memory bandwidth, and power. That is precisely why the losses exist. Zitron is using the phrase to diminish the technology, but it actually undercuts his own financial argument, because if it were boring cloud software the economics would already work. The correct characterisation is the opposite of boring: it is a category of software with the cost structure of a utility and the pricing expectations of SaaS, and that mismatch is the whole problem. Anyone in inference optimization should find this the most telling slip in the clip.
- **Unreliability and cost are the same problem.** The standard mitigations for confabulation, retrieval grounding, self-consistency sampling, verifier passes, multi-agent review, all work by spending more compute per answer. So the reliability fix moves against the margin fix. That tension is the actual engineering frontier, and it is why routing matters: send only the queries that need expensive verification down the expensive path.
- **What would falsify Zitron on the technical side.** A sustained order-of-magnitude reduction in cost per reliably-correct task, achieved through some combination of sparsity, quantization, distillation into small specialist models, and better routing, while capability holds. That is a measurable thing, and it is roughly the job description of Amit's field.
- **The distribution-versus-value point has an engineering reading too.** If adoption is driven by mandate rather than demonstrated utility, then measured usage overstates value, which means the revenue base is softer than run-rate figures imply. Retention and depth-of-use metrics matter more than seat counts here, which is exactly the "collapsing wow factor" problem Snowflake's Sait Izmit described from inside a deployment.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Zitron's financial base is real: his reporting on OpenAI's audited 2025 accounts shows $13.07 billion revenue against $34 billion in costs, a roughly $20.9 billion operating loss and a $38.53 billion net loss attributable, versus $5.09 billion lost in 2024 on $3.7 billion revenue. The industry-level picture supports the concern: roughly $725 billion of planned 2026 hyperscaler capex against something on the order of $25 billion in direct AI services revenue, with the big five now funding capex partly through debt, $108 billion raised in 2025, because aggregate capex exceeds cash flow after buybacks and dividends. His separate forecast is that OpenAI runs out of cash by 2027, triggering a chain reaction through tech valuations.

Where the "no real value" implication fails on the same data he cites: Microsoft's AI business is at a $37 billion annual run rate growing 123% year over year, AWS at roughly $150 billion annualised growing 28%, and Google Cloud's backlog exceeds $460 billion. Backlog is contracted future revenue from customers who signed voluntarily. That is inconsistent with a pure narrative-driven bubble and consistent with a real product being oversupplied. The most probable state of the world is not "con" and not "imminent AGI," it is a genuine technology in the middle of a classic infrastructure overbuild, where the technology survives and a large fraction of the invested capital does not. Railways and fibre both went that way.

Two further caveats on the clip specifically. First, "cure cancer" and "replace all jobs" are the maximalist claims of the loudest promoters, not the median claim of the labs' technical communications, so knocking them down is easier than the real argument. Second, Zitron has built a large and successful media business on this thesis. That does not make him wrong, and his primary-source reporting is a genuine contribution, but the incentive to escalate is symmetric with the incentive the labs have to inflate. Read him for the numbers and discount the adjectives.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Kill the "boring cloud software" assumption inside your own org.** The single most useful thing to communicate upward from an optimization team is that generative inference does not have SaaS unit economics, and that the gap closes through engineering rather than scale. Zitron's error is the same error most finance functions make, and correcting it is how efficiency work gets funded.
- **Measure depth, not adoption.** If Zitron is right that adoption is fear-driven, then seat counts and query volumes overstate value. For any internal AI deployment, track repeat usage per user, task completion rate, and whether users return after a bad answer. That is the metric that distinguishes a real product from a mandate, and it is the same instrumentation that identifies which query classes justify expensive routing.
- **Instrument cost per reliably-correct answer, not cost per token.** This is the metric that resolves the reliability-versus-margin tension in one number, and it is the one that would empirically settle the argument in this clip. Define correctness per task class, then compute total spend including retries, verifier passes, and retrieval, divided by verified-correct outputs. Almost nobody tracks this, and it is the number that tells you whether a cheaper model is actually cheaper.
- **Position for the overbuild scenario rather than either extreme.** Assume the technology persists and the capital does not. Practically that means investing personal skill in efficiency, small-model deployment, and routing rather than in tooling that only makes sense with unlimited compute, and it means treating any AI-concentrated portfolio exposure as cyclical rather than structural.
- **Read the primary reporting rather than the clip.** Zitron's own write-up of the OpenAI financials is the useful artifact. The podcast is the argument; the article is the evidence.
