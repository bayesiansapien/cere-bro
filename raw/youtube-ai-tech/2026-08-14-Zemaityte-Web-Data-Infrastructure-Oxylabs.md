# How Web Data Infrastructure Powers the Next Generation of AI — Patricija Žemaitytė, Oxylabs

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-14
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1UmZHb_E_SM

## TL;DR
A vendor talk from Oxylabs that is worth reading anyway, because the operational numbers are specific and the war stories expose real constraints in the retrieval layer that sits under every grounded model. Three stories, each an adaptation forced by a customer with an unreasonable deadline. First, a request for a video API for AI training at 5 petabytes per month in two weeks, which started as a downloader and expanded through transcripts, then subtitles when it turned out the client meant subtitles and not transcripts, then multilingual video search, then metadata and channel information, becoming a product suite in three months. The client has since pulled 30 petabytes and, she notes without elaboration, has not paid. Second, latency: a 2024 request for sub-second SERP delivery against a 4-second baseline, shelved when the client never tested it, revived in 2025 by a client wanting sub-second with zero data retention in two weeks. The insight was that the old scraper was slow because it was built for completeness, capturing ads, widgets, rich results, AI overviews and every layout variant, while an AI-facing search API only needs organic results, top stories and news. Rebuilt from scratch, it hit 650ms P90 in under two weeks, got hard-blocked live on the demo call, was rebuilt again around browsers which are fundamentally hostile to latency budgets, and now runs at 550ms average. Volume went from 400 million to nearly 6 billion daily requests. Third, scale: an anti-blocker service pushed from 10,000 to 60,000 requests per second in under two months, where a request means a full end-to-end job including routing, rendering, proxy handling, browser execution, parsing, retries, normalisation and delivery. The real bottleneck was not capacity but load testing, since synthetic traffic is easy and organic-shaped traffic is not, and telemetry itself becomes part of the load at that volume. They stalled at 20,000 RPS in testing and eventually accepted that production traffic is the only real test. Project 60 is now project 150. Her thesis is that the next generation of AI is gated by infrastructure rather than models, which is self-serving but not wrong.

## Key Takeaways
- The framing is that training still matters but is no longer sufficient, so the model layer is increasingly expected to sit inside a live retrieval layer. She cites Google's own grounding documentation positioning Search as the connection between models and current public knowledge, which is a fair external anchor for a claim she has commercial reason to make.
- Video pipeline requirements, stated as scale rather than feature: 5 petabytes per month, two-week deadline, and a pipeline covering collection, transfer, storage and delivery with reliability compatible with AI training workloads. Her point that this stops being a product feature and becomes infrastructure at that volume is the correct read.
- The transcript-versus-subtitle failure is the most instructive small detail. They built transcript support, every client request failed, and the diagnosis was not a bug: the client needed subtitles, meaning the timed caption track shipped with the video, not a generated transcription. Two different artefacts with different provenance and different alignment guarantees, and the requirement was never specified precisely enough to distinguish them.
- Multimodal training data demand is concrete and unglamorous. Beyond video files, clients want transcripts, subtitles, metadata, channel information, and multilingual search to find videos in specific languages. The structural context around the content turned out to be as valuable as the content.
- The economics footnote she drops and does not dwell on: 30 petabytes delivered, payment outstanding. For a talk about infrastructure enabling AI, the unpriced observation is that the data layer has weak pricing power against large AI buyers.
- Why the fast search API is not the old scraper optimised: the general scraper maximises coverage, capturing ads, widgets, rich results, AI-generated results and layout variants, and that completeness is the cost. An AI-facing API narrows to organic results, top stories and news and discards the heavy layout. Scope reduction, not micro-optimisation, is what made sub-second reachable.
- Latency trajectory: 4 seconds baseline in 2024, 650ms P90 on the first rebuild, 550ms average today. Her honest claim about how the second-generation gains were made is that there was no single trick, just a hunt through layouts, parsers, sessions and proxies for a second here and a second there.
- Getting blocked live on the demo call is the best moment in the talk, and the lesson she draws is the right one: there is a difference between a system that works in development, one that works in test, and one that survives reality. The rebuild had to lean on browsers, which she describes accurately as amazing, slow, expensive, complex, and deeply incompatible with low latency.
- Volume: 400 million to almost 6 billion daily requests, which she correctly frames as a change in operating model rather than growth, because it changes cost structure, observability and failure domains.
- Scale detail worth holding onto: 60,000 requests per second where each request is an end-to-end scraping job spanning routing, rendering, proxy handling, browser execution, parsing, retries, normalisation and delivery. She explicitly warns against reading it as HTTP requests per second, which would understate the work by orders of magnitude.
- Adding 2,000 servers does not solve it. What is needed is architecture, reliable central components, observability that still tells the truth at volume, and testing that resembles reality enough to be informative.
- The bottleneck appeared in load testing rather than in an outage, and they hit a wall at 20,000 RPS. Her framing of the real problem is precise: the system was working, and the question was whether they had any way to know it could go further. Uncertainty was the bottleneck.
- Observability at scale becomes self-referential. Collecting logs is hard, processing them is harder, and at that load the telemetry is itself a meaningful fraction of the load and the complexity.
- They scaled gradually and ultimately accepted that real testing happens with production traffic. It worked, which is a lucky outcome presented as a methodology.
- Project 60 became project 150 while the scaling work was still in flight, which is her evidence for scale never being a finish line.
- Her definition of the business, and the actual pitch: not proxies, but the maintenance burden. Targets change, layouts change, detection changes, market changes, client needs change, so this is an adapt-forever business rather than a build-once one.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The single most useful transferable idea here is her diagnosis of why the fast search API could not be the old scraper made faster. The general scraper was slow because it was correct in a dimension nobody in the AI use case needed, namely completeness over every widget, ad unit, rich result and layout variant. Going from 4 seconds to sub-second required changing what the system computes, not how fast it computes it. That is the same structural move as pruning: you do not get large wins by executing the full computation faster, you get them by establishing that most of the computation is not load-bearing for the target task, then removing it. The reason this is worth naming is that her second-generation gains, the hunt through layouts, parsers, sessions and proxies for a second at a time, are the kernel-optimisation analogue, and the ordering matters. Scope reduction bought roughly 3.5 seconds and micro-optimisation bought a hundred milliseconds. Teams instinctively do these in the wrong order because micro-optimisation feels like engineering and scope reduction feels like giving up.

The latency numbers deserve to be placed inside an inference budget, because she never does and the placement changes how you think about grounded systems. A 550ms average retrieval hop is not a small line item. It is comparable to or larger than the full generation latency of a small model on a short answer, which means in any grounded pipeline the retrieval layer, not the model, frequently owns the critical path. That has a direct consequence for routing work: if you route between a fast cheap model and a slow expensive one to manage latency, and both are behind a 550ms retrieval hop, you are optimising a component that contributes a minority of end-to-end time. The model-side latency spread that routers typically arbitrage can be smaller than the variance in the retrieval hop. Worse, she quotes 550ms average and 650ms P90 for different system generations, and averages are the wrong statistic here. Agentic systems issue many retrievals per task, so end-to-end latency tracks the tail of the retrieval distribution rather than its mean, and a sequence of ten retrievals is governed by something close to the P90 of a single one. Any router that treats retrieval as a fixed constant is mismodelling the thing it is optimising.

Her scale story contains an observation that generalises directly to inference serving and that most people learn the hard way: at high enough throughput, telemetry becomes part of the load. This is exactly the situation with per-token instrumentation in a serving stack, where the logging and metrics path competes for the same CPU, memory bandwidth and network that the serving path needs, and the instrumentation you added to diagnose a throughput problem becomes a contributor to it. The stronger version of her point is that the observability system and the serving system share a failure domain, so the moment you most need telemetry, saturation, is exactly the moment telemetry is most likely to be degraded or lying. Her phrasing, observability that still tells the truth, is the correct requirement and it is much harder than observability that exists.

The load-testing problem she describes is the deepest thing in the talk and she undersells it. Synthetic traffic is easy, organic-shaped traffic is hard, and they hit a wall at 20,000 RPS where the system was functioning but they had no evidence it would go further. This is a statement that their load generator did not reproduce the distribution of real work, and for scraping the distribution is over target sites, layouts, block states, proxy health and retry depth, all of which are heavy-tailed and correlated. It maps precisely onto a mistake that is endemic in inference benchmarking: benchmarking a serving stack with uniform-length prompts and uniform-length completions when production traffic has a heavy-tailed length distribution, bursty arrivals, and correlated cache hit patterns. A vLLM deployment benchmarked on synthetic uniform traffic will report throughput numbers that production does not reproduce, for the same reason her load test stalled, because continuous batching efficiency, KV-cache pressure and preemption behaviour are all functions of the length distribution rather than the mean length. Her conclusion, that the only real test is production traffic, is a counsel of despair that has a better alternative in both domains: replay recorded production traces rather than generating synthetic ones. She does not mention trace replay, which for a company with 6 billion daily requests of recorded traffic is a conspicuous gap.

The two-week deadline recurs three times and is presented as a feature of innovation culture. It is worth reading it the other way. Each two-week build was followed by a rebuild: the video downloader by transcripts then subtitles then search then metadata, the first fast search API by a total rebuild after it was blocked on the demo call. The pattern is not that pressure produces innovation, it is that pressure produces a probe that reveals the real requirement, and the real system gets built second. That is a defensible development model, but it is defensible as cheap requirement discovery, not as engineering velocity, and the subtitle incident is the cleanest illustration: two weeks of work whose entire value was learning which artefact the client actually meant.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The critical context missing from this talk is that the open web she depends on is closing, and the timing makes the omission awkward rather than merely incomplete. Cloudflare began blocking AI crawlers by default in July 2025 and launched Pay Per Crawl, which returns HTTP 402 and lets publishers set a price for crawler access, with over 2.5 million sites fully disallowing AI training by August 2025. The escalation is imminent: from 15 September 2026, one month after this talk, Cloudflare's defaults block mixed-use crawlers, those blending search, agent use and training, from any page carrying ads, and Pay Per Crawl is evolving into Pay Per Use. The web is now sorting into open, blocked and paid tiers. Her entire narrative treats blocking as an engineering adversary to be out-engineered, which is how a proxy provider has to see it, but a 402 payment wall is not an anti-bot system you defeat with better browser orchestration. It is a pricing regime, and it converts her margin structure from an engineering problem into a procurement problem.

The legal picture is more favourable to her than the access picture, and it is worth separating the two because they are often conflated. Scraping public data has held up: hiQ v. LinkedIn and Van Buren narrowed the CFAA, and in January 2024 the Northern District of California granted Bright Data summary judgment against Meta on the grounds that Meta's own terms did not bar logged-out scraping of public data. So collecting public pages is not the exposure. The live exposure is circumvention and contract, and Reddit's late-2025 suit against Perplexity and several data collection providers is the case to watch precisely because it invokes DMCA Section 1201, alleging circumvention of rate limits and anti-bot measures rather than unauthorised access. That theory targets the exact capability Žemaitytė describes as her company's core competence, dealing with antibot systems. It was still pending as of April 2026. Separately, EU Commission guidance in 2026 requires AI developers to honour machine-readable opt-outs, which pushes obligation onto the buyer of the data as well as the collector.

Her strongest claim, that the next generation of AI will be powered by better infrastructure rather than better models, is directionally supported by market structure even though she has every incentive to say it. Third-party data providers are positioned to benefit as AI training demand grows and first-party access tightens, which is the standard consequence of a resource becoming contested. The honest version of her thesis is narrower than the one she states: models are increasingly commoditised and access to fresh, licensed, structured web data is increasingly not, so the scarce input is shifting. That is a claim about relative scarcity, not about models ceasing to matter, and the evidence for it is the 402 status code rather than anything in her latency numbers.

One number to treat sceptically. The 550ms average for the fast search API is quoted without a percentile distribution, geography, or target mix, and her own earlier figure for the previous generation was a P90. Comparing a current average against a prior P90 flatters the improvement, and for anyone sizing a retrieval budget the average is the wrong statistic, since multi-hop agent workflows are governed by tail latency. Ask for P95 and P99 by geography before designing around this.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Put a retrieval latency line item into every grounded-system budget you build, and use a tail percentile rather than a mean. At 550ms average, the retrieval hop can exceed the generation latency of a small model, which means model-side routing for latency is optimising a minority of the critical path. For multi-hop agent workflows, size against P95, because ten sequential retrievals are governed by the tail of the single-hop distribution, not its mean.
2. Apply her scope-reduction-before-micro-optimisation ordering to your own latency work, in that order. She got roughly 3.5 seconds from deciding what not to compute and about a hundred milliseconds from tuning how it computes. In inference terms, establish what the task does not need (shorter contexts, smaller beams, fewer reranked candidates, dropped modalities) before you go hunting in kernels. The kernel work is more satisfying and usually smaller.
3. Replace synthetic load testing with production trace replay wherever you benchmark serving throughput. Her team hit an unexplained ceiling at 20,000 RPS because synthetic traffic did not reproduce the real distribution, and the identical failure is endemic in vLLM benchmarking, where uniform prompt and completion lengths produce throughput numbers that production does not reproduce because continuous batching, KV-cache pressure and preemption all depend on the length distribution rather than its mean. Capture real length distributions and arrival patterns and replay them.
4. Budget telemetry as part of the serving load and verify it degrades gracefully. Her observation that telemetry becomes part of the complexity at scale applies directly to per-token instrumentation in a serving stack, which competes for the same CPU and network as the serving path. Test explicitly what your metrics pipeline does under saturation, because that is the state in which you will need it and the state in which it is most likely to lie.
5. If any pipeline you own consumes scraped web data, get the access-cost trajectory in front of whoever owns the budget now rather than after 15 September 2026. Cloudflare's default block on mixed-use crawlers for ad-bearing pages lands that day and Pay Per Crawl is becoming Pay Per Use. Data acquisition is moving from an engineering line item to a metered one, and the change is scheduled rather than speculative.
6. Distinguish the two legal risks rather than treating scraping as one question. Collecting public pages is comparatively settled in the collector's favour after hiQ and Meta v. Bright Data. Circumventing rate limits and anti-bot measures is not, and Reddit v. Perplexity is testing exactly that under DMCA 1201 against both a model company and its data suppliers. If you procure web data, the diligence question is not "is scraping legal" but "does your collection involve circumvention, and does your contract indemnify me for it."
7. Specify data artefacts precisely in any data contract you write, because the subtitle incident cost two weeks of vendor work and would have cost you a sprint. Transcript and subtitle are not synonyms, they differ in provenance and timing alignment, and the same ambiguity exists for metadata, captions and channel context. Name the exact artefact and its alignment guarantees.
