# From AI-Assisted to AI-Native: Building a Frontier Development Team (Clare Liguori, AWS)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqlWNihgdjI

## TL;DR
Liguori, a senior principal engineer at AWS working on Kiro, reports that Amazon's internal pilots show a median 4.5x productivity improvement and sometimes above 10x, against the 10 to 20% she personally felt from every prior generation of AI coding tooling. The finding that matters is not the multiplier, it is the split: across 50 teams in Amazon Stores, half achieved under 3x while half hit 4.5x or better, and the difference was not the tools (90% used Kiro either way) but whether teams intentionally restructured how they worked rather than sprinkling agents on top of existing practice. She names five habits, is unusually candid about the confounds in each headline anecdote, and closes on the real costs: burnout, cognitive load, review being harder than writing for early-career engineers, and decision-making replacing code as the bottleneck.

## Key Takeaways
- **Three defining behaviours of a "frontier developer":** hands-off coding (they write 1 to 2% of shipped code), infrequent interaction (aiming for agents to run unattended for hours), and minimal idle time (multiple agents in parallel against a task backlog).
- **The three anecdotes, with her own caveats attached.** Bedrock Mantle rebuilt an inference data plane originally estimated at 30 people over 18 months with six people in 76 days, but those six included two distinguished engineers and were among the best in the company. A Prime Video 10-day sprint cut a delivery estimate from 90 weeks to 24, but the team had no on-call, few meetings, and a senior engineer had spent the prior three weeks decomposing everything into small well-scoped tasks with detailed requirements. The Amazon Stores pilot is the credible one: 50 teams, normal seniority distribution, existing brownfield codebases, observed over most of a year, measured on deployment velocity to production rather than commit count.
- **Habit 1, invest in agent context.** Everything normally transferred through Slack, onboarding, mentoring, code review, and standups has to be written down. The operational trigger they built: every time the agent makes a mistake or does something the wrong way, ask what is missing from the skills or steering files.
- **Habit 1's corollary, prune the context.** Sonnet 3.7-era steering files were full of "do not" rules for model quirks that no longer exist. The new habit is asking whether a given instruction is still needed or is now just bloating context. Very few teams do this.
- **Habit 2, slow down to speed up.** Almost every interviewed team reported productivity *falling* during intentional adoption. Real engineering work comes first: building agent context, improving error messages in existing tools so failures are legible to the model, building new tools and MCP servers, restructuring codebases for navigability, and in some cases changing language. She has seen teams move off Python and JavaScript because untyped languages give the model no compiler signal, toward TypeScript and increasingly Rust for its error messages.
- **Habit 3, feed agents rather than babysit them.** The core insight behind the step change. If you are in a back-and-forth conversation all day, waiting 30 to 60 seconds per turn, you cannot parallelise and you cannot leave. The alternative is giving the agent what it needs *plus a way to self-validate*, so it only returns when it compiles, passes tests, and meets a coverage bar. Then push that into the steering file so it happens by default.
- **Habit 4, make intent explicit.** Iterating with an agent on code when the *intent* was wrong is the expensive failure. For ambiguous complex features, write the specification first (or have the model draft it), because iterating on a document is far cheaper than iterating on changes spread across a codebase.
- **Habit 5, shift testing left.** Linters, unit, integration, performance, and security tests are what let the agent self-correct for hours. Her specific investment: mock services with deterministic responses that run entirely locally, so the agent never waits on cloud dependencies. Faster feedback means more loops per unit time. Her honest framing is that this was always good hygiene and the ROI is only now high enough to actually fund it.
- **The costs are named, not hidden.** "Flow max" burnout, with engineers staying up late chasing the prompt that will make an agent run productively overnight. Cognitive load from parallel agents and constant terminal-tab switching. And reviewing AI output being harder than writing it, particularly early in career, because senior engineers have spent years building the review muscle that juniors have not.
- **New bottlenecks replace the old one.** When code takes one to two months instead of nine to twelve, the two months of decision-making and the launch approval process become the long pole. Frontier teams now spend more time deciding than coding, which makes fast reversible decisions the highest-leverage organisational change.
- **Do not scale too broadly too fast.** Amazon deliberately learned on a pathfinder team, then a sprint, then 50 teams. 2026's problem is scaling to 2,000. Rolling out organisation-wide before best practices exist just produces many teams doing it badly.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **The self-validation loop is the actual mechanism, and it is a compute-for-attention trade.** Babysitting means one human blocked on one agent. Feeding means the agent burns tokens on its own verification cycles while the human works elsewhere. The multiplier does not come from the model being smarter, it comes from moving the human off the critical path. That reframes the economics: you are deliberately spending more inference to buy back human serialisation, which is almost always the right trade at current token prices versus engineer salaries.
- **Fast local feedback is the rate limiter on autonomous runs.** Deterministic local mocks instead of live integration tests is the highest-leverage optimization in the talk. If each verification cycle costs 30 seconds locally versus 5 minutes against cloud services, the agent completes ten times as many correction loops in an unattended hour. This is straightforward throughput arithmetic and it explains why the "slow down to speed up" investment pays off.
- **Typed languages as an error-signal channel.** The argument is that compiler diagnostics are a free, high-precision reward signal, while untyped languages force the model to guess and hand back plausible-looking code. Same principle Stephanie Jarmak identified with tool error messages: the quality of your failure output determines how many turns the agent wastes.
- **Context pruning as a live maintenance task.** Steering files accumulate model-specific workarounds that become dead weight. Every stale "do not" costs prefill tokens on every single call and can actively misdirect a newer model. This is the same version-skew problem Imad Touil raises for skills, and neither speaker has a systematic answer beyond "build the habit."
- **Specification-first is cheap iteration.** Editing a design document is O(document), editing a generated implementation is O(codebase). Correcting intent before generation avoids paying generation cost twice.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The tooling context: Kiro was previewed at re:Invent in December 2025 as an autonomous coding agent claimed to work independently for days, and launched internationally on May 7, 2026 as a ground-up replacement for Amazon Q Developer rather than an update to it. Its central positioning is spec-driven development, requiring structured specifications before code generation, which is exactly Liguori's habit four productised. At the AWS New York Summit in June 2026 that framing was pushed further toward aerospace-style specification standards. So the product and the practice are the same argument, which is worth remembering: this is a talk about Amazon's internal use of an AWS product, given by an engineer who builds that product.

The number to hold against the 4.5x claim is METR's randomized controlled trial from July 2025: 16 experienced open-source contributors completed real tasks **19% slower** when permitted to use AI tools, while estimating afterwards that they had been roughly 20% faster. The mechanism METR identified was added cognitive load and context-switching, which is precisely the cost Liguori names in her burnout section. That perception gap is the single most important caveat on any self-reported productivity figure, and Amazon's pilots are self-reported and self-measured.

Two things keep the comparison fair rather than dismissive. First, METR itself published an update in February 2026 stating it believes developers are likely more sped up now than its early-2025 estimates suggested, and that it is redesigning its experiment. So the -19% is a measurement of early-2025 tools and early-2025 practice, not a permanent finding. Second, METR measured individual developers on their own familiar repositories using AI conversationally, which is exactly the "babysitting" mode Liguori argues does not work. Her claim is specifically that the gains require abandoning that mode. The two results are less contradictory than they appear, and independent data points the same way: Faros found developers on high-AI-adoption teams interact with 9% more tasks and 47% more pull requests per day, which is a parallelism effect rather than a per-task speed effect.

Where the talk should be discounted. The Bedrock Mantle and Prime Video figures compare an *estimate* to an *outcome*, which is the oldest measurement error in software. A 30-person 18-month estimate for a system that six distinguished engineers then built in 76 days may say as much about the original estimate and the original scope as about the tooling. The 90-weeks-to-24-weeks figure is a revised estimate, not a delivery. Liguori flags the confounds in both cases herself, which is genuinely to her credit and rarer than it should be. Commits and deployment velocity are also Goodhart-vulnerable proxies: an agent that produces many small commits and frequent deploys registers as productive whether or not the shipped value increased. The 50-team pilot at least used deployment to production rather than raw commits, which is the better of the two, but neither measures defect rate, rework, or whether the features mattered. Nothing in the talk addresses long-run code quality or maintenance burden, which is where the bill for this usually arrives.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Build the deterministic local harness before anything else.** For Amit's work this is the single highest-return investment and it maps cleanly: an agent working on quantization or kernel changes needs a fast local loop that runs a small calibration set, checks perplexity or accuracy delta against a threshold, and runs a microbenchmark, all without touching a cluster or waiting on a queue. If that loop takes minutes rather than tens of minutes, an agent can iterate unattended. If it requires a GPU cluster job, it cannot. Everything else in this talk depends on this one thing existing.
- **Define the self-validation bar explicitly, then encode it.** Do not ask an agent to "optimize this kernel." Give it the acceptance criteria: accuracy within X of baseline on this eval, latency below Y at batch size Z, no regression on these three shapes, and the exact command that checks all of it. Then move that into the steering or skills file so it applies by default. This is the difference between babysitting and feeding, expressed concretely.
- **Audit and prune steering files against the current model quarterly.** Every model-specific workaround written for an older model is now prefill cost plus possible misdirection. Pair this with Touil's model-upgrade regression step: re-run tasks with and without each instruction and delete what no longer changes the outcome.
- **Fix error messages in internal tooling, treating it as agent-facing API work.** Profilers, benchmark scripts, and conversion tools that fail with opaque messages cost the agent a full turn each time. Verbose, actionable failures are the cheapest reliability improvement available and they help humans too.
- **Budget for the productivity dip and say so out loud.** The organisational failure mode Liguori admits to as a leader is expecting immediate gains. Anyone proposing this transition should state up front that output falls for roughly two months, and get that agreed before starting, or the investment gets cancelled mid-way.
- **Treat the perception gap as a real risk in your own case.** Given METR's finding that developers believed they were 20% faster while being 19% slower, do not trust your own sense of speedup. Measure something concrete before and after: tasks closed per week, or experiments completed per week, on a fixed definition. Self-report is known to be wrong in a known direction.
- **Watch the review burden on junior colleagues.** Liguori's observation that reviewing AI output is harder than writing it for early-career engineers is the most actionable people-management point here, and it compounds with the closing-of-the-bottom-rung effect noted in the [Ed Zitron analysis](./2026-08-27-AI-Is-A-Massive-Scam-Ed-Zitron-DOAC.md). Junior engineers need deliberate review practice, not just more agent output to review.
