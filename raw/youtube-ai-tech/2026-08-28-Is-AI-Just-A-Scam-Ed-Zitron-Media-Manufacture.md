# IS AI JUST A SCAM?

**Channel:** The Diary Of A CEO
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CwLxp-C4nWY

## TL;DR
A second, shorter cut from the same Ed Zitron interview as the August 27 clip, but with a different emphasis. Where the earlier clip attacked the financials, this one attacks the manufacture of consent. Zitron's charge is that generative AI was sold in the register of magic (it will replace all jobs, it will cure cancer) while the actual product is "boring cloud software that's extremely expensive and unprofitable, and also unreliable at its core." He alleges the overstatement spans capability, roadmap, and underlying financials simultaneously, and that it works by exploiting structural weaknesses in journalism, in the economy, and in the parties nominally responsible for scrutiny: sell-side analysts and governments. His summary phrase is "a directionless egregore of capitalism," and his mechanism claim is that if the narrative frightens and demonises labour hard enough, adoption becomes coerced rather than chosen.

## Key Takeaways
- **The distinctive claim here is about adoption mechanism, not product quality.** "The largest non-consensual push of technology in history" is an argument that demand was manufactured through fear of professional obsolescence rather than earned through demonstrated utility. That is a different and more interesting allegation than "the product is bad."
- **He names three simultaneous overstatements:** what the technology can do now, what it will do later, and the financial condition of the companies making the claims. Bundling these is deliberate, and it is also where the argument becomes hard to falsify, since each requires separate evidence.
- **"Boring cloud software" is the most useful phrase in the clip.** It reframes LLM APIs as what they materially are, metered remote compute with a probabilistic output. Strip the framing and the appropriate question becomes the ordinary enterprise software question: what does it cost, what does it replace, and how reliable is it.
- **"Unreliable at its core" is a correct technical statement, imprecisely deployed.** Non-determinism and hallucination are architectural properties, not bugs pending a fix. Where he overreaches is treating that as disqualifying rather than as a constraint that determines which applications are viable.
- **The media-mechanism critique has a specific target: three years of outlets asserting "this will take your job, you must use this, or you will fall behind."** He is describing a feedback loop in which coverage creates adoption pressure, adoption pressure validates coverage, and neither is grounded in measured outcomes.
- **The accountability failure he identifies is sell-side analysts and governments,** which is a more precise claim than blaming hype generally. Sell-side incentives around companies they cover, and government incentives around industrial policy, both point the same direction.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The technically substantive claim to extract here is the reliability one, because it determines application viability and it is the part Amit can reason about rigorously.

"Unreliable at its core" is accurate in a specific sense: an autoregressive model samples from a distribution, so identical inputs need not produce identical outputs, and outputs carry no calibrated confidence about factuality. That is not a defect awaiting a patch, it is what the architecture is. The engineering consequence is that any deployment must either tolerate a non-zero error rate or wrap the model in a verification layer that catches errors deterministically. This is exactly the pattern that shows up repeatedly in the AI Engineer conference talks in this same corpus: Cloudflare's three-agent draft-verify-tone chain, Notion's generate-three-score-pick-one, Ramp's human-in-the-loop approval gates, and formal verification approaches. The industry has independently converged on verification wrappers because the core is probabilistic. Zitron treats that as evidence of a broken product. It is better read as evidence of a maturing engineering discipline around a probabilistic component, in the way that error-correcting codes are not evidence that storage media are a con.

The "boring cloud software that's extremely expensive" framing is where his argument and Amit's job intersect most directly. If the honest description of an LLM API is metered remote compute, then the entire competitive question is cost per unit of delivered capability, which is precisely the quantity that quantization, pruning, distillation, and routing move. Zitron's critique implicitly prices the field's value at the size of the cost gap, and that makes efficiency work the most commercially load-bearing research direction in the industry rather than a supporting one.

One thing worth noting about his rhetorical structure: bundling capability overstatement, roadmap overstatement, and financial overstatement into a single charge makes the whole unfalsifiable while each part is individually testable. The financial claim is the strongest and best-evidenced. The capability claim is partially true and depends heavily on which promises you count. The roadmap claim is unfalsifiable by construction, since it concerns the future. A careful reader should evaluate them separately, which is what the earlier clip's myth-list format actually enables and this clip's rhetoric does not.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
On the financial substance, Zitron's reporting is specific and holds up. OpenAI lost $20.9 billion on $13.07 billion of 2025 revenue, with total 2025 spending of $34 billion and losses up nearly eightfold year over year. Cost of revenue, the line for actually serving model outputs, went from $2.65 billion in 2024 to $7.5 billion in 2025, and OpenAI spent $5.02 billion on Azure inference in the first half of 2025 alone. Cumulative losses have been reported at $38.53 billion. Zitron's own characterisation is that the losses are far higher than most believed and that he cannot see a path to sustainability.

The mechanism behind those losses supports his "extremely expensive" framing more than his "con" framing. SemiAnalysis found that a user can burn hundreds of dollars of tokens on a $20 monthly subscription and thousands on a $200 subscription. That is a pricing-architecture failure: flat fees written against unbounded consumption. It is a real and serious problem, and it is a solvable one through metering, routing, and rate design, none of which requires the technology to work better than it does.

Two things to hold against the clip. First, "non-consensual push" is doing a lot of work. Roughly a billion people have chosen to use these products, which is weak evidence for coercion, though Zitron's narrower point about professional adoption pressure in workplaces is more defensible. Second, his priors are directional and disclosed elsewhere: he has argued Apple's roughly $14 billion AI spend is a bet on the bubble collapsing and that Apple will "watch everything burn." He is a critic with a thesis and a public position, which is legitimate but means this is advocacy rather than assessment.

It is also worth noting what the record does not support in his framing. The claim that the product is merely boring cloud software sits awkwardly against the measured results in this same corpus: Sourcegraph's CodeScaleBench found retrieval precision on organisation-scale tasks going from P@5 = 0.007 without an MCP layer to 0.471 with it, with roughly 30% lower cost per task and 38% faster execution. Ramp reported a 97% increase in follow-up with lower-intent leads, the most causally clean metric in that whole conference. These are unglamorous, narrow, measurable productivity gains on specific workflows. They are exactly what "boring cloud software" that genuinely works looks like, and they are inconsistent with "con" while being entirely consistent with "wildly overstated."

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Adopt "boring cloud software, priced by the token" as the internal framing for AI project justification.** It forces the right questions: what is the cost per completed task, what does it replace, what is the measured error rate. Amit can use this framing to kill weak projects faster and to defend the strong ones on numbers rather than narrative.
- **Design for the probabilistic core rather than arguing about it.** Zitron's reliability critique is technically correct and the correct response is architectural: a deterministic verification layer around every probabilistic step, scoped narrowly (numeric checks, schema validation, formal proofs where available) rather than a general-purpose critic. This is the pattern the entire AI Engineer track independently converged on.
- **Use the financial figures as the business case for efficiency research, and be specific.** $7.5 billion of annual cost of revenue at one company, growing nearly threefold year over year, is the market for quantization, distillation, and routing. Any project proposal Amit writes should quote that trajectory rather than arguing efficiency matters in the abstract.
- **Fix the metering mismatch where he can influence it.** The subscription tail problem is a pricing-architecture failure that routing directly addresses. Position routing work as unit-economics defence on the heavy-usage decile, which is where all the loss is, rather than as average-cost reduction.
- **Read the three overstatement claims separately, and hold the field to the same standard.** Financial claims: well evidenced, act on them. Capability claims: partly true, so measure your own workloads rather than trusting vendor benchmarks. Roadmap claims: unfalsifiable, discount accordingly. That discipline is useful in both directions, it applies to Zitron's own predictions too.
