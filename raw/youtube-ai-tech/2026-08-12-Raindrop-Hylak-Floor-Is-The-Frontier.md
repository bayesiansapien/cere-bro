# Designing Agents (The Floor Is the Frontier) — Ben Hylak, Raindrop

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jHMiYtjoJfA

## TL;DR
Hylak's central distinction is benchmark maxxing versus floor raising, and his argument is that the floor is where the money and the trust actually live. The ceiling is the most impressive emergent thing your agent can do; the floor is the worst thing it can do, meaning recommending a competitor, deleting data, or sending an AI-slop email to a customer because it technically had mailbox access. Trust breaks at the floor, not at the ceiling, and he points at the sycophancy incidents as floor failures rather than capability failures. The most contrarian thing he says, delivered at a conference track literally titled continual learning, is that there is very little continual learning happening in the real world at labs or in shipped products. His attack on prevailing eval practice is that it is stuck in the chatbot era: a curated thousand-example dataset with known answers made sense when you knew 80 to 90 percent of what users would ask, and it breaks the moment you change model or swap harness, at which point 80 percent of your tool-call assertions are worthless. His practical position is that since the prompt is now the entire harness and codebase, evals should look like code, meaning unit and end-to-end tests run locally, not managed prompt playgrounds in the cloud. The three tactical lessons are the parts nobody else is saying: clusters are not issues because you cannot reliably track cluster boundaries over time and cluster labels collapse distinct root causes; code mode applied to traces means writing classifiers and running them sandboxed at production scale; and agents are bad at anomaly detection, so extract deterministic signals like keyword frequency spikes first and use the agent to investigate anomalies you already found.

## Key Takeaways
- The framing question is not what issues your agent has but how you make it better, because some issues are unsolvable at your layer and some are not worth solving. His example: do not train your own frontier-level model to fix something a model upgrade will fix for free.
- Ceiling versus floor, defined precisely. Ceiling is the craziest emergent capability, the thing nobody expects it could do. Floor is the worst thing it can do. His examples of the floor are all business-catastrophic rather than benchmark-visible: recommending a competitor, deleting data, sending slop email from an inbox it had access to.
- His claim about which one matters: the floor is what breaks user trust, and the visible societal failures of agents so far, sycophancy in particular, are floor problems rather than capability problems.
- The blunt observation about the track's own premise: there is not much continual learning in the real world. Not at the labs, not in shipped products. He would rather have a dialogue than present frameworks, because there are no good standards yet.
- Why agent is the right word after all, having previously hated it: agents behave like self-aware entities that roam an environment, and when they hit a roadblock they get creative. That creativity is simultaneously the source of the value and the source of the catastrophe, because "I'll just decompile this" is a solution you never imagined and sometimes did not want.
- Why chatbot-era evals worked and no longer do: with a chatbot you knew the answer to most questions users would ask, so string matching and fact checking covered 80 to 90 percent of the surface. That assumption is gone.
- The eval fragility argument, which is the strongest practical point in the talk: hand-built eval sets break on model change and harness change. Write an eval asserting a specific tool call, switch to a different agent CLI, and 80 percent of your suite is invalid. The one guarantee is that things keep changing.
- The theatre test, and it is a good one to apply to your own process: would you actually delay shipping a model upgrade by two weeks to update your evals? Most teams say no, which tells you what those evals are worth.
- Why lab language misleads product teams: labs and downstream companies use the same word for different activities. Labs build general-purpose systems where an API-level failure means something different; companies are trying to imbue domain knowledge about the shape of their data, what it means, and how to access it. Copying lab benchmark practice imports the wrong responsibility model.
- The responsibility-shape question from their quiz, which is the right way to size your floor risk: are your users domain experts, and is the agent replacing or augmenting them. Tab completion is trivially reversible by deleting the suggestion, a coding CLI failure is often user error and recoverable, an AI doctor is a completely different shape of responsibility.
- Evals as code, not as prompts. Because the prompt is now the whole harness rather than a string, evals should look like unit tests and end-to-end tests, run locally. He cites Sentry's Vitest-based eval package and notes OpenAI calls the same thing macro evals. He says he barely sees anyone using managed cloud prompt playgrounds anymore.
- Two numbers per issue, and he insists these are non-negotiable: when it started and what percentage of users it affects. Agents generate an unbounded number of problems, so triage requires novelty and blast radius. "This started yesterday" makes you ask what changed, model or downstream dependency. Three users versus a hundred thousand is a different decision entirely.
- Scale changes the entire approach, and his first question to any customer is how many users they have. At tens of millions of messages a day, experiments on a small free-tier sample are extremely valuable. At five to ten users, which can still be a critical internal enterprise app, A/B testing is the wrong tool and he says so.
- Tactical lesson 1, clusters are not issues. Clustering all traces is useful for one-off error analysis but does not scale as an issue-tracking primitive: temporal clustering is unreliable, you do not control the boundaries, and what counts as the same issue is company-specific. His example is a "pricing issues" cluster that merges wrong price quoted with wrong refund calculated, two entirely different root causes. And a cluster gives you neither of the two numbers you need.
- Tactical lesson 2, code mode scales to traces. Write classifiers as code, run them in a sandbox, run them at production scale. This is the MCP code-mode pattern applied to observability.
- Tactical lesson 3, agents are bad at anomaly detection but good at investigating anomalies. Pull out every deterministic signal you can, keyword frequency being his example, and hand the agent a concrete spike to investigate rather than asking it to find something odd.
- Products mentioned: Raindrop as hosted issue detection, explicitly positioned as Sentry for agents; Workshop as their open-source local tracing tool, which he recommends specifically for experimenting with self-healing loops because the agent can extend the tool itself; and howtoeval.com as their no-nonsense guide.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The ceiling-floor decomposition is the most portable idea here and it maps onto compression work more cleanly than onto agent work. Every compression evaluation I have seen reports the ceiling: mean accuracy, mean perplexity, aggregate benchmark score. None of them report the floor, which for a quantised model is the worst thing it now does that the full-precision model did not. The floor failures of compression are specific and known: refusal behaviour degrading so the model answers something it should decline, calibration collapsing so it states wrong answers with high confidence, non-English output degenerating into repetition, long-context retrieval silently returning the wrong span. A mean accuracy delta of half a point can hide all of those, because they are tail events and the mean is not sensitive to tails. Hylak's argument that the floor is what breaks trust is exactly the reason quantised models get described as feeling worse while benchmarking as equivalent. The actionable form of this is that a compression report needs a worst-case section, and the metrics in it should be tail metrics: worst-case per-domain degradation, maximum confidence on wrong answers, rate of catastrophic output modes.

His eval-fragility argument has a sharper version for research than for product. He says evals break on model change and harness change, and that hand-built suites are a liability because they slow you down. In compression the equivalent brittleness is that your eval suite is written against a specific base model family, and when the family changes, the calibration set, the layer-sensitivity profile, and often the metric thresholds are all invalid. The response he recommends, evals as code, is right for a different reason than he gives: code-shaped evals are cheap to regenerate, so the suite becomes a function of the model rather than a fixed artifact. Concretely, an eval defined as "for each layer, assert activation divergence below threshold T computed from this model's own full-precision baseline" survives a base model change; an eval defined as "assert perplexity below 6.2" does not.

The clusters-are-not-issues lesson is the deepest technical point and it generalises to anything you track over time. His stated reasons are exactly right: cluster assignment is not stable under distribution shift, boundaries are set by the algorithm rather than by you, and the semantic granularity that matters is domain-specific. This is why unsupervised grouping fails as a monitoring primitive and why classification against a fixed taxonomy succeeds even though the taxonomy is imperfect. The compression analogue is a failure I have watched happen: grouping regression examples by embedding similarity to find what a quantised model broke. The clusters look meaningful, they are unreproducible across runs, and they merge "lost the arithmetic carry" with "lost the units conversion" into a "math errors" bucket that suggests no fix. Defining a fixed set of named failure classes and writing a classifier per class is less elegant and vastly more useful, because the count per class is comparable across model versions.

Code mode applied to traces is the highest-leverage tip in the talk and it transfers directly. The pattern is: instead of an LLM reading each item and judging it, an LLM writes a program that judges all items, and you run the program cheaply at full scale. For compression evaluation this is the difference between a judge model scoring ten thousand outputs and a written classifier scoring ten million, and the second is not just cheaper but deterministic, which means the score is comparable across runs. It also composes with his anomaly-detection point: the deterministic classifier finds the spike, and the expensive model is spent only on explaining the spike. That two-tier structure, cheap deterministic detection plus expensive model-based investigation, is the correct cost architecture for any large-scale eval pipeline and it is a routing decision in the same family as the ones you make for inference.

The anomaly detection claim deserves a note because it is stated more strongly than the evidence supports and it is still probably right. Language models are poor at anomaly detection for a structural reason: anomaly means rare relative to a distribution, and a model prompted with a sample has no reliable estimate of base rates, so it flags whatever is salient rather than whatever is unlikely. That is not a capability gap that scale fixes, it is a missing statistic. Give the model the base rate, as a keyword frequency or a per-class count over time, and it becomes good at the reasoning part. Applied to your own work: do not ask a model which of these quantised outputs look wrong, compute the divergence distribution, take the tail, and ask the model why those specific ones diverged.

The scale-dependence point is worth internalising as a general rule about method selection. His answer to what should I do is what is your n, and the honest consequence is that at n equals five the entire statistical apparatus is unavailable and you are doing case analysis. Research evaluation has the same structure and the same common error: reporting a mean over a small eval set as if it were an estimate, when the correct treatment at that size is to look at every failure individually. Compression evals on small domain-specific sets are exactly this case, and per-example inspection beats an aggregate you cannot put an error bar on.

Where I would push back is the "there is not much continual learning in the real world" claim, which is true as stated and slightly unfair given the same-day evidence. [Applied Compute's talk](2026-08-12-Applied-Compute-Denton-Hinting-Distillation-Quadrants.md) showed two production deployments with numbers, one of them a fully online train-and-serve loop, which is precisely the thing he says nobody does. The reconciliation is that his customer base is application teams downstream of models, and for them continual learning is not available as a tool, so from where he sits it does not exist. That is a real observation about diffusion rather than about capability, and it is the useful version of his point: the techniques exist, almost nobody outside the vendors can use them yet, and floor raising with prompts, harness changes, and classifiers is what is actually available. It also sits usefully against [Hooker's thesis](2026-08-12-Adaption-Sara-Hooker-Gradient-Free-Continual-Learning.md) that adaptation is the new frontier, since Hylak is describing what adaptation looks like when you have no access to weights at all.

This is also the cleanest statement yet of the position the [benchmaxxing critique](2026-08-02-Benchmaxxing-Plague-Surge-AI.md) implies but does not name, and it complements [Build Evals That Actually Matter](2026-07-19-Build-Evals-That-Actually-Matter.md) by supplying the taxonomy that talk lacked. The [SonderMind evals-driven guardrails work](2026-07-25-Evals-Driven-Guardrails-Mental-Health-SonderMind.md) is the same argument from a domain where the floor is the only thing that matters.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The company and the tools check out. Hylak is co-founder and CTO of Raindrop, previously at Apple and SpaceX, and Raindrop's positioning is agent monitoring and observability with the explicit thesis that agent failures differ from traditional software failures because of non-determinism and an unbounded input and output space. Workshop is real, open source at raindrop-ai/workshop, and was covered by VentureBeat in May 2026. The technical details matter for whether you would use it: local-first tracing with replay, all telemetry streaming to a localhost dashboard, every token and tool call and decision captured, and state in a single SQLite file with no server and no Docker. That is a genuinely low-friction setup and the SQLite detail is the reason it is usable for research work rather than only for product teams. howtoeval.com exists and contains the same benchmark-maxx-versus-raise-the-floor framing he presented, so the guide is the durable artifact of this talk.

Code mode is not a Raindrop invention and the numbers behind it are worth knowing because they explain why the pattern scales. Cloudflare shipped a code-mode MCP server in 2026 covering over 2,500 endpoints, wrapping the API surface in a typed SDK, with a claimed 99.9 percent reduction in input tokens versus exposing tools directly. The mechanism is the same one Hylak is recommending for traces: move from many model calls over data to one model call that writes code which processes the data. Sentry's MCP server does use Vitest for its eval package, so his citation is accurate, and Sentry's own MCP-plus-agent debugging workflow is documented. See the [MCP Apps](2026-08-02-MCP-Apps-Extending-The-Frontier.md) notes for the adjacent tooling context.

One thing to verify before adopting his advice wholesale. His claim that nobody uses managed prompt playgrounds anymore is an observation from a customer base of agent-building companies, and it is self-serving given that Raindrop sells the alternative. The claim that the prompt is now the whole harness is the load-bearing premise, and it is accurate for coding agents and tool-heavy agents while being much less true for the many production systems that are still a prompt template plus a retrieval call. If your system is the latter, prompt-level tooling is not obsolete for you. The sycophancy reference in the talk is garbled in the transcript but clearly points at the well-documented GPT-4o sycophancy episode, which does support his framing: that was a floor failure that no capability benchmark caught and that visibly cost trust.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Add a floor section to your compression reports and make it the section you read first. Ceiling metrics you already have. The floor metrics to add: worst-case per-domain accuracy delta rather than mean, maximum confidence on incorrect answers, rate of degenerate output modes such as repetition loops, refusal-behaviour drift on a fixed safety probe set, and non-English degradation. A half-point mean delta routinely hides all of these because they are tail events.
2. Instrument the two numbers for every regression you track: when it appeared and what fraction of your eval distribution it affects. Applied to compression, that means every regression gets tagged with the recipe version that introduced it and the share of the eval set it touches. Without those you cannot triage, and the number of things a compressed model does slightly worse is effectively unbounded.
3. Replace embedding-cluster analysis of regressions with a fixed named taxonomy plus one classifier per class. His reasoning applies exactly: cluster boundaries move between runs so the counts are not comparable across model versions, and semantic clusters merge failures with different root causes and therefore different fixes. Name your failure classes once, write a classifier per class, track counts.
4. Implement the code-mode pattern in your eval pipeline this week, because the cost argument is decisive. Have a model write deterministic classifiers, run them sandboxed over the full output set, and reserve model-based judging for investigating the cases the classifiers flag. That gets you full-population coverage, run-to-run comparability, and a two-tier cost structure instead of paying a judge per output.
5. Stop asking a model to find what looks wrong and start giving it the tail of a computed distribution. Compute activation divergence, logit KL, or per-token entropy delta between full-precision and compressed, take the extreme tail, and ask the model to explain those specific cases. Models lack base rates, which is why they are bad at detection and good at explanation once you supply the statistic.
6. Apply the theatre test to your own eval suite. If a new base model dropped tomorrow, would you delay evaluating it by two weeks to rewrite your evals? Whatever you would abandon in that scenario is not load-bearing. Rewrite it as code parameterised on the model's own baseline rather than on absolute thresholds, so it survives the change.
7. Size your method to your n before choosing it. Small domain-specific eval sets do not support aggregate reporting, and the honest treatment at that size is per-example inspection of every failure. Save the experimental apparatus for the cases where you have the population to support it.
8. Install Workshop for your own agent-assisted research workflow, since the friction is near zero. Local tracing, replay, SQLite file, no server. The specific use worth trying is the self-healing loop he recommends it for: capture the trajectory when an agent-run sweep goes wrong, replay it, and let the agent extend the tooling to cover what it could not see.
