# The Case for Data Centers in Space

**Channel:** Y Combinator
**Published:** 2026-08-05
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A9JDkiYEhfY

## TL;DR
Philip Johnston, co-founder and CEO of StarCloud, on why orbital compute stopped being a joke. The thesis rests on a convergence of two curves rather than one: launch cost falling fast enough to make mass in orbit cheap, and terrestrial datacentre construction becoming politically impossible fast enough to make ground capacity expensive. The company ran the break-even calculation explicitly and got $500 per kilogram of launch cost for orbital datacentres to pencil, against roughly $50 per kilogram for the space-based solar concept they started with and abandoned. StarCloud-1 flew in November 2025 with an Nvidia H100 submerged in phase change material, trained NanoGPT in orbit, and ran Gemini. Roughly 100 VCs said no before the first cheque. The engineering is now split fifty-fifty between two problems: rejecting heat in a vacuum, and keeping high-density silicon working under radiation. The founder's single most portable piece of advice is to book the launch before you know what you are putting on it.

## Key Takeaways
- **The economics hinge on one number: $500 per kilogram.** That is StarCloud's computed break-even launch cost for orbital datacentres. Space-based solar needed $50 per kg and was therefore abandoned after two months, along with the original company name, Lumin Orbit.
- **Why datacentres and not asteroid mining, orbital manufacturing or hotels: no re-entry.** The product is bits, and bits come down over a laser link. Everything else in the "cheap launch unlocks this" category requires returning mass to Earth, which is the expensive half.
- **Space-based solar dies on transmission efficiency.** Roughly 95 percent of the energy is lost beaming power from orbit to ground. Moving the compute to the power rather than the power to the compute is the entire pivot.
- **The two hard problems are thermal and radiation, and the team is split evenly on them.** Everything else, interconnect included, they are letting others solve. Interconnect is a SpaceX contract for Starlink laser terminals on the next 20 satellites.
- **Radiation strategy is component selection over rad-hardening.** Test ten commercial or automotive-grade parts, pick the best survivor, add shielding. Explicitly modelled on how SpaceX drove satellite cost down. They claim to be the only people who know where an H100, H200 and B200 fail under heavy ions and high-velocity protons.
- **Thermal on StarCloud-1 was a hack that does not scale.** The entire assembly, GPU, memory, power delivery, submerged in phase change material. Low duty cycle: run, melt the wax, wait for it to solidify. StarCloud-2 moves to direct-to-chip liquid cooling running continuously.
- **The roadmap is three products.** StarCloud-1, proof that an H100 works in orbit. StarCloud-2, a 10 kW spacecraft selling compute to government and military satellites, plus a Bitcoin mining ASIC and AWS Outpost hardware. StarCloud-3, a 200 kW, 3-tonne, 6-metre spacecraft, 50 per Starship, roughly 10 MW of new compute per launch.
- **Scale claim: FCC filing for 88,000 satellites, about 20 GW.** Largest terrestrial datacentre today is around 1 GW. They claim up to 10 TW fits in dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit.
- **The cost delta against incumbents is the real story.** A prime quoted $75M to $100M for what StarCloud-1 did. They flew StarCloud-2 including launch for $2M.
- **Timeline is entirely gated on Starship.** End of 2028 for the ramp that makes them competitive with terrestrial datacentres, contingent on Starship cadence, with Falcon 9 being phased out.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The thermal constraint is the one worth thinking hardest about, because it inverts an assumption most datacentre intuition is built on. On Earth, heat leaves a rack by conduction into a fluid and convection into air, and the cost of removing a watt scales roughly linearly with the watt. In vacuum there is no conduction or convection path to anywhere. The only mechanism is radiation, which goes as the fourth power of absolute temperature times emitting area. That fourth-power dependence is the entire game: it means running the radiator hot is enormously more efficient per square metre than running it cool, so the design pressure is to accept the highest junction and coolant temperatures the silicon will tolerate. A chip that can run reliably at a 20°C higher coolant temperature does not save 20 degrees of cooling, it reduces the required radiator area substantially. That is a fundamentally different optimization target from terrestrial design, where the incentive runs the other way.

This has a direct implication for anyone thinking about compression and efficiency in this context. On Earth, the case for quantization and sparsity is watts and dollars. In orbit it is radiator square metres and therefore deployed mass, which converts to launch cost at $500 per kilogram. The conversion factor from FLOPs-per-watt to dollars is far steeper, which means the marginal value of an efficiency gain is much higher in orbit than on the ground. If StarCloud's economics are real, orbital compute becomes the most aggressive possible customer for low-precision inference, structured sparsity and anything that reduces joules per token, because every joule saved is radiator mass not launched. Their claimed radiator, 10x better mass per watt and 500x better cost per watt than the ISS, is the headline metric, though he concedes immediately that the ISS is an easy benchmark.

The radiation strategy deserves attention because it is a soft-error problem dressed as a hardware problem. Bit flips from heavy ions and protons are not preventable at reasonable shielding mass, so the mitigation is a mix of shielding, component selection and, in his phrasing, software. That last word is doing a lot of work. Silent data corruption in a training run is the failure mode that should worry anyone here: a flipped bit in a gradient or an optimizer state does not crash the job, it quietly degrades the result, and detecting it requires either redundant computation or checksummed state. Terrestrial hyperscalers already treat silent data corruption as a real problem at fleet scale; in orbit the rate is higher by orders of magnitude. This is the same argument as the [Goldsboro correlated-failure analysis](../deep-science/2026-08-04-Goldsboro-B52-Nuclear-Near-Miss.md): you need a verification channel that is independent of the thing that is failing, and ECC on memory does not cover the datapath.

The DC power point made in passing is genuinely underrated and is the part that applies on the ground today. Solar panels generate DC. GPUs consume DC. Terrestrial solar installations convert DC to AC to feed the grid and then back to DC at the rack, losing several percent in each direction plus the mass and cost of the converters. StarCloud strips the AC-DC converters entirely and runs DC-to-DC. As one of the hosts notes, this unlocks a design for terrestrial solar-direct GPU datacentres too, and the same argument applies to any co-located generation. The gain is not huge per conversion but it compounds with the cooling load avoided by not dissipating conversion losses.

The other architectural note: they modified the H100 heavily, stripping AC-DC converters, cold plates and other mass, then stiffening the board and making it radiation tolerant. Nvidia is now working with them on a purpose-built space part, referred to in the interview as a Rubin-generation space chip. If a chip vendor is designing SKUs for orbital thermal and radiation envelopes, that is a stronger signal about the seriousness of the category than any funding round.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The verifiable claims hold up well. StarCloud-1 launched in November 2025 on a SpaceX rideshare, a roughly 60 kg satellite about the size of a small fridge, carrying an Nvidia H100 that operated in orbit. It ran a version of Google's Gemini and trained NanoGPT, Andrej Karpathy's small GPT implementation, on the complete works of Shakespeare, which is reported as the first LLM trained in space. Nvidia has publicly backed and promoted the company. The FCC accepted StarCloud's filing on 13 March for as many as 88,000 satellites across a range of low Earth orbits, and the company's longer-term concepts include four-kilometre solar arrays powering a five-gigawatt orbital datacentre. The company is based in Redmond, Washington. The $170M Benchmark-led round and the "fastest growing unicorn in YC history" framing come from the interview and I could not independently verify the round size, so treat that specific figure as founder-stated.

The regulatory tailwind is real and larger than the clip conveys. On 14 July 2026, Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order making New York the first US state to impose a statewide moratorium on new hyperscale datacentres, barring construction of facilities drawing 50 MW or more for up to a year. The driver was explicitly electricity bills and public pressure, with New York already paying the fourth-highest energy prices in the country at 56 percent above the national average as of April 2026. Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have separately proposed a federal datacentre construction ban. Johnston's line about it being banned in New York and likely in seven other states is not hyperbole, and the political environment has moved faster than the interview even suggests.

Two of the myth-busting claims late in the interview should not be repeated without checking. The water analogy, that a several-hundred-megawatt datacentre consumes about as much water as a McDonald's, is only defensible for a fully closed-loop design, and the honest framing is that closed-loop trades water consumption for power consumption rather than eliminating a resource cost. The stronger claim, that adding datacentres increases grid capacity and brings prices down over time, is contested and sits awkwardly against the reason for the New York moratorium: rising consumer bills in the fourth most expensive electricity market in the country. There is a real argument that new load can finance transmission and generation buildout that eventually lowers unit costs, but presenting it as settled while the political backlash is being driven by the opposite lived experience is the weakest passage in an otherwise careful interview.

The load-bearing uncertainty is launch cost, and Johnston is admirably direct that they are not out of the woods. Their break-even is $500 per kilogram. The rideshare they booked cost roughly $300,000, and current SpaceX rideshare pricing sits well above their break-even on a per-kilogram basis. Everything depends on Starship reaching a cadence and price point that has been forecast repeatedly and slipped repeatedly. His own timeline, end of 2028 for competing with terrestrial datacentres, already assumes Starship ramps roughly on schedule. One further arithmetic check worth flagging: 50 StarCloud-3 units at 3 tonnes each is 150 tonnes per Starship, which is at or above the optimistic end of published Starship payload figures to LEO, so the 10 MW per launch number assumes best-case performance.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
The most immediately useful reframe is the constraint substitution. StarCloud is a bet that the binding constraint on AI compute has shifted from silicon supply to power siting, and that the correct response is to move compute to where power is unconstrained rather than to move power to where compute is convenient. Whether or not orbit is the right answer, the diagnosis is worth taking seriously as a planning assumption: if datacentre siting is the bottleneck, then joules per token becomes a strategic variable rather than a cost-optimization detail, and compression work moves from the efficiency column to the capacity column. That is a materially better argument for a pruning or quantization roadmap than cost savings, and it is the framing to use when justifying that work.

Concretely, add an energy-per-token metric alongside latency and quality in any compression or routing evaluation, if it is not already there. Most compression work is measured in parameters, memory footprint and latency, none of which map cleanly to joules. The orbital case makes the conversion explicit because joules turn into radiator mass turns into launch cost, but the same quantity is what will price terrestrial capacity in a siting-constrained world. Measuring it now costs little and it is the number that will matter.

The second transferable item is the silent corruption problem, which applies well before anyone puts a GPU in orbit. If a training or inference pipeline has no mechanism to detect a wrong result that did not crash, it has no defence against the failure mode that matters most at scale. Redundant computation on a sampled fraction of batches, checksummed optimizer state, and periodic deterministic replay of a fixed input are all cheap. The orbital case just makes the rate high enough that you cannot ignore it.

Third, the founder's operating advice generalises past space. Booking a launch before knowing the payload is a commitment device that converts an open-ended research problem into a dated engineering one, and the reported result was that the payload got dramatically more ambitious under the deadline rather than less. The direct analogue for a research programme is to commit to the evaluation date and the artifact format before committing to the approach. This is the same discipline as building the eval before the technology in [the Waymo talk](2026-08-03-Waymo-Dolgov-Demo-Is-1-Percent.md), arrived at from the opposite direction.

Finally, note the market signal in the funding story. Roughly 100 VCs passed at a $10M post-money valuation, then another 20 after Demo Day. The hosts attribute the reversal partly to the view that software no longer has a moat, with SaaS multiples compressing as coding agents got good. Whatever the cause, capital has rotated toward hard tech where the defensibility is physical rather than algorithmic. For anyone weighing where optimization expertise is scarcest and best rewarded, the intersection of AI efficiency and physical infrastructure constraints is where that rotation is pointing.
