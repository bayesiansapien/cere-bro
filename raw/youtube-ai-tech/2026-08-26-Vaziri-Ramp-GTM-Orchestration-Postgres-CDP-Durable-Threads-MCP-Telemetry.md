# The Building Blocks of GTM Orchestration — Arman Vaziri, Ramp

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VjEP0xqTUI0

## TL;DR
Vaziri leads product and sales-led growth engineering at Ramp, and this is the most architecturally detailed of the seven GTM talks at this conference. The end goal is orchestration, meaning you describe a motion in natural language and it fans out across every execution channel simultaneously. His running example is offering Titleist Pro V1s to golfers at East Coast construction companies, spinning up the audience, the incentive, outbound sequences, ad creative, landing pages and in-app notifications from one stated intent. He notes in passing that it works really well, which is the sort of detail that makes the example credible. The diagnosis is that good ideas are abundant and everything downstream of the idea is the bottleneck, so the work is reducing coordination cost. The stack: an internal customer data platform on Postgres, chosen explicitly for transactional guarantees and referential integrity across CRM, product and third-party entities, fed by Kafka for real-time events and by dbt on Snowflake with reverse ETL for batch. Unstructured data is chunked, embedded and served from turbopuffer with hybrid vector, attribute and keyword search. Every agent run is a durable Temporal thread where each tool call and model call is an activity. The strategic move that separates this from the other talks is GT MCP: the same tools the background agents use are exposed to employees, so people build their own automations, and those automations become the product roadmap.

## Key Takeaways
- The bottleneck is not ideas, it is everything after the idea. Pulling an audience, writing enablement material, and convincing people to follow a playbook is a months-scale process, which caps how much experimentation an organisation can run regardless of how creative it is.
- Postgres was chosen for the online CDP over the obvious warehouse-native option, and the reasons given are transactional guarantees and referential integrity between entities spanning CRM, product and third parties. Attribution to the right level of detail is called out as a surprisingly hard problem, along with provenance metadata for where each fact came from and when.
- Three ingestion paths feeding one substrate. Real-time events onto Kafka topics and consumed back into Postgres. Online batch jobs that are mostly API calls, doing pre-computation and pre-enrichment across an addressable market that is effectively the entire US and now expanding internationally. Offline batch in dbt and Snowflake, piped back via reverse ETL into the same layer.
- Unstructured data is treated as the most valuable asset in the warehouse, and Vaziri says so directly. Call transcripts, emails, notes on the real-time side; enablement materials, product knowledge and playbooks on the batch side. All chunked, embedded, and queried through combined vector, attribute and keyword search so an agent pulls only account-scoped context rather than the raw corpus.
- Every agent execution is a durable thread on Temporal with each tool call and model call as an activity. Worker death resumes with accumulated state rather than reprocessing the thread from the beginning. Two other capabilities he names matter as much: config-scoped tool calls, so different agents get different tool, integration and skill surfaces, and built-in human-in-the-loop pause-input-resume.
- Entity resolution is persisted once rather than recomputed per consumer. Mapping meeting attendee emails and titles back to accounts is genuinely hard at Ramp because the same email can act on behalf of multiple businesses, so it is a fuzzy match. Resolving once and persisting means every downstream consumer of "this meeting" inherits the answer.
- Solve for one team, then scale horizontally. Most GTM workflows overlap across teams, everyone outbounds and everyone has meetings, while a few are team-specific like QBR generation. The horizontal extension is mostly new skills, new data integrations and new ingestion, with the embeddings, custom instructions and durable execution reused as-is.
- Skills exist so users can customise output format and content selection, and Vaziri attributes adoption directly to this. Different account managers want different pre-meeting brief formats, and letting them express that in text is what got them to use it.
- The nightly fan-out is the concrete operational pattern: a scheduled job spawns a set of agents, one per account, each with access to the Postgres CDP, the vector store, system-owned meeting-prep skills, and the user's own custom instructions.
- GT MCP is the most strategically interesting decision in the talk. Employees get a window into the exact tools the background agents use, so they build their own agents and automations. Ramp then analyses the reasoning traces from those remote MCP tool calls.
- User-built automations function as product discovery. When someone connects to the MCP and builds something, they have handed you a problem statement, a proposed solution, and often the prompts, skills and vibe-coded application. Productionising and distributing that is far cheaper than discovering the need through interviews.
- Campaign selection is framed explicitly as a multi-armed bandit, exploring new possibilities against exploiting known returns, with guardrails for compliance, rules of engagement, and not repeating the same action.
- His advice for a small company is to build narrow vertical solutions and stitch them together later. Three years ago there were two of them putting GPT-3.5-generated personalised copy into outbound sequences. You cannot spend a year building the perfect architecture first.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The Postgres choice is the decision most worth defending, because the fashionable answer is to serve everything from the warehouse or a document store and Vaziri went the other way. The property he needs is referential integrity across entities that originate in different systems, where the same real-world company appears as a Salesforce account, a product workspace, a third-party enrichment record and a set of email addresses that may each act for several businesses. Without foreign key constraints and transactional writes, resolution errors do not surface as errors, they surface as an agent confidently generating a meeting brief for the wrong account. That is the failure mode that destroys rep trust permanently, and it is unrecoverable downstream because nothing later in the pipeline has the information to detect it. A warehouse cannot enforce these constraints at write time, and a KV store gives up on them by design. Note the contrast with the Notion talk from the same conference, which serves a denormalised profile from DynamoDB for millisecond point lookups. Both are right for what they optimise: Notion optimised read latency for a profile that is assembled offline, Ramp optimised write-time correctness for entities that arrive continuously from conflicting sources. The generalisable rule is that the store should be chosen by which invariant is expensive to violate, and for entity resolution that invariant is integrity, not latency.

Persisting resolved entities once rather than per consumer is a small point with an outsized payoff, and it is the same principle as materialising an expensive feature transform rather than recomputing it in every model that consumes it. The fuzzy match is expensive, non-deterministic if done with a model, and worst of all inconsistent across consumers if recomputed, so two downstream systems can disagree about which account a meeting belongs to. Resolving once turns a correctness risk into a cache hit.

The turbopuffer choice deserves attention beyond the GTM context, because its architecture is the clearest current example of a trend that is reshaping serving cost across the board. It separates compute from storage with object storage as the durable layer and NVMe plus RAM purely as cache for the active working set. The published economics are stark: roughly $70 per TB per month for S3 plus SSD cache against roughly $1,600 per TB per month for RAM cache plus 3x SSD replication, up to a 95% reduction against in-memory vector databases, with sub-10ms p50 and proven scale at 2.5 trillion documents and 10 million writes per second, used by Cursor, Anthropic, Notion and Atlassian. The structural insight is that most vector workloads have a working set far smaller than the corpus, so paying RAM prices for the whole index is paying for cold data at hot prices. That observation generalises directly to model serving. KV cache offload to CPU memory or NVMe, weight streaming for MoE experts that are inactive on most tokens, and paged attention are all the same move: identify that the hot working set is a small fraction of the total state, tier the storage accordingly, and pay for capacity where it is cheap and for bandwidth only where it is needed. An MoE model where each token activates a small subset of experts has exactly the access-skew profile that makes object-storage-backed vector search work, and the design question is identical, namely how accurately you can predict the working set one step ahead of needing it.

The hybrid retrieval choice is also correct and under-argued in the talk. Combining vector similarity with attribute filters and keyword matching in one query is not a convenience feature, it is what makes account-scoped retrieval sound. Pure vector search over a corpus spanning thousands of accounts will surface semantically similar content from the wrong account, and no amount of embedding quality fixes it because the wrong-account document genuinely is similar. The attribute filter is a hard constraint that semantic similarity cannot express. Any RAG system with a tenancy or scoping dimension needs filtering as a first-class part of the query rather than as post-hoc reranking, and post-filtering after top-k retrieval silently degrades recall when the filter is selective.

The multi-armed bandit framing is the part with the most direct carryover to routing work, and the literature moved substantially in 2026. OrcaRouter, published May 2026, combines a LinUCB contextual bandit over lexical and sentence-embedding features with a hybrid offline-online learning protocol, and ranked second on the RouterArena leaderboard with an arena score of 72.08, hitting 75.54% accuracy at $1.00 per 1,000 queries. That hybrid structure is the important part. Pure online bandits pay real regret during exploration, which in a production router means serving worse answers to real users while learning, and pure offline routers cannot adapt when the query distribution or the model lineup shifts, which now happens monthly. Bootstrapping the bandit from offline preference data and letting online feedback adapt it is the configuration that survives contact with production. PILOT does the same thing with human preference data plus binary online success signals, and MixLLM uses policy gradient over a contextual bandit with domain-aware tags from unsupervised fine-tuning. The theoretical grounding is there too, with LinUCB variants achieving provably sublinear regret under unstructured context evolution. Vaziri's campaign problem and the model-routing problem are the same problem with different arms.

The GT MCP move is the strategic idea worth stealing and it is not really about GTM. By exposing the background agents' exact tool surface to employees, Ramp converted internal tooling into a telemetry channel. Every automation someone builds is a labelled statement of an unmet need plus a candidate solution, expressed in prompts and skills that can be read. The alternative discovery mechanism is interviewing people about what they need, which is slower, lower fidelity, and biased toward what people can articulate. The cost of this design is real and unmentioned: exposing the full tool surface to arbitrary internal builders means the blast radius of a badly configured user agent is the same as a production one, which is where config-scoped tool calls have to be doing more work than the talk credits them for.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The infrastructure claims check out and the components are all real production choices rather than aspirational ones. turbopuffer's architecture is exactly as characterised, object storage as the durable layer with NVMe and RAM as cache, and the cost differential against in-memory vector databases is documented at roughly 95%, with 2026 pricing on pure usage for writes, queries at around $1 per PB, and GB-month storage, against tier minimums of $16, $256 and $4,096 per month. At 100 million to 1 billion 1536-dimension vectors the practical range is $500 to $2,000 per month versus $5,000 to $20,000 on Pinecone Serverless. Production scale is 2.5 trillion documents and 10 million writes per second across Cursor, Anthropic, Notion and Atlassian, which makes Ramp's choice a mainstream one rather than an early bet.

On the bandit framing, the 2026 routing literature is well past the point where this is speculative. There is now a survey specifically on dynamic model routing and cascading for efficient LLM inference, a RouterArena leaderboard with published accuracy-per-dollar figures, and multiple production-oriented systems with hybrid offline-online learning. The most useful number to carry from OrcaRouter is 75.54% accuracy at $1.00 per 1,000 queries, because it establishes a public reference point on the cost-quality frontier that internal routers can be measured against, which the field lacked a year ago.

The one thing to hold lightly is the orchestration vision itself. Everything Vaziri describes as built, the CDP, durable threads, hybrid retrieval, skill library, nightly fan-out, GT MCP, is concrete and in production. The golf-balls-to-East-Coast-construction-golfers scenario is presented as the direction of travel, funnelling into an internal application called Ramp Revenue, with channel owners reviewing and signing off. He is careful about this and the talk is structured to make the point that the vertical builds are the foundation for the horizontal orchestration rather than the orchestration already existing. Read it as a roadmap with unusually solid foundations rather than as a shipped capability. The Pro V1 experiment itself apparently ran and worked, which is a data point about the idea, not about the automation.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Choose the store by which invariant is expensive to violate. Ramp picked Postgres for entity resolution because a referential integrity failure produces a confident wrong answer with no downstream detection. Notion picked DynamoDB for profile reads because latency was the binding constraint. Both correct, and the question to ask of your own context layer is which failure you cannot recover from.
2. Resolve entities once and persist the result. Fuzzy matching is expensive, non-deterministic and, worst of all, produces inconsistent answers across consumers when recomputed. This is the same argument as materialising a feature transform rather than recomputing it per model.
3. Make attribute filtering a first-class part of retrieval queries, not a post-hoc rerank. In any multi-tenant or account-scoped corpus, pure semantic similarity will confidently return the right content from the wrong scope, and post-filtering after top-k silently destroys recall when the filter is selective.
4. Study turbopuffer's tiering as a template for inference-side memory work. Working set far smaller than total state, object storage for capacity, NVMe and RAM for the hot fraction, 95% cost reduction against holding everything in memory. The MoE analogue is exact: experts inactive on most tokens are cold data being paid for at hot prices, and the design question in both cases is how well you can predict the working set one step ahead.
5. Adopt hybrid offline-online learning for any router you build, not pure online bandits. OrcaRouter's LinUCB over lexical plus embedding features, bootstrapped offline and adapted online, hit 75.54% accuracy at $1.00 per 1,000 queries and second on RouterArena. Pure online exploration means serving worse answers to real users while learning; pure offline means no adaptation when the model lineup changes.
6. Use RouterArena as an external reference point. Publishing accuracy-per-dollar against a public leaderboard is now possible, and an internal router that has never been placed on that frontier has no evidence it beats a simple baseline.
7. Represent every tool call and model call as a durable activity. A completed model call is spent money, so resuming past it rather than replaying it is a direct saving on top of the reliability win, and config-scoped tool access plus native human-in-the-loop pause-resume come with the same runtime.
8. Expose your internal tool surface via MCP and treat what people build on it as telemetry. Every user-built automation is an unmet need plus a candidate solution written down. It is a far better discovery channel than interviews, and the reasoning traces from remote tool calls are analysable. Budget for the blast-radius problem this creates with per-agent tool scoping.
9. Build narrow and stitch later. Vaziri's answer for a two-person team was to solve one specific automation end to end, learn how it actually works, and generalise from working code. The alternative, a year designing the correct architecture, is the failure mode described independently in the Snowflake talk at the same conference.
