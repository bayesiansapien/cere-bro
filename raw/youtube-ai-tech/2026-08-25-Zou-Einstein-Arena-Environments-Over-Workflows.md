# Einstein Arena: Harnessing Collective Agent Intelligence for Open Science — James Zou, Together AI

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-25
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mMNkdYnIVC4

## TL;DR
James Zou, splitting time between Together AI and Stanford, argues that the field should stop designing agent workflows and start designing environments. A workflow specifies how an agent should work through steps, prompts, tools and instructions. An environment specifies only where it works, supplying incentives, infrastructure, guardrails and resources, then letting behaviour emerge. His thesis is that as agents get more capable, prescriptive workflows become a ceiling rather than a scaffold. The demonstration is EinsteinArena, an intentionally agent-native platform where a puzzle gates humans out and any agent in the world can enter, pick from curated problems that have both a real human research community and a deterministic verifier, submit solutions scored in real time on a public leaderboard, read and download other agents' solutions, and talk to each other in per-problem discussion forums. Launched around March 2026, within weeks agents had produced new best-known results on 11 problems. The headline case is the kissing number in 11 dimensions, stuck at 582 from 1980, improved to 592 in 2022, to 593 by DeepMind's AlphaEvolve, and then to 604 by collaborating agents on the arena, a jump no single frontier model could produce alone. The same environment with the verifier swapped for a compile-benchmark-test harness produced kernel optimisations exceeding 2x over prior state of the art, including on paged attention, now running in production at Together AI. A second environment, DSGym, exposes a problem worth internalising: 20 to 50% of tasks in widely used data science benchmarks can be solved without ever touching the underlying data.

## Key Takeaways
- The central abstraction: environment over workflow. Design the incentive and verification structure, not the procedure. Zou's framing of the progression is models, then agents and harnesses, then environments, and he claims the third stage is already here.
- EinsteinArena's design choices are the substance. Agent-native access via a skills document, a deliberate reverse-CAPTCHA so humans must prove they are agents to enter, open participation for any agent, and a real-time leaderboard.
- Problem selection has two hard criteria that are doing most of the work: an existing human research community that cares, and a deterministic verifier that scores solution quality unambiguously. Without the second, none of this functions. Without the first, the results would not matter.
- Solutions are visible and downloadable. Agents can read each other's submissions and build on them, which is what converts a leaderboard from a benchmark into a search process with shared state.
- Collaboration and competition run simultaneously and deliberately, mirroring how human research communities actually operate. The discussion forum is not decoration: Zou shows an exchange where one agent asks whether others have tried a particular SDP approach and receives negative results back, and credits the forum as important to the breakthrough.
- The kissing number result is the load-bearing evidence and it comes with an explicit claim about irreducibility: no single agent, not GPT-5.5 nor a Claude model, could solve it alone. Zou shows a lineage trace of solutions being taken, refined and re-optimised across agents.
- Why it matters beyond mathematics: denser non-overlapping sphere packings in high dimensions correspond to better codes, including error-correction codes for information transfer. The mathematical object has direct engineering consequence.
- Kernel optimisation reuses the identical environment with a different back end. Compile, benchmark, test, verify speed and correctness, feed results back as a live leaderboard. The environment is the reusable asset, the verifier is the swappable part.
- The persona result is the most immediately actionable finding in the talk. Agents were given differentiated roles and priors, one focused on profiling, one on memory consumption, one on precision and tensor computation. Diversity of prior, not just of sampling, produced better collective search.
- Speedups exceeded 2x on some production kernels including paged attention, initially for specific shapes and subsequently generalised across shapes and hardware types, and the resulting kernels are in production at Together AI. That is a real deployment claim, not a benchmark claim.
- DSGym is a unified evaluation and training environment for data science agents with a unified interface, code execution, and the ability for agents to spin up many Docker containers to test approaches in parallel.
- The shortcut finding is the most transferable negative result in the talk: across three popular data science benchmarks, 20 to 50% of tasks were solvable without using the underlying datasets at all, purely by reasoning or other shortcuts. Any reported score on those benchmarks is inflated by an unknown amount.
- DSGym's own tasks were built to avoid that: scientific analysis tasks curated from recently published papers with human expert review, and predictive modelling tasks from recent still-open Kaggle competitions with high-quality data and evaluation.
- Frontier models score under 50% on DSGym, so it is unsaturated. And the environment doubles as a training factory, generating execution-verified trajectories used to fine-tune small open models to best-in-class performance on data science tasks, small enough to run on a laptop.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The kernel result is the part worth studying closely, because it is a working instance of something you would otherwise have to build from scratch, and because its structure explains why it worked.

What Together AI built is a population-based search over kernel implementations where the fitness function is a real compile-and-benchmark harness rather than a learned reward model. That single choice is what makes the whole thing sound. A verifier that compiles, runs, times and checks correctness is exactly the setting where you can let agents explore aggressively, because the cost of a bad proposal is a failed benchmark rather than a silently wrong answer that propagates. This is the same reason kernel optimisation, competitive programming and formal mathematics keep producing agent successes while open-ended research tasks do not: the verifier exists and is cheap. Zou's environment thesis is really a claim about verifier availability dressed as a claim about design philosophy. Where you have a deterministic verifier you can build an environment and let search run. Where you do not, you are stuck writing workflows, and no amount of design taste substitutes.

The persona finding is the most practically useful thing in the talk and the least explained. Three agents with different priors, profiling-oriented, memory-oriented, precision-and-tensor-oriented, collaborating and competing, outperformed the undifferentiated version. The reason this is not merely ensembling is that kernel optimisation has a strongly multi-modal search landscape where the binding constraint changes by region: some shapes are memory-bandwidth bound, some are occupancy bound, some are limited by precision-driven instruction selection, and an agent whose prior points at the wrong constraint wastes its entire budget. Assigning distinct priors is effectively partitioning the search space by suspected bottleneck, which is what a good human performance engineer does implicitly and what a single agent with a generic prompt does badly because it regresses to the most commonly discussed optimisation in its training data. The transferable rule is that when your search landscape has multiple distinct bottleneck regimes, diversify the prior rather than the temperature.

Paged attention exceeding 2x deserves scrutiny and the talk supplies the caveat itself: initially for specific shapes. That is the honest framing and it is also where the real work is. A 2x on a specific shape in a paged attention kernel is entirely plausible, because shape-specialised kernels routinely beat general ones by that margin when the general kernel is carrying branch logic and conservative tiling for shapes it is not currently running. The interesting claim is the generalisation across shapes and hardware types, which is where shape-specialised wins usually evaporate into a dispatch table that is expensive to maintain. The fact that these are in production at Together AI is the strongest evidence, because production kernels have to survive shape distributions the benchmark did not contain.

The DSGym shortcut result generalises directly to inference and compression benchmarking and is the finding to actually change behaviour over. Twenty to fifty percent of tasks solvable without the data means the benchmark was measuring prior knowledge and pattern completion rather than the capability it claimed. The exact analogue in your own work: a quantisation or pruning evaluation where a large fraction of the eval set is answerable from the prompt alone, or from memorised training data, so a heavily compressed model scores well because the surviving capacity is sufficient for retrieval-from-memory even though the reasoning capacity it was supposed to measure is gone. The ablation Zou ran is the one to steal. Run your eval with the input degraded or removed and see what score remains. Whatever remains is your floor, and any improvement you report needs to be measured against that floor rather than against zero.

The execution-verified trajectory pipeline is the other directly reusable piece. An environment with a real verifier is simultaneously an evaluation harness and a data generator, because every successful trajectory it produces is a verified training example with no human labelling and no reward-model approximation. Getting small open models to best-in-class on a task family by fine-tuning on execution-verified trajectories from your own environment is a much cheaper path to a specialised model than distilling from a frontier model, and it produces artefacts you can inspect. The constraint is the same one as everywhere else in this talk: it only works where the verifier is real.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The core results check out, and the record is slightly messier and more interesting than the talk lets on. The published work is arXiv 2606.10402, "Harnessing the Collective Intelligence of AI Agents in the Wild for New Discoveries," from Stanford and Together AI, and EinsteinArena is live and describes itself as an agent-native platform for open distributed research with per-problem verifiers, public leaderboards and discussion forums, focused on mathematical tasks where progress is unambiguously measurable. The state-of-the-art count has moved: as of May 2026 the platform reported 12 new results better than any previous human or AI solution, against the 11 Zou cites in this talk.

The kissing number progression is real but the talk compresses a step. The 592 lower bound in 11 dimensions was established by Mikhail Ganzhinov, a doctoral candidate at Aalto University, and the framing in Aalto's own coverage is pointedly that a human researcher bested AI in the process. AlphaEvolve then raised it from 592 to 593. On EinsteinArena, agents first improved it to 594 and subsequently built on that result to reach 604. Zou goes straight from 593 to 604 and omits the 594 intermediate, which actually undersells his own argument, since the two-stage improvement is stronger evidence for the cumulative-collaboration mechanism than a single leap would be. His "with AlphaZero" in the transcript is a garbled reference to AlphaEvolve.

That correction matters for how much weight to put on the collaboration claim. Zou asserts that no single frontier model can solve the problem alone. That is credible for a construction problem in 11 dimensions requiring iterated refinement of a specific numerical configuration, and the published lineage trace is the evidence. But the honest comparison is not agents-versus-single-model, it is agents-with-shared-state-and-many-attempts versus a single model with one attempt, and the talk does not report a compute-matched baseline. Whether the gain comes from collaboration specifically or from sheer sampling volume with a shared solution pool is not established here, and it is the question a reviewer would ask first. The Ganzhinov result is a useful reminder that the human-versus-AI framing on this problem has gone both directions inside eighteen months.

DSGym is published as arXiv 2601.16344, presented at ICML 2026, with code on GitHub, and its actual scope is substantially larger than the talk's "over a dozen different tasks." It integrates established benchmarks including MLEBench and QRData and adds two original datasets: DSBio, 90 bioinformatics tasks drawn from academic literature, and DSPredict, 92 Kaggle competitions spanning time series, computer vision, molecular property prediction and single-cell perturbation. The shortcut-solvability filtering Zou describes is a documented part of the pipeline, applied as an explicit quality filter over existing benchmarks rather than as a one-off observation, which makes the 20 to 50% figure a systematic measurement rather than an anecdote. The execution-verified trajectory synthesis for fine-tuning is also documented as a first-class feature.

One structural caution about the environment thesis itself. Every success reported here sits in a domain with a cheap deterministic verifier: sphere packing has a checkable non-overlap condition, kernels have a compiler and a stopwatch, Kaggle tasks have held-out scoring. Zou's own problem-curation criterion requires a deterministic verifier as a precondition for admission to the arena. So the talk demonstrates that environments beat workflows in verifiable domains, and says nothing about the domains where most scientific work happens and where the absence of a verifier is the actual bottleneck. That is a real result, not a small one, but the framing as a general progression from workflows to environments is broader than the evidence.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Run the shortcut ablation on your own evaluation suites this week. Take every benchmark you use to justify a pruning, quantisation or routing decision and score it with the critical input removed or corrupted. Whatever fraction remains solvable is your floor, and any compression result you have reported without subtracting that floor is overstated by an unknown amount. Zou found 20 to 50% on published data science benchmarks, and there is no reason to expect compression evals are cleaner.
2. Build the kernel arena for your own hardware, because the ingredients are the ones you already have. You have a compiler, a benchmark harness, and a correctness check, which is a complete deterministic verifier. Put multiple agents behind it with a shared solution pool and a live leaderboard, and let them read each other's submissions. This is the highest expected-value item in the talk for your work and it does not require the arena infrastructure, only the verifier and shared state.
3. Diversify agent priors by suspected bottleneck rather than sampling temperature. For kernel work, spawn one agent primed on profiling and occupancy, one on memory bandwidth and tiling, one on precision and tensor-core instruction selection, and let them compete. The mechanism is search-space partitioning by bottleneck regime, which is why it beats N identical agents, and it maps cleanly onto the regimes you already reason about when hand-tuning.
4. Treat any verified environment you build as a training data generator, not just an evaluator. Execution-verified trajectories from your own kernel or routing harness are labelled data with no annotation cost and no reward-model error, and Zou's result is that fine-tuning small open models on such trajectories reaches best-in-class on the task family. For routing specifically, every logged decision with a measured downstream outcome is a verified trajectory you are probably discarding.
5. Apply the environment-versus-workflow test as a triage heuristic on your own agent projects. Ask whether a cheap deterministic verifier exists. If yes, stop writing the workflow and build the environment, because the workflow is capping you. If no, the environment framing does not apply and Zou's evidence does not transfer, since every result in this talk comes from a verifiable domain.
6. Do not repeat the "no single model can do this" claim without the caveat. The published work does not report a compute-matched single-agent baseline, so the gain is not cleanly attributable to collaboration rather than to sampling volume with shared state. Also note the 594 intermediate step the talk omits, and that the 592 bound came from a human researcher who beat AlphaEvolve to it.
7. Read the two papers rather than relying on the talk, since both understate themselves in the retelling. arXiv 2606.10402 for EinsteinArena and arXiv 2601.16344 for DSGym, with DSGym code on GitHub. DSGym's real scope, 90 bioinformatics tasks plus 92 Kaggle competitions with documented shortcut filtering, is considerably more useful than the dozen tasks the talk mentions.
