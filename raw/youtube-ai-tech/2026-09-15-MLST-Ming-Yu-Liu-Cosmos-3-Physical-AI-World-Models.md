# How Physical AI Learns Across Language, Video and Action (Ming-Yu Liu, NVIDIA)

**Channel:** Machine Learning Street Talk
**Published:** 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6tLBApQN-g

## TL;DR
Ming-Yu Liu leads the Cosmos research at NVIDIA, and this is a walkthrough of Cosmos 3's architecture and the case for neural simulators. The architecture is two towers: an autoregressive vision-language "reason tower," and a bidirectional diffusion generation tower initialised from the reason tower's weights, generating video, audio and action jointly. Action is a first-class modality, so the model's next observation can be caused by its own output. The most transferable argument in the episode has nothing to do with robotics: Liu's case for neural simulation rests on *rank preservation*, not fidelity. You do not need the simulator to be accurate, you need it to order candidate policies the same way the real world does. That reframing collapses an expensive evaluation problem into a cheap one, and it applies directly to checkpoint selection in any compression or optimization pipeline. Independent work (RoboWorld) has since measured that correlation at ρ=0.970.

## Key Takeaways
- **Cosmos 3 is built on a mixture-of-transformers architecture**, launched 1 June 2026 at GTC Taipei, fully open: weights and datasets on Hugging Face, code and post-training recipes on GitHub.
- **Two towers with different attention regimes.** Reason tower: autoregressive, one token at a time, vision encoder bolted to an LLM. Generation tower: bidirectional diffusion, every token attends to every other token within the generated chunk, so the chunk is internally coherent.
- **The generator is initialised from the VLM's weights.** The generative model inherits the understanding model's representations rather than learning them again.
- **Action is a first-class modality, not a conditioning signal.** The model's action output causally produces its own next observation, which is what makes closed-loop simulation possible at all.
- **Heterogeneous sampling rates are handled by a shared temporal position embedding.** Video frame rates, audio Hz and action frequencies all get normalised onto one scale so tokens know which other tokens are contemporaneous and what the relative time distances are. Liu calls this the critical piece that makes the omnimodel work.
- **Forward dynamics, inverse dynamics and policy are trained jointly under a shared capacity constraint.** All three are different slices of the same observation-action correlation. The information bottleneck forces sharing, and the paper reports measured synergy.
- **Cross-embodiment transfer works through visual-action pattern similarity, not action-space alignment.** Abundant egocentric human video transfers to scarce robot data because a humanoid manipulating an object looks like a human doing it, even when the action spaces do not correspond.
- **Liu concedes the model cannot know when transfer is harmful.** Asked directly, his answer is "the model doesn't really know." That is an honest admission of an open failure mode.
- **Three sizes: Super (frontier fidelity), Nano (mid, easy to post-train), Edge (4B, shipped 20 July 2026).** Edge targets Jetson Thor, Orin and DGX Spark.
- **Edge exists to eliminate the datacenter round trip.** Network availability is not guaranteed in deployment and some tasks have hard deadlines.
- **He sketches an escalation architecture: edge model handles what must finish now, "call for help" to a powerful model for hard tasks.** This is cascade routing with a physical real-time floor.
- **Neural simulators will be reward-hacked and he says so.** His position is that any simulator with exploitable patterns will be exploited, that this is not unique to neural ones, and that regularisation methods will be found. He explicitly does not claim to know how.
- **Manipulation is the hard case, navigation is largely solved.** Navigation avoids contact. Manipulation requires it, and contact brings occlusion and deformation.
- **Passive verification comes before training data.** His staged view: use world models to *rank* checkpoints now, use them to *generate training data* later, because ranking tolerates much lower fidelity.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The two-tower design is worth reading as an architectural answer to a mismatch problem rather than as a robotics detail. Understanding wants causal autoregression, because language is sequential and the reasoning has to condition on what came before. Generation of a video-audio-action chunk wants bidirectional attention, because coherence within the chunk requires every part to see every other part. Those are incompatible attention masks in one stack. The usual resolutions are to pick one and accept the cost, or to run two separate models and lose representation sharing. Cosmos does neither: it keeps both regimes as separate towers inside one model, and it seeds the bidirectional tower from the autoregressive tower's weights so the representation transfers even though the attention pattern does not.

Framed as a mixture-of-transformers, this is a different axis of sparsity from the MoE work you already know. Standard MoE routes at the FFN level within a layer, on a per-token basis, with a learned router. Mixture-of-transformers routes whole transformer stacks by *modality and task*, with a routing decision that is structural rather than learned. The consequence for serving is significant: expert selection is known before the forward pass, not discovered during it. No load-balancing loss, no capacity-factor tuning, no all-to-all across experts, no dropped tokens. You know from the request type which tower runs. The cost is coarser granularity and no ability to specialise below the modality boundary. That tradeoff, structural routing with predictable memory movement versus learned routing with finer specialisation, is the live question in multimodal serving and this is a clean data point on the structural side.

The temporal position embedding deserves more attention than its thirty seconds. Every multimodal system faces the same problem: modalities arrive at incompatible rates and the model needs to know what was simultaneous. The naive fixes are resampling everything to a common rate, which either discards high-frequency information or pads low-frequency streams, or per-modality embeddings, which leaves the model with no cross-modal notion of "at the same time." Normalising all signals onto a shared temporal coordinate and embedding position in that coordinate preserves native rates while making both simultaneity and relative distance directly readable from the embedding. The audio-video alignment case is the obvious one. The interesting one is action, where control frequencies are high and video is slow, and the model must associate a burst of control signal with one visual transition.

The joint training of forward dynamics, inverse dynamics and policy under a shared bottleneck is the multi-task story told properly. His argument: all three are queries against one underlying object, the observation-action correlation. Forward dynamics asks what follows given state and action. Inverse dynamics asks what action produced this transition. Policy asks what action achieves the goal. Given enough capacity a model could learn three separate solutions. Constrain capacity and it is forced to find the shared structure, and the shared structure is the thing that generalises. This is the same logic as multi-task distillation, where the constraint is the mechanism rather than an unfortunate limitation, and it argues for deliberately under-provisioning capacity on multi-task objectives rather than scaling until each task is comfortable.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The product facts check out. Cosmos 3 launched 1 June 2026 at GTC Taipei as an open physical AI foundation model built on a mixture-of-transformers architecture, described by NVIDIA as the first fully open omnimodel with native vision reasoning and multimodal generation across text, image, video, ambient sound and action. Weights and some training data are on Hugging Face, code and post-training recipes on GitHub, deployment via NIM microservices. The tiered family was completed on 20 July 2026 with Cosmos 3 Edge at 4 billion parameters for memory-efficient high-throughput edge inference. The release includes task-specialised checkpoints including Cosmos3-Nano-Policy-DROID, a vision-language robot policy model for manipulation, which corroborates his claim of state-of-the-art pick-and-place results from post-training on DROID data. NVIDIA also launched a Cosmos Coalition with Agile Robots, Black Forest Labs, Generalist, LTX, Runway and Skild AI.

The rank-preservation argument, which is the intellectual core of the episode, has independent quantitative support that Liu does not cite and which is stronger than his own framing. RoboWorld (arXiv 2607.01060) built neural simulators specifically for generalist robot policy evaluation and reports Pearson ρ=0.970 against the RoboArena leaderboard across eight evaluated policies, with the simulator's ranking closely matching real-world ordering. The cost figure is the one to remember: replicating the RoboArena benchmark across eight policies took 100 H100 GPU hours. Set that against fleets of human drivers or physical robot test rigs and the economics are not close. Separately, work on interactive world simulators (arXiv 2603.08546) found strong simulated-to-real correlation across diverse tasks and, more aggressively than Liu's staged position, found that policies trained on world-model-generated data performed comparably to policies trained on the same volume of real data. That result suggests his "verification first, training data later" sequencing may already be conservative.

Where the episode is weakest is on the reward-hacking question, and to his credit Liu does not pretend otherwise. His answer is essentially that all simulators get exploited, neural ones will too, and people will figure out regularisation. That is probably true as a historical generalisation and it is not an answer. It also understates a specific asymmetry: a physics engine's exploits are bugs in hand-written code, findable, fixable and typically visibly unphysical. A learned simulator's exploits are regions where the model's distribution diverges from reality, and those are invisible by construction, because if you knew where the model was wrong you would have fixed it. The policy is optimising directly against the simulator's error surface. This matters more for the training-data use case than the verification use case, which is a good reason to take his staged sequencing seriously even if the correlation results say you could move faster.

The cross-embodiment transfer story also has a gap he names but does not resolve. He argues transfer works because visual-action patterns are similar across embodiments even when action spaces are not, and when asked whether the model knows when *not* to transfer, says plainly that it does not. That is the failure mode with safety consequences: a humanoid inheriting a human manipulation prior for a motion that is safe at human joint torque limits and destructive at the robot's. Nothing in the described architecture detects that the prior is inapplicable. In a system aimed at homes with children and pets, which is the deployment scenario he invokes himself, undetected negative transfer is the interesting risk, not simulator exploitation.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
The rank-preservation reframing is the thing to steal, and it transfers cleanly out of robotics into compression work. Liu's argument is that policy evaluation does not require an accurate simulator, only one that orders candidates correctly, because the purpose of the evaluation is to select. The same holds for every checkpoint-selection problem you run. When you sweep quantization configurations, pruning ratios or distillation recipes, you are producing many candidates and needing an ordering, not a calibrated absolute number. The expensive full evaluation exists to produce a number you will report once, at the end, on the winner. Build a cheap proxy evaluator, validate it once against the expensive one by measuring rank correlation across a batch of candidates you have already fully evaluated, and if Spearman or Pearson comes back high, run the sweep on the proxy and reserve the full eval for the final two or three. RoboWorld's ρ=0.970 at 100 H100-hours for eight policies is the shape of the win. The step people skip is the validation: measure the correlation before trusting it, and re-measure when the candidate distribution shifts, because rank preservation is a property of the candidate set and not of the proxy alone.

The second transferable idea is the escalation architecture with a hard deadline, which is a harder and more interesting routing problem than the one LLM serving usually poses. In text routing, escalating to a larger model costs latency and money. In Liu's setup, some tasks must complete on the edge device because the deadline is physical and the network may not exist. That means the router cannot be a pure quality-versus-cost optimiser, it needs a feasibility check: is there time to escalate at all, given the deadline and the current network state. If you ever work on routing under a latency SLO rather than a latency preference, this is the structure. The routing decision becomes two-stage, first "can I escalate," then "should I," and the fallback path must be genuinely capable rather than a degraded stub, which is exactly why the 4B Edge model is a real model rather than a distilled rump.

Third, concretely worth an afternoon: Cosmos 3 is fully open, weights and post-training recipes both, and Edge is 4B. If you want a current, well-documented mixture-of-transformers implementation to read rather than a paper description of one, this is the most accessible artefact available, and the structural-versus-learned routing comparison against your MoE work is a direct read of the code. Liu also notes the repo ships agent skills for picking up the API, and says he personally watches the issue tracker.

Fourth, the capacity-constraint result is a design prior worth adopting: when training one model on several tasks that are genuinely views of the same underlying structure, under-provision capacity deliberately. Slack lets the model learn separate solutions. The bottleneck is what forces it to find the shared one, and the shared one is what transfers.
