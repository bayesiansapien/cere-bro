# "AI IS A MASSIVE SCAM!"

**Channel:** The Diary Of A CEO
**Published:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJBp2WPSZR0

## TL;DR
A short clip in which Ed Zitron, a 16-year tech industry veteran and the industry's most prominent AI-economics critic, argues that generative AI is fundamentally a con: sold as magic, delivered as expensive and unreliable cloud software. Steven Bartlett runs him through a myth list and Zitron rejects three claims outright. That AI is producing enormous economic growth (his counter: OpenAI lost $20.9 billion last year and no operator will state a path to profitability). That AI will replace all human jobs (his counter: no economic data supports it). And that the US must spend trillions to beat China (his counter: the race framing is manufactured, and the "wrong hands" concern is moot because the models are already in the hands of Zuckerberg, Altman, and Amodei). He calls it the largest non-consensual push of technology in history.

## Key Takeaways
- **The financial claim is the load-bearing one and it is checkable.** OpenAI's 2025 loss figure of $20.9 billion is not rhetoric, it is a number that either survives scrutiny or does not. Zitron's entire argument stands or falls on unit economics, which makes it unusually falsifiable for a critique of this genre.
- **The absence of a stated profitability path is his sharpest observation.** His point is not that these companies are unprofitable, which is normal for growth-stage technology, but that none of them will assert they are on a path to profitability, "because they can't." That is a specific, testable claim about disclosure rather than about performance.
- **On job displacement he demands economic data, not anecdote.** This is the correct standard. Aggregate labour market statistics are the arbiter, not vendor case studies or survey sentiment.
- **The China race argument is reframed as a spending justification rather than a strategic reality.** His question, what does the race actually do for us, is worth taking seriously as a challenge to the capex narrative even if one disagrees with the conclusion.
- **The "wrong hands" reversal is rhetorically effective and substantively narrow.** Concentration of frontier capability in a handful of private hands is a real governance problem. It is not an argument that the technology does not work, and Zitron conflates the two.
- **He is explicit that he likes technology,** which distinguishes this from generic tech skepticism. His stated objection is to being misled, not to the field.
- **Bartlett notes Zitron is the first guest with this position,** which is a fair signal about how unrepresentative the view is among the show's guest pool, and also a signal about the guest pool.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
This is the clip in this whole corpus most directly relevant to Amit's job, and the reason is not obvious from the framing.

Strip away the "con" language and Zitron's thesis reduces to a single technical claim: inference cost per unit of delivered value exceeds the price the market will pay, and the gap is not closing fast enough to be outrun by growth. That is not a critique of AI, it is an argument that inference optimization is the highest-leverage engineering problem in the industry. Every one of Amit's specialisations is a direct attack on the denominator of Zitron's argument. Quantization reduces memory bandwidth per token. Pruning and distillation reduce FLOPs per token. Routing reduces the fraction of queries that touch a frontier model at all. MoE architectures reduce active parameters per forward pass. If Zitron is right that the economics are broken, then the work Amit does is the thing that fixes it, and the value of that work is a function of exactly how broken the economics are.

The most important detail he does not mention in this clip but which underlies it: the subscription model's exposure. A flat monthly fee against unbounded token consumption is a short volatility position on user behaviour. The worst users are the most engaged users, and the more capable the model becomes at agentic multi-turn work, the more tokens a single task consumes. Reasoning models made this dramatically worse, because chain-of-thought spends output tokens that the user never sees and cannot be charged for separately under a subscription. That is a structural mismatch between pricing and cost that no amount of scale fixes, and it is why routing matters commercially and not just academically: the only way a flat-fee product survives is if the marginal query gets served by the cheapest model that can satisfy it.

Where the argument is weakest technically: Zitron treats current unit economics as the permanent state. Cost per token at fixed capability has fallen by roughly an order of magnitude per year through a combination of hardware, quantization, serving-stack improvements (continuous batching, paged attention, speculative decoding), and architectural efficiency. A business losing money at today's cost curve is not necessarily losing money at next year's. The honest version of his critique is a race condition: does cost decline faster than usage intensity grows. That is an empirical question with a real answer, and neither side of this debate is measuring it properly.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The numbers check out, and the underlying reporting is more damaging than the clip conveys.

Zitron's own reporting has OpenAI losing $20.9 billion on $13.07 billion of 2025 revenue, with total spending hitting $34 billion in 2025 and losses increasing nearly eightfold year over year. Cost of revenue, the line that captures actually serving model outputs, rose from $2.65 billion in 2024 to $7.5 billion in 2025. OpenAI spent $5.02 billion on inference with Microsoft Azure in the first half of 2025 alone. Separate reporting puts cumulative losses at $38.53 billion, which Zitron describes as far higher than most believed and mounting at a dramatic rate.

The most technically interesting corroboration comes from SemiAnalysis on subscription unit economics: it is possible to burn hundreds of dollars of tokens on a $20-a-month subscription and thousands on a $200-a-month subscription. That is the mechanism behind the loss figures and it is a pricing-architecture failure, not a technology failure. It also means the losses are concentrated in a heavy-usage tail, which is precisely the population that routing and caching optimizations target.

Two caveats on the framing. First, "lost $20.9 billion" and "the technology is a con" are different claims, and the clip slides between them. Amazon lost money for years, Uber lost tens of billions, and neither was a con. Sustained losses during infrastructure buildout are evidence about business models, not about product utility. Second, Zitron's position on Apple is instructive about his priors: he has argued that Apple's roughly $14 billion AI spend is a bet on the bubble collapsing and that Apple will "watch everything burn." That is a directional macro call, and it means his AI commentary should be read as a thesis with a position behind it rather than as neutral analysis. He may well be right, but he is not disinterested.

Third, on the jobs claim, his standard is correct and his conclusion was accurate as of this recording. Aggregate labour-market data has not shown broad AI-driven displacement. That is a statement about a lagging indicator, though, and absence of effect in aggregate statistics three years into a deployment cycle is weak evidence about the next five.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Treat Zitron's cost-of-revenue numbers as the market-size estimate for inference optimization work.** OpenAI's cost of revenue going from $2.65 billion to $7.5 billion in one year is the addressable problem. Any technique that cuts serving cost 30% at constant quality is worth billions annually at one company. Amit should use these figures explicitly when justifying compression and routing projects to leadership, because they convert an engineering argument into a financial one.
- **Build the race-condition analysis nobody in this debate has done.** Plot cost per token at fixed capability against tokens consumed per completed task, both over time. If the second curve is rising faster than the first is falling, Zitron wins on the economics regardless of how good the models get. That single chart is a genuinely valuable internal artefact and a plausible publication.
- **Attack the subscription tail specifically.** The losses are concentrated in heavy users, which makes per-user routing policy the highest-value lever: cheap model by default, escalate on measured difficulty, cache aggressively across a user's session. Framing routing work as margin defence on the loss-making decile is a much stronger business case than framing it as average cost reduction.
- **Note that reasoning models are the pricing problem's accelerant.** Output tokens spent on hidden chain-of-thought are pure cost with no user-visible deliverable to price against. Any work on reducing reasoning-token count at fixed accuracy (adaptive thinking budgets, early exit, distilling reasoning traces into shorter ones) sits directly on the bleeding edge of this economics problem.
- **Hold both claims separately.** "The unit economics are currently broken" is well evidenced and should inform where Amit spends his research time. "The technology is a con" does not follow from it, and conflating them is the flaw in the argument, not a reason to dismiss the data.
