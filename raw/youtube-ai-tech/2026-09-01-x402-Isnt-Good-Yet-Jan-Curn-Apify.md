# x402 isn't good (yet) | Jan Curn, Apify

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h6mi88VrPtQ

## TL;DR
The most technically honest talk in the agentic-commerce track, deliberately modelled on David Cramer's "MCP isn't good yet" from the prior year, and made credible by the fact that Curn shipped an x402 integration two days earlier that took the number of tools on the agentic market from about 2,000 to about 22,000. His three concrete complaints are real protocol defects, not gripes. The spec mandates HTTP 402 as the first response while MCP's auth flow mandates 401, and both enforce it, so companies end up standing up duplicate API hosts per payment protocol. There is a double-spend window between signature and on-chain settlement, so a client can sign the same funds a thousand times, and it is unfixed in both the `exact` and the newer `upto` schemes. And metered billing (Apify's actors run from seconds to hours) had no native scheme for over a year, forcing a charge-then-refund workaround with two blockchain transactions. His workaround is `agi.apify.com`, a single markdown page instructing agents how to buy a prepaid Apify token, which sidesteps the whole mess.

## Key Takeaways
- **The double-spend window is the most serious finding and it is a genuine protocol flaw.** Between the client sending a signature and the facilitator settling on-chain, nothing prevents the same wallet and same funds being signed into a thousand parallel requests. For a zero-marginal-cost API you can do the work before settling and absorb the risk. For non-trivial work, or work where you pay an external service, you complete the job and discover the money is gone. His mitigation is to do the work only after settlement, which requires you never to fail after taking payment.
- **The 402-versus-401 collision is a real interoperability defect.** x402's specification requires 402 Payment Required as the server's first response, with no alternative. MCP's auth flow requires 401. You cannot return two status codes. The observed industry response is to stand up dedicated hosts (`x402.example.com`, `mcp.example.com`, `mpp.example.com`), which Curn correctly calls an anti-pattern with a good analogy: imagine twenty different Amazons, one per card network. His proposed fix is a `Payment-Required` header rather than a status code, and he notes that using 402 is great marketing and bad engineering.
- **The metered-billing gap took over a year to address and then did not fix the underlying problem.** x402 launched May 2025 with only the `exact` scheme, a fixed fee per call. Coinbase announced `upto` in December 2025 to handle variable usage, and shipped it roughly half a year later. `upto` lets a client authorise a $5 maximum against which the server charges actual usage, which solves variable pricing and leaves double-spend untouched.
- **Their interim workaround and its costs.** Charge a fixed amount via `exact`, do the work, refund the unspent remainder. It works, and it means two blockchain transactions, two settlement delays, extra fees, and a requirement that the client trust the server to actually refund.
- **Batch settlement is the promising new scheme and Curn describes it accurately.** Deposit once into on-chain escrow, receive a cryptographic voucher, sign off-chain micropayments against that voucher per request, and periodically settle the accumulated batch on-chain before releasing the escrow remainder. They had not implemented it yet at the time of the talk.
- **The standards proliferation list is the most useful single artefact in the talk.** L402, Mastercard Agent Pay, x402 (Coinbase), KYA Pay (Skyfire with Visa), AP2 (Google), ACP (OpenAI and Stripe), Tab (Visa), UCP (Google and Shopify), ACTP (Alipay), MPP (Stripe and Tempo), AMP (Alipay), one from UnionPay, APP (OKX), and Agent Pay for Machines (Mastercard). Every payments incumbent wants a share of the future transaction volume.
- **His crypto argument is unusually well constructed, and he explicitly disclaims being a crypto holder.** Two reasons. First, card, PayPal, and ACH rails are too expensive for microtransactions. Second, and more interesting, buyer disputes: in human commerce there are trust signals and fraud tooling, but with an agent you genuinely do not know who you are transacting with, so chargebacks are unworkable and the transaction must be one-way and final. Third, a genuinely decentralised chain can be a public standard that no single company controls, unlike a Visa or Mastercard rail whose owner can extract fees from a dominant position.
- **Apify context:** roughly 45,000 tools ("actors") spanning social media and e-commerce extraction, travel, search, maps, and increasingly agentic automation, with the community earning over $1 million a month in payouts.
- **His volume estimate is the honest one:** roughly $1 million a month of transaction volume, which he says plainly is nothing relative to the real economy. He also reports x402 running roughly 20 times larger than Stripe's MPP by both transaction count and volume.
- **His thesis for when this inflects is the sharpest prediction at the conference.** Once token subsidies end and agents actually pay for the tokens they consume, the build-versus-buy calculation shifts toward buying external services, and that is when agentic payments explode.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The double-spend analysis is worth working through carefully because it is a classic distributed-systems problem in a new costume, and Curn's framing of the mitigation is exactly right.

The vulnerability window exists because verification and settlement are separated in time. The facilitator can verify that a signature is valid and that the wallet currently holds sufficient funds, but "currently holds" is a read that goes stale the instant it returns. A thousand concurrent requests each pass verification against the same balance. This is a check-then-act race, structurally identical to the TOCTOU bugs that plague filesystem and permission code, and the standard fixes apply: hold a lock (escrow), or make the operation atomic (settle before acting), or make it idempotent with a nonce the verifier tracks. Batch settlement is the escrow answer, and it is the correct one, because escrowed funds are debited from a balance the client can no longer double-commit.

The economic asymmetry Curn identifies is the part worth generalising. Whether the window matters depends entirely on the marginal cost of the work. At zero marginal cost you can serve optimistically and eat the occasional loss. At meaningful marginal cost, especially when you pay an upstream provider, optimistic service turns a race condition into a direct cash loss. This maps precisely onto Amit's domain: an inference provider serving a metered endpoint has substantial marginal cost per request (GPU seconds, and possibly upstream API cost), so it sits firmly in the pay-first category. Any agentic-payment integration for inference must settle before generating, not after, and that decision has a latency cost that has to be budgeted.

The 402-versus-401 conflict is a lesson in protocol design that Curn states well: reusing a status code for marketing value creates a namespace collision, because HTTP status codes are a single-valued field and headers are not. The general principle is that semantics belonging to an orthogonal concern belong in an extensible field, not in a single-valued one. This is the same reason content negotiation lives in headers rather than in the method.

The `agi.apify.com` solution is the most pragmatic engineering in the talk and it is a pattern worth naming. Rather than integrate N payment protocols into a stable public API that tens of thousands of customers depend on, expose one agent-facing endpoint that sells a prepaid token in whatever protocol, then let the agent use the existing API and MCP surface normally. This decouples payment-protocol churn from API stability, which matters enormously when the protocol landscape has fourteen contenders and the `upto` scheme took seven months to ship. It also inverts the usual integration direction: instead of making the API payment-aware, make payment a separate one-time purchase of an existing credential. For Amit, the analogue is obvious: sell prepaid inference credits through whatever agentic rail is current, and keep the inference API itself protocol-agnostic. Curn's point that the markdown page can iterate freely because agents pick up new versions, while an API must stay backwards compatible forever, is the underrated half of the argument.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Curn's technical claims check out against the specification, which is unusual for a conference talk making critical claims.

The x402 specification now documents three payment schemes and they are exactly as he describes. `exact` requires the client to effect an on-chain payment of a precise amount to a specified recipient. `upto` authorises a maximum per request with the seller settling actual usage up to that cap. `batch-settlement` is for high-throughput payments where many requests are authorised individually and redeemed later in batches: the buyer deposits into on-chain escrow once, signs off-chain cumulative vouchers per request, and the seller verifies vouchers quickly and redeems them on-chain in batches. The `upto` timeline is corroborated: announced December 2025, shipped roughly mid-2026, and it did not resolve double-spending, since it caps the amount without preventing the same wallet being reused across parallel transactions. The specification and reference implementation live under the x402-foundation GitHub organisation.

Note that batch settlement is essentially the same architecture Circle independently built as nanopayments (off-chain signed authorisations against a funded balance with batched on-chain settlement) and that Circle uses EIP-3009 signatures for. Two independent teams converged on escrow-plus-off-chain-vouchers, which is strong evidence it is the right design and that the original `exact` scheme was simply under-specified for the workload.

On scale, Curn's roughly $1 million a month is broadly consistent with the public picture and possibly generous. Cumulative x402 volume is around $50 million over roughly 165 to 170 million transactions with an average transaction value near $0.13, and adoption tracking suggests roughly half of 2026's settled transactions are gamified rather than genuine commerce, putting real economic volume near $28,000 a day. His characterisation, "this is nothing," is the accurate one, and it is notably more honest than the framing in the AWS, Circle, and Edge & Node talks at the same event.

His claim that his integration 10x'd the agentic market is the kind of thing worth flagging as self-reported and mechanically true but economically misleading. Going from about 2,000 to about 22,000 listed tools multiplies supply, not demand. A marketplace with 22,000 paywalled endpoints and $28,000 a day of real transaction volume is supply-constrained in no sense whatsoever.

Two things the talk gets slightly wrong or overstates. He says 402 was introduced "almost 30 years ago" in the original HTTP specification, which is right in spirit (402 has been reserved since HTTP/1.1 in 1997) though it was reserved rather than defined for use. And his decentralisation argument, that a public chain avoids a Visa or Mastercard extracting fees from a dominant position, is weakened by the fact that over 90% of x402 activity runs on Base, which is Coinbase's own Layer 2, with Coinbase as the dominant facilitator. The protocol is under Linux Foundation governance, and the practical concentration is high, so the decentralisation benefit is currently aspirational rather than realised.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **If Amit ever meters an inference endpoint, settle before generating, never after.** GPU seconds are real marginal cost, so the double-spend window converts directly into cash loss. Budget the settlement latency (roughly 200 to 400 milliseconds) into the request path and treat it as non-negotiable. This is the single most consequential engineering conclusion in the talk.
- **Copy the prepaid-token decoupling pattern rather than integrating payment protocols into a stable API.** One agent-facing endpoint that sells credits in whatever rail is current, plus an unchanged protocol-agnostic service API. With fourteen competing standards and a seven-month lead time on a single scheme, this is the only sane integration strategy, and it lets the payment surface iterate at agent speed while the API stays stable.
- **Use `batch-settlement` rather than `exact` or `upto` for anything metered.** Escrow plus off-chain vouchers is the design that actually closes the double-spend window, and two independent teams (x402 and Circle) converged on it. The other two schemes are under-specified for variable-cost work.
- **Recognise check-then-act races when they appear in payment or quota systems.** Verification against a mutable balance is stale the moment it returns. The fixes are escrow, atomic settle-then-act, or nonce-tracked idempotency. This applies directly to rate limiting and quota enforcement in any multi-tenant serving system Amit builds.
- **Take Curn's inflection prediction seriously as a planning assumption.** When token subsidies end and agents pay real prices for tokens, build-versus-buy shifts toward buying external services, because the cost of an agent generating a capability itself starts to exceed the cost of paying for it. That is a specific, falsifiable prediction about when this market inflects, and it is the most useful forward-looking statement at the conference.
- **Cite Curn's volume figure rather than the vendors' when discussing this space.** Roughly $1 million a month, or approximately $28,000 a day of genuinely economic volume, against fourteen competing standards backed by every major payments company. The infrastructure is real and the market is not there yet, and Curn is the only speaker who says so plainly.
- **Note the supply-versus-demand asymmetry as a general marketplace lesson.** 10x-ing listed tools does not 10x transactions. When evaluating any marketplace claim, ask whether the growth metric measures supply or demand, because supply is cheap to multiply and demand is not.
