# The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Separating the Task from the Model

**Channel:** AI Engineer  
**Published:** 2026-07-23  
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GgLQ02aO-hs  

## TL;DR
Maxime Rivest and Isaac Miller make the case that a repeated AI task should be a function: named, with defined inputs and outputs, so the implementation inside is free to change. Fix that contract and every new technique (agents, tools, loop engineering, recursive language models) becomes a one-line experiment instead of a rewrite. Their sharpest claim is that a complete task specification needs three languages, not one: natural-language instructions for what should happen, code for what must happen, and examples for what good looks like. Once you have all three, the goal is fully specified and can be optimized automatically. The headline number is Shopify cutting an AI workload's cost by 550x, achieved not by cleverness but by keeping the contract fixed and letting search find the cheapest model that still passed.

## Key Takeaways
- **Specs, code, and evals map onto three genuinely different expressive needs.** Instructions are efficient for what you can articulate (his analogy: explaining a board game to a friend versus making them learn it from self-play like AlphaZero). Code is the only reliable way to enforce hard constraints. Examples are the only way to transmit the latent long tail, which is why internships exist. His father could not explain in words or in code how he knew a tree was a maple.
- **The constraint layer is the underrated one.** His invoice example: run a cheap predict first, escalate to chain-of-thought only if the cheap path returned nothing, and hard-throw to a human if the value is negative. Those are code-level invariants that must hold regardless of model capability. His line is worth keeping: even with AGI, I still want these things to be true.
- **The 550x Shopify result came from swapping an expensive model for a cheap one while keeping business logic intact.** The flexible contract is what made the swap safe, and the cost reduction is what made a previously infeasible data scale feasible.
- **Two things shipping in DSPy 4.** `dspy.flex`, which extends the optimization target from few-shot examples to prompts to actual code, learning a custom harness for a function over time. And qualitative learning, which is explicitly framed as an open research question.
- **The qualitative learning argument against conventional evals is the most interesting idea in the talk.** Three problems with hand-built evals: defining good is hard for real problems; scalar labels destroy information (knowing an email is bad carries far less signal than knowing what to change); and any hill you construct is a proxy for reality. The proposal is to let models interpret whatever textual feedback already exists in the environment (traces, user actions, product analytics) and convert that into the eval and the hill, refining both over time.
- **Their answer to "what about AGI" is the correct one and deserves to be quoted.** Intelligence is very different from being all-knowing. Ask Einstein to help with your emails and he would ask what an email is. A capable model will not know your context, your relationships, or your problem, so the last-mile learning problem survives arbitrary capability gains.
- **The ecosystem argument:** a fixed signature means research from anywhere (GEPA out of Berkeley, recursive language models out of MIT, multi-module GRPO) becomes something you can test in one line without changing your integration.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
This is the most directly applicable talk at the event for routing and compression work, because it is an argument about where the optimization boundary belongs.

The mechanics of the 550x result are worth being precise about, because it is easy to misread as "cheap models are fine." What actually happened is that a fixed input/output contract plus an eval turned model selection into a search problem with a measurable acceptance criterion. Without the contract you cannot swap models because you do not know if you broke anything; without the eval you cannot accept the swap. The 550x is the size of the gap between what teams pay by default and what the task actually requires, and that gap exists because the default is to pick a model once, by vibes, and then couple everything to it. This is exactly the thesis of routing work, stated as a software engineering practice rather than a systems one. The DSPy formulation is arguably the better framing: routing is not a runtime dispatch problem first, it is a specification problem, and you can only route safely over tasks whose contract and acceptance criterion are explicit.

`dspy.flex` extending the optimization target to code is the more consequential of the two DSPy 4 items. The progression (few-shot examples, then instructions, then harness code) is a progression up the abstraction ladder of what a search process can modify. Each step requires the model to be more capable, which they note directly: the earlier optimizers used code to find few-shot examples because models were not good enough to optimize instructions. That dependency is the interesting one, because it means optimizer capability and model capability compound, and the ceiling on automatic optimization moves with the frontier.

The qualitative learning idea has a specific structural resemblance to distillation that they did not draw out. In distillation you convert a rich signal (a teacher's full output distribution) into training pressure on a student, and the reason it beats hard-label training is that the soft targets carry more information per example. Their complaint about scalar evals is the same complaint: good/bad is a hard label, whereas "here is what would improve this email" is a soft target. Qualitative learning is, in effect, distillation applied to the eval function rather than to the model. That reframing suggests the known failure modes should transfer too, particularly the risk that the student learns the teacher's quirks. An eval derived from production feedback will encode whatever biases that feedback carries, and unlike a hand-built eval, nobody audited it.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The Shopify figure is real and reported independently of the talk. Rivest [disclosed it on the AI Engineer podcast](https://finance.biggo.com/news/ec96e5648c368f3f) as roughly a 550x cost reduction on a metadata extraction workload across all shops, achieved by swapping an expensive model for a cheap one while keeping business logic and evals intact. Worth noting the shape of that workload: metadata extraction at catalog scale is high-volume and structurally simple, which is the regime where the cheap-model gap is largest. A 550x reduction should not be read as a general expectation for reasoning-heavy tasks.

[GEPA](https://dspy.ai/getting-started/gepa-optimization/) is as described, an error-driven reflective prompt optimizer that analyzes failures, generates targeted natural-language feedback, and refines prompts. It gained fast adoption from mid-2025 because it frequently outperforms hand-engineered prompts while being notably sample-efficient, which matters because optimizer runs cost tokens. The recursive language models work Miller attributes to Alex Zhang at MIT is a real approach to long-context problems, and his framing is honest: it may or may not help your task, but the point is that testing it costs one line.

Their qualitative learning direction is not unique to DSPy, and knowing the neighboring work is useful. [Agentic Context Engineering](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04618) attacks the same problem of evolving contexts for self-improving models from a different angle. This is an active research area with no settled answer, so treat DSPy 4's version as one bet among several rather than as a shipped capability.

The one claim I would flag as underexamined is the implicit assumption that a fixed signature is always the right boundary. For tasks where the output format itself should co-evolve with capability (open-ended agentic work, exploratory research, anything where a better model would produce a differently-shaped answer), freezing the interface constrains the ceiling. Their examples (invoice extraction, grammar correction, email drafting, spec-to-PR) are all tasks with genuinely stable contracts. That is not an accident, and it marks the boundary of the method.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **Reframe your routing work as contract-first rather than dispatch-first, and steal their three-language specification.** For each task class your router handles, write down all three artifacts: the natural-language instruction, the code-level invariants that must hold regardless of which model served the request, and a small eval set defining acceptance. Most routing systems have the first and third at best. The missing middle, hard-coded invariants enforced outside the model, is what lets you route aggressively to cheap models without gambling. His invoice example is the template: cheap path, escalate on failure, hard-fail to human on invariant violation.
2. **Run their escalation pattern as an explicit cost tier in your router.** Predict-then-escalate-to-reasoning-on-empty-result is a two-tier cascade with a deterministic trigger, which is strictly better than a learned router for the cases where failure is detectable. Find every task in your stack where failure is cheaply detectable (empty extraction, malformed JSON, failed assertion, out-of-range value) and put the cheap model first with a deterministic escalation. This is free money and it requires no model.
3. **Instrument the qualitative feedback you are already discarding.** Before betting on DSPy's qualitative learning, collect the raw material: for one workload, log the textual signals already present (retry text, user rephrasings, downstream edits to the model's output, agent retrospectives). That corpus is what any of these methods needs as input, and it takes weeks to accumulate. Starting the collection now is decoupled from whether you adopt their approach, and it is the same asset Moritz Johner's agent retrospectives produce at Form3.
