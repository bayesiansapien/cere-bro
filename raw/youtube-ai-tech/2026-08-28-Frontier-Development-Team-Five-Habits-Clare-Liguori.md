# From AI-Assisted to AI-Native: Building a Frontier Development Team | Clare Liguori, AWS

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqlWNihgdjI

## TL;DR
Liguori, a senior principal engineer on Kiro, reports that inline completion, chat, and vibe coding each delivered only 10 to 20% productivity gain in her own experience, while Amazon's internal pilots are now showing a median 4.5x with some teams above 10x. The decisive finding comes from a structured Amazon Stores pilot across 50 teams with a normal seniority distribution working on existing brownfield codebases: half achieved under 3x and half achieved the step-function gains, and 90% of both groups used the same tools. The difference was not tooling, it was that the successful teams intentionally restructured how they worked. Five habits emerged: invest in agent context, slow down to speed up, feed agents rather than babysit them, make intent explicit before generating code, and shift testing left. She then spends the last third of the talk on what breaks, which is the most valuable part.

## Key Takeaways
- **Frontier developer defined by three behaviours:** they write 1 to 2% of the code they produce, they aim for agent runs of up to hours without intervention, and they minimise idle time by running multiple agents in parallel against a task backlog.
- **The three data points, in ascending credibility.** Bedrock's new inference data plane was estimated at 30 people over 18 months and was built by six people in 76 days, but those six included two distinguished engineers, so it proved possibility rather than reproducibility. A Prime Video 10-day sprint cut a 90-week delivery estimate to 24 weeks, but the six engineers had no on-call, few meetings, and a senior engineer had spent the prior three weeks pre-decomposing every task into small well-scoped items with detailed requirements. The Amazon Stores pilot is the real evidence: 50 teams, mixed seniority, existing codebases, measured on deployment velocity to production rather than commit count.
- **Habit one, agent context as a written artefact.** Everything normally transferred through Slack, onboarding, mentorship, code review, and standups has to be written down. The operational habit is a trigger: every time the agent makes a mistake or does something the wrong way, ask what is missing from the skills or steering files. The inverse habit matters equally, and is newer: as models improve, ask whether a given instruction is still needed or is now just bloating context. Her example is that Sonnet 3.7 quirks required many explicit "do nots" that Opus 4.5 no longer needs.
- **Habit two, slowing down to speed up, and every interviewed team reported this.** Productivity went down during adoption. The prerequisite engineering work is real: build agent context, improve existing tools' error messages so the model can understand failures, build new tools and MCP servers, restructure the codebase for agent navigability, and in some cases change language. She reports teams moving off Python and JavaScript because untyped languages give the model no compiler signal, toward TypeScript, with Rust becoming popular internally specifically for its error messages.
- **Habit three, feeding versus babysitting, is the mechanism behind the step function.** If you are in a conversational loop waiting 30 to 60 seconds per turn, you cannot run agents in parallel and cannot exceed roughly linear gains. Feeding means specifying the task plus how the agent can self-validate, so it self-corrects and only returns when it clears a defined quality bar (compiles, tests pass, coverage adequate). The next level is moving that into the steering file so it happens without prompting.
- **Habit four, make intent explicit.** Iterating with an agent on code when the intent was wrong is less productive than iterating on a specification document first, because a document is a smaller and more coherent object to revise than changes spread across a codebase. Amazon's behaviour-driven development practice made this natural to adopt.
- **Habit five, shift testing left, specifically to shorten the agent's feedback loop.** Linters, unit tests, integration tests, performance and security tests. Her most concrete recommendation: mock out dependent services with deterministic local responses so the agent can run the full loop on a laptop without spinning up cloud services. Faster loops mean more self-correction cycles per unit of wall-clock time. Her framing is honest, these are practices everyone always knew they should have, and the ROI is finally high enough to justify them.
- **What breaks, part one: burnout.** She cites "FOMO-mat" style behaviour, engineers staying up late trying to craft the prompt that will let an agent run overnight. Cognitive load rises with parallel agents and constant terminal-tab switching.
- **What breaks, part two: review is harder than writing for early-career engineers.** Senior engineers have spent years reviewing others' code and have the muscle. Junior engineers do not, so a workflow that is 98% review is disproportionately taxing for them. This is the most important organisational observation in the talk and it has direct hiring and training implications.
- **What breaks, part three: leadership refuses the slowdown.** She indicts herself and her peers for asking why teams are not faster now that the tools exist, while the required investment takes about two months.
- **What breaks, part four: the bottleneck moves.** Once code takes one to two months instead of nine to twelve, decision-making speed and launch approval processes become the long pole. Frontier teams spend more time making decisions than writing code. Her prescription: make fast decisions, especially easily reversible ones.
- **Scaling is the open problem.** 2026's task at Amazon is going from 50 pilot teams to roughly 2,000, and she explicitly warns against going broad too fast, because the pilots are what produced the practices.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The context-pruning habit is the one Amit should take most seriously, because it is a measurable optimization that almost nobody performs. Steering-file instructions accumulate monotonically, since every agent mistake adds a rule and nothing ever removes one. But those instructions are a fixed per-request prefix tax paid on every single call, and the specific instructions Liguori cites (working around Sonnet 3.7 quirks) became dead weight the moment the model changed. That is stale context consuming tokens and attention on every request indefinitely. The engineering discipline needed is a steering-file regression test: on each model upgrade, ablate instruction blocks and measure whether task success degrades. Blocks that can be removed without loss are pure savings, and they also improve the signal-to-noise ratio of what remains. This is structurally identical to pruning, remove the parameters that no longer contribute, and it improves the effective use of the remaining budget rather than merely making it cheaper.

The babysitting-versus-feeding distinction is a throughput argument and worth stating formally. Interactive coding is a synchronous request-response loop where the human is the rate limiter and utilisation is bounded by one agent per human. Feeding converts it to asynchronous batch submission with self-validation, which lets a human drive N agents concurrently. The gain is not a better model, it is parallelism unlocked by removing the human from the inner loop. Two things gate N: how long an agent can run before needing intervention (which is what self-validation extends) and the human's review bandwidth (which is why review capability becomes the binding constraint). That is exactly why she reports the bottleneck migrating to decisions and review, it is Amdahl's law applied to an engineering organisation, and the serial fraction is now human judgment.

The typed-language and error-message findings are the same insight in two forms: agents need a fast, high-fidelity, machine-readable oracle. A compiler is a cheap deterministic verifier with precise localisation, and an untyped language forces the model to guess and defer verification to runtime or to the human. Deterministic local mocks serve the same function for integration behaviour. Generalised: the value of an agentic loop scales with the quality and latency of its verifier, which is the same conclusion the Cloudflare, Notion, and Lean 4 talks in this corpus each reach independently. For Amit's own workflows, the analogue is making benchmark and eval harnesses fast, local, deterministic, and precise in their failure messages. A three-hour GPU benchmark is a terrible oracle for an agentic loop regardless of how good the agent is.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Every number in the talk is corroborated by AWS's own published material, which is both reassuring and the central caveat.

AWS confirms six senior engineers rebuilt the Bedrock inference engine, originally estimated at 30 developers over 12 to 18 months, in 76 days. The Amazon Stores structured pilots report a median 4.5x gain in normalised deployment velocity with some teams exceeding 10x, and separately report individual developer productivity around 20x on normalised commit velocity, with commits rising from 2 per week to 40. AWS Professional Services has published its own account of becoming a frontier team, with specific examples: Perfect Order Experience going from a two-week feature cycle to shipping in an afternoon, and WW Grocery cutting design document creation from five days to a few hours. The tooling stack is Kiro plus Amazon Bedrock AgentCore plus Strands, and AWS's own framing matches Liguori's central claim, that tools compound only when the workflow is redesigned around them. Kiro has continued shipping, with Kiro Pro Max appearing in the June 15, 2026 AWS roundup.

The caveat is unavoidable and Liguori does not address it: every one of these figures is self-reported by the vendor selling the tool, measured with internally chosen metrics, with no control group and no external audit. She deserves real credit for the methodological honesty she does show, she explicitly discounts the Bedrock result for engineer selection bias, discounts the Prime Video sprint for artificial conditions and three weeks of prior task decomposition, and names the Amazon Stores pilot as the more credible evidence because it used normal teams on existing code and measured shipping velocity rather than commits. That is better epistemic hygiene than most vendor talks.

But two problems remain. First, commit velocity going from 2 to 40 per week is a 20x change in a metric that measures activity, not value, and is trivially inflatable by an agent that commits more granularly. Deployment velocity is a better proxy and still not an outcome measure. Second, the 50-team split, half under 3x and half above, is presented as evidence that working style is causal. It is equally consistent with selection: teams whose leads were enthusiastic enough to restructure their workflow are also teams likely to be stronger on other dimensions and to report more favourably. Without randomised assignment of the working-style intervention, "how they worked" and "who they were" cannot be separated. Note that even a 3x floor across all 50 teams is a large effect, so the pilot's weakest claim is still substantial.

Worth flagging for calibration: independent research on AI coding assistance has produced considerably more mixed results than vendor pilots, including findings that experienced developers on familiar codebases can be slower with AI assistance while believing they were faster. Liguori's own anecdotal 10 to 20% figure for pre-frontier tooling is roughly consistent with the modest independent estimates, which is a point in her favour, her claim is that a workflow change, not the tools, produces the discontinuity. That claim is plausible and remains unverified outside Amazon.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Build a steering-file regression test and run it on every model upgrade.** Ablate instruction blocks, measure task success, delete what no longer earns its tokens. This is the highest-leverage habit in the talk and the one nobody does. For Amit it is directly analogous to pruning, and the measurement is straightforward: tokens removed, plus success rate held constant.
- **Make the eval and benchmark harness the fast local oracle.** The single biggest determinant of how long an agent can run unattended is verifier latency and precision. Amit should audit his own harnesses on three axes: does it run locally, is it deterministic, and does its failure output localise the problem precisely. Deterministic mocks for expensive dependencies (a GPU-free path, a small-model stand-in for pipeline correctness) buy agent autonomy directly.
- **Rewrite prompts as task-plus-self-validation-criteria, not as tasks.** The concrete change: every agent instruction should include how the agent verifies its own work and what quality bar returns control. Then promote the recurring criteria into the steering file. This is what converts an interactive loop into a batch submission and it is the mechanism behind the whole productivity claim.
- **Budget the slowdown explicitly and tell your manager the number.** Roughly two months of investment before gains appear, per Liguori. Teams that skip this get under 3x. Leadership that will not accept it will not get the gains, and she says so from the leadership side.
- **Treat review capacity as the new constraint and train for it deliberately.** The finding that reviewing AI output is harder than writing it for early-career engineers is the most actionable organisational item here. If Amit mentors or hires, review skill is now a primary competency rather than a byproduct of seniority, and it needs explicit teaching rather than accumulation by osmosis.
- **Anticipate the bottleneck migration before it arrives.** If implementation compresses 5x, then decision latency, approval processes, and review throughput become the schedule. For a research team this means experiment-selection speed and result-review speed dominate, so the leverage moves to cheap reversible experiments and fast triage rather than to faster implementation.
- **Discount the headline multiples and act on the mechanism.** The 4.5x and 20x figures are vendor self-reported with activity-based metrics and no control. The mechanism, remove the human from the inner loop by giving the agent a fast verifier, is sound, testable on Amit's own work, and does not depend on believing the numbers.
