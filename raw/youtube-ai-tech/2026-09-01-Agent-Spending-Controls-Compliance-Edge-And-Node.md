# Agent Spending Without Controls | Rodrigo Coelho & Pranav Maheshwari, Edge & Node

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZyGMqdIpPoE

## TL;DR
Edge & Node, the team behind The Graph protocol (1.8 trillion queries served since 2018), argues the missing layer in agentic payments is not the rails but compliance. Traditional payment controls assume a human in the loop making an authorisation decision, and agents transact at machine speed around the clock. Their product, Ampersend, is an aggregator marketplace of paid MCP tools plus a wallet, installed into a coding agent as a single skill file. The demo shows Claude Code with the skill file successfully retrieving a specific person's email at Mastercard by paying a paid endpoint, while the same prompt without it returns only the email format. A second demo places a sub-$10 Shopify UCP order end to end from the terminal. The third and most substantive demo turns on wallet screening via TRM and shows a sanctioned "bad claw" wallet's transactions moving from authorised to rejected on the block-list reason.

## Key Takeaways
- **The thesis: paid MCP tools are coming and free ones are the anomaly.** Their argument is that an agent's capability is bounded by the tools it can reach, that the valuable tools (Exa, Firecrawl, and the growing set) are or will be paid, and that no user will enter a credit card into dozens of individual MCP providers. So the aggregator plus wallet becomes the access layer.
- **Distribution via a skill file is the notable product decision.** One skill file installed into the agent replaces per-provider signup, credential management, and card entry. This is the same pattern as the Stripe and Metronome talk at this conference, vendors shipping skills to make their surface agent-usable, and the same as the c15t talk shipping docs into node_modules. Skills are becoming the distribution channel.
- **Compliance is framed as the enterprise blocker, and the framing is credible.** With agents you are presented with a wallet address and nothing else. Enterprises need to know whether a counterparty is sanctioned, whether it has been involved in terrorist financing, and who the identity behind it is. All of this infrastructure exists in traditional finance and none of it is wired up for agent wallets.
- **The accountability argument is the sharpest point in the talk.** At the end of the chain there is a named human, a chief legal officer or chief policy officer, who must sign off, and that person needs confidence that the system will not allow an agent to hallucinate, go off the rails, overspend, or breach policy. Compliance failures in this domain carry fines in the tens or hundreds of millions, sometimes billions. That is why the sign-off does not happen on a demo.
- **The screening demo is the most useful thing shown.** With screening disabled, both a clean wallet and a sanctioned wallet complete a 0.1-cent payment to a scraping service. With TRM screening enabled, the sanctioned wallet's transactions are rejected with the reason given as block-listed address. It is a small demo of a large point: the compliance check is a per-transaction gate that either exists or does not.
- **Their prior art is real and predates the standard.** Edge & Node built a micropayment system for Graph queries in 2021 and referenced the 402 spec in a blog post at the time. When Coinbase released x402, they contributed to the spec, particularly a batching protocol to reduce gas fees on nanopayments, and joined the foundation.
- **Adoption reality, stated honestly:** they are seeing retail experimentation, notably via OpenClaw, and explicitly not enterprise adoption yet. Enterprises are building infrastructure for when the use cases break through.
- **The Cloudflare pay-per-crawl point is the strongest business case in the talk.** If an agent is crawling a website rather than a human viewing it, advertising is worthless, so the publisher's revenue has to come from a micropayment at the gateway. That is a real economic gap with a real mechanism.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The technically interesting content is the batching contribution and the compliance-gate placement, and both matter for anyone building agent infrastructure.

Batching to amortise gas is the same optimization as request batching in inference serving, and the tradeoff is identical. Individual nanopayments are dominated by fixed per-transaction cost, exactly as individual token generations are dominated by fixed kernel launch and weight-load cost. Batching amortises the fixed cost across N items and buys throughput at the price of latency, because item one waits for the batch to fill. The design question is the same one continuous batching answers in serving: how long do you hold a payment before settling, and can you admit new items into an in-flight batch. The fact that Edge & Node arrived at batching from a payments direction and vLLM arrived at it from a serving direction is a good illustration that this is a general property of systems with high fixed and low marginal cost per unit.

The compliance gate's placement is the more consequential design question and the talk does not fully address it. A per-transaction screening call sits in the critical path of every payment, which means an agent making thousands of nanopayments pays the screening latency thousands of times. That is the same problem Kanish Manuja described for guardrails in the LLM gateway talk at this conference, and the same solutions apply: give screening its own time budget so it never becomes the rate-determining step, cache screening decisions per counterparty with a TTL, and build a fallback for when the screening provider is down, including the fail-open versus fail-closed decision. For sanctions screening the answer is clearly fail-closed, which makes the screening provider's availability a hard dependency on the whole payment path. None of that is discussed, and it is the difference between a demo and a production system.

The paid-MCP thesis also has a direct cost-modelling consequence Amit should note. If tools become metered, then an agent's total cost per task is no longer just tokens, it is tokens plus tool calls priced individually. That changes the optimization objective: reducing tool calls becomes as valuable as reducing tokens, and it makes precomputation and caching of tool results a direct financial saving rather than a latency one. Every talk in this conference that recommended materialising a repeated aggregation or precomputing a context bundle becomes a stronger recommendation under metered tools.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The standard is real and institutionally serious. The volume numbers are not what the framing implies, and this is the essential context for the entire agentic-commerce track at this conference.

x402 launched in May 2025 as a Coinbase project using USDC as the settlement currency. The x402 Foundation was announced by Coinbase and Cloudflare on September 23, 2025, and formalised by the Linux Foundation on April 2, 2026 at the MCP Dev Summit North America, with 22 launch members including Adyen, AWS, American Express, Base, Circle, Fiserv, Google, KakaoPay, Mastercard, Microsoft, Polygon Labs, PPRO, Shopify, Solana Foundation, Stripe, thirdweb, and Visa. Five deployments were running in production by April 2026: Coinbase Agent.market, Stripe Machine Payments, CoinGecko paid endpoints, Circle Wallets reference workflow, and the Cloudflare Agents SDK. USDC accounts for close to 100% of agentic transfer volume.

The headline adoption figures look impressive. By late April 2026 Coinbase reported 69,000 active agents, 165 million transactions, and roughly $50 million in cumulative volume. By June 2026 cumulative transactions crossed 160 million with more than 90% on Base. A 30-day window around August 2026 processed between 14 and 17.8 million transactions.

**Then the deflating detail.** Average transaction value on Base is roughly $0.13, per Token Terminal. And adoption tracking suggests that of roughly 167 million settled transactions in 2026, about half are gamified rather than genuine commerce, leaving real volume at approximately $28,000 per day. CoinDesk's March 2026 assessment was blunt in the headline: the protocol wants to fix micropayments but demand is just not there yet.

That reframes this talk considerably. Coelho is admirably honest about the state of play, saying explicitly that they see retail experimentation and no enterprise adoption, and that everyone is building infrastructure for when the use cases break through. The numbers support him: an institutional standard with Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, AWS, and Google as members, running roughly $28,000 a day of genuine economic activity. The infrastructure is far ahead of the demand, which is a normal and not necessarily fatal condition for a payments standard, but it means the compliance layer they are building is a prerequisite for a market that does not yet exist rather than an unblocker for one that does.

Two notes on the demo itself. It struggled on stage, with the presenter skipping past a transaction view that would not load and returning to it later, which is worth remembering when assessing the frictionlessness claim. And the first demo, an agent autonomously paying to retrieve a named individual's personal email address, is presented as a capability win. It is also a live demonstration that a metered data-broker endpoint plus an agent wallet removes every friction from personal-data acquisition, which is a privacy consequence the talk does not acknowledge and which regulators plainly will.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Add tool-call cost to the agent cost model now, before tools are metered.** If paid MCP is the direction, then cost per completed task is tokens plus priced tool calls, and reducing tool calls becomes a direct financial lever. Amit should instrument tool-call counts per task alongside token counts, because that is the metric that will matter and it is cheap to start measuring today.
- **Treat per-transaction compliance screening as a gateway dependency and design it accordingly.** Own time budget so it never becomes the rate-determining step, per-counterparty decision caching with a TTL, and explicit fail-closed behaviour with a defined outage path. This is the guardrail-reliability pattern from the Twilio talk applied to payments, and it is what the demo omits.
- **Set a per-agent hard spending cap at the wallet, not in the prompt.** The talk's own framing is that a named human is accountable for an agent that overspends. A prompt instruction is not a control. A wallet-level cap with a separate authorisation path for exceeding it is, and it is the same recommendation the Stripe billing talk makes independently.
- **Use the $28,000-per-day figure to calibrate urgency.** The standard is institutionally backed and the infrastructure is being built out by every major payments company, so it is worth understanding. But genuine transaction volume at that level means there is no near-term commercial pressure to adopt, and anyone claiming agentic commerce is here now is describing a roadmap. Build with it, do not bet a product on it this year.
- **Note the batching-as-amortisation parallel as a general design principle.** Systems with high fixed and low marginal cost per unit converge on batching, whether the unit is a token or a payment. The interesting design question is always the admission policy, whether new items can join an in-flight batch, and that is where continuous batching beat static batching in serving.
- **Flag the privacy exposure to anyone building on this.** An agent that can autonomously pay a data broker for a named individual's email has no friction left in personal-data acquisition. If Amit's organisation deploys agent wallets, the tool allowlist matters more than the spending cap, because the reputational and regulatory exposure from a compliant $0.13 transaction against a data broker is far larger than the money.
