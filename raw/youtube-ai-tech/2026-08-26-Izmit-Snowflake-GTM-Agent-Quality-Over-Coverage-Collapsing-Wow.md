# Building GTM AI Agents: Lessons from Deploying to 6,000 Users — Sait Izmit, Snowflake

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DrTdD-ttjCY

## TL;DR
Izmit runs Snowflake's internal AI tooling for a sales organisation of roughly 5,000 people inside a 10,000-person company. Their go-to-market assistant launched September 2025, has answered over 1.2 million questions, and currently handles about 40,000 a week. This is the rare deployment talk with enough operational history to say something non-obvious, and the four claims that matter are all counterintuitive. First, quality beats coverage so decisively that he would rather answer 50 questions at 95% than 100 at 70%, because user trust is earned slowly and lost in five questions. Second, most AI initiatives that fail do not fail on technology, they fail on activation, and he spends 60 to 70% of his time on change management rather than engineering. Third, the "collapsing wow factor": once your innovation becomes habit it becomes the baseline, and if you stop iterating you get switched away from within a month or two. Fourth, and this is the honest confession of the talk, they launched to 6,000 people with a nine-page agent instruction document version-controlled in a Google Doc, and 80% of the current architecture does not match the original design diagram. Thirty to forty percent of ongoing sprint capacity goes to re-architecting on new primitives, and he presents that as correct rather than as failure.

## Key Takeaways
- The scale numbers are the credential: 1.2 million questions answered, 40,000 per week, 6,000 go-to-market users, 15 semantic views, 85 tables, 3,000 columns, five to six MCP connections, close to 20 skills. Sixty percent of the data was added in the six to seven months after launch, not before it.
- Quality over coverage, stated as a hard rule. Fifty questions at 95% beats 100 at 70%, because the first five questions a user asks determine whether they return, and winning back a burned user is 10x the effort of the initial win. Their team slogan is "quality is P-minus-one."
- The 150-question spreadsheet is the single most transferable practice in the talk. Before touching the agent, Izmit took the sales process and wrote 150 questions sellers would actually ask, then evaluated against them. Engineering objected that the agent did not have that data. His answer, that it does not matter because these are the questions your users will ask regardless, is the correct framing: the eval set is defined by user intent, not by system capability. Initial score was 50%.
- Phased launch with distinct exit criteria per phase, not just a percentage ramp. Pilot proves accuracy with AI-native volunteers who tolerate rough edges. Beta at 10%, about 600 people, proves MVP completeness by revealing where data requests concentrate, and proves stickiness. They exited beta at above 70% weekly-active retention. GA follows.
- The adoption diagnostic is a clean separation of two failure modes that get conflated. If only 20% of the org tried the product, that is not a product problem and no amount of engineering fixes it. If they tried it and did not return, that is a product problem. Knowing which curve you are looking at determines whether the next sprint is features or sales meetings.
- Collapsing wow factor is the strategic claim. Delight becomes habit, habit becomes baseline, and the baseline resets the bar you must clear. His prescription is paranoia proportional to satisfaction: every time users are happy, ask what you show them in two months.
- The maturity ladder he observes across sales teams has four rungs. Talk to your data, which kills dashboard and analyst dependency. Automate my workflows, enabled by MCP connections, where the user becomes an orchestrator. Team-level building, where GTM teams escape the IT backlog and build their own skills, dashboards and alerts. Hyper-personalisation with living customer context. Stopping at rung one gets you disrupted in a month.
- The architectural evolution is documented as a sequence of forced moves, which is more useful than a finished diagram. Launched with a nine-page instruction doc, a couple of Cortex Analyst tools over semantic views, and a Cortex Search service, versioned in Google Docs. Then CI/CD, because Google Docs did not scale. Then eval infrastructure with unit tests and routing tests. Then a skill library, because business processes no longer fit in agent instructions. Then MCP. Then progressive disclosure, because orchestration instructions hit the agent-instruction limit. Then user memory, task scheduling, and Slack as a second surface.
- Thirty to forty percent of sprint capacity is re-architecture, and Izmit argues against over-investing in current architecture precisely because the primitives keep changing underneath you. The enterprises he sees failing are the ones evaluating frameworks in search of the correct architecture instead of shipping.
- Log mining with LLM classification is where the compounding comes from. Classifying 40,000 questions a week into a topic taxonomy gives real-time feature-gap detection, including behavioural distress signals: users repeating a question, or swearing at the agent, mark quality failures without any explicit feedback mechanism.
- The sales-enablement loop is the strongest concrete example. Detecting a knowledge gap from logs used to require interviewing 100 sellers a week. Now it is one query, and the response is to pull from Confluence, Jira, Slack and PRDs, generate battle cards, and feed them back into the agent within minutes. A content pipeline closed end to end by log analysis.
- Cross-team matchmaking is the emergent capability nobody designs for: different sales teams approaching the same account from different angles do not know about each other, and the log layer does, so the system can introduce them.
- The platform effect is stated plainly: the first features are hard, the next ones are easy, and once you tap the logs the curve goes exponential.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The quality-over-coverage rule is usually presented as product wisdom, and it is worth restating as the routing and abstention problem it actually is, because that version is actionable. A system that answers 100 questions at 70% is not better than one answering 50 at 95% along any axis the user perceives, because the user cannot tell in advance which bucket their question landed in. Every answer is therefore discounted by the system's global reliability. The fix is not to answer fewer questions, it is to know which questions you answer well and to decline the rest explicitly. That is a calibration problem, and it is the same machinery as a routing confidence threshold: estimate per-query competence, serve above threshold, abstain or escalate below it. Izmit's 150-question spreadsheet is in effect a labelled competence dataset, and the fact that he built it before the agent existed is what makes it uncontaminated by system capability. The generalisable form is that your eval set should be sampled from the intent distribution, not from the capability distribution, and any team whose benchmark was assembled by asking what the system can do has built a benchmark that cannot detect its own blind spots.

The progressive disclosure adoption is the most technically interesting forced move in the sequence, and the causal chain deserves spelling out because it recurs everywhere. Agent instructions grow monotonically as business processes are encoded. Instruction length hits a hard limit, and well before that hard limit it hits a soft one, because attention dilutes across long contexts and instructions buried in the middle stop being followed. The lost-in-the-middle degradation is well documented and it means that instruction quality falls before instruction capacity does. Progressive disclosure resolves this by layering: metadata at startup, roughly 100 tokens per skill covering only name and description, the full skill body under about 5k tokens loaded only when triggered, and supporting material loaded only during execution. The mechanism is routing applied to context rather than to models. You are selecting which subset of a large instruction corpus to admit into the window based on a cheap relevance check, which is structurally the same decision as selecting which model tier to route a query to, and it has the same failure mode, namely that the cheap selector's recall bounds the whole system. If the description-level match misses a relevant skill, no amount of downstream capability recovers it. Anyone deploying skills at scale should be evaluating the trigger step as a retrieval problem with its own recall metric, separately from end-task accuracy, and Izmit's "routing tests" in the eval infrastructure suggest Snowflake reached the same conclusion.

The log-mining loop is worth taking seriously as an optimization technique, not just a product-management one, and Izmit gestures at the interesting engineering when he says classifying at that volume without breaking the bank is technically fun. Forty thousand questions a week is roughly 2 million a year, and classifying each with a frontier model is straightforwardly wasteful. The standard structure is a cascade: a cheap embedding-plus-clustering pass or a small classifier handles the bulk where the topic is unambiguous, and expensive classification is reserved for the tail that the cheap path is uncertain about. This is the canonical routing win because classification is exactly the task where small models close most of the gap, and because the label distribution is heavily skewed so the head is cheap by construction. The second-order point is that the logs are also a distillation corpus. A year of production queries with outcomes attached is a better fine-tuning set for a task-specific small model than anything you could buy, and the categories Izmit is already extracting are the natural task boundaries along which to carve specialist models out of the monolith.

The re-architecture claim deserves a push-back, or at least a qualification, because as stated it is close to unfalsifiable. "Do not over-invest in your architecture, expect to rewrite it" is correct advice in a fast-moving primitive landscape and it is also exactly what a team with no architectural discipline would say. The distinguishing question is which parts they did not rewrite. Reading the sequence carefully, the answer is visible and it is the useful part: the data consolidation into Snowflake, the role-based access controls inherited from the warehouse, and the semantic views over structured data are load-bearing and unchanged, while everything above them, instruction management, orchestration, skills, interface, has churned. That is a coherent position rather than a lack of one. Invest permanently in the data and governance layer, treat the agent scaffolding as disposable. The mistake the enterprises he criticises make is not planning, it is planning at the disposable layer, which is where planning has the shortest half-life.

One thing the talk understates: 30 to 40% of sprint capacity spent tracking primitive churn is a large tax, and it is only worth paying because each new primitive, skills and progressive disclosure especially, removed a constraint they had already hit. The version of this advice that generalises badly is chasing primitives you have no constraint for. The discipline in their sequence is that every re-architecture is preceded by a named limit they ran into, and that ordering is what separates it from novelty-chasing.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The platform details check out. Snowflake Intelligence was renamed Snowflake CoWork on 2 June 2026 at Summit, alongside a companion product CoCo, and the rename tracked a genuine scope expansion from conversational data Q&A into a top-level agent that runs investigations, produces artifacts including PDFs and slide decks, and takes action in systems outside Snowflake. The underlying stack Izmit names, Cortex Analyst over semantic views for structured data and Cortex Search for unstructured, is the documented composition of the Agentic AI Platform, which also covers Cortex Agents, Cortex AISQL, Openflow and Horizon governance. His claim that agents inherit warehouse role-based access controls is the substantive architectural argument for the whole approach and it is accurate: consolidating first-party, third-party, Salesforce and call transcript data into the warehouse means authorisation is enforced once, at the data layer, rather than reimplemented per agent. That is a materially better security posture than agents federating across fifteen SaaS APIs with fifteen permission models, and it is the strongest reason to prefer this design that Izmit himself does not press hard enough.

His progressive disclosure move lands squarely on the emerging consensus rather than ahead of it. Anthropic introduced Agent Skills in October 2025 and published the specification as an open standard on 18 December 2025, and the three-level structure, metadata always loaded at roughly 100 tokens per skill, body under about 5k loaded on trigger, resources loaded during execution, is now implemented across Claude Code, Codex CLI and Gemini CLI. The research question is live rather than settled: an arXiv paper from July 2026, "Is Progressive Disclosure All You Need for Long-Context Agents?", puts the technique under direct scrutiny, which is worth reading before treating layered loading as a solved problem. The motivating failure, context rot and lost-in-the-middle degradation, is well established, and the honest statement is that progressive disclosure addresses it well enough to unblock production systems while the question of whether it is sufficient remains open.

The one place to be sceptical is the interpretation of the retention and adoption numbers, not their accuracy. Above 70% weekly-active retention at beta exit is a strong figure, but the beta population was the 10% closest to AI-native early adopters after a pilot selected for enthusiasm, so it is close to a best case rather than a representative one. Similarly, the 20%-tried-it diagnostic is used to deflect blame from the product to activation, and while the logic is sound in general, low trial rates are not always independent of product reputation. Word of mouth from a bad pilot suppresses trial, which makes the two failure modes he separates cleanly less separable than he presents them. Neither point undermines the advice; both are worth holding when applying the diagnostic to a deployment that is genuinely struggling.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Build the eval set from the intent distribution before building the system, and specifically before knowing what the system can do. Write the questions your users will ask, not the ones you can answer, and accept the bad initial score. Any benchmark assembled after the fact inherits the system's blind spots and cannot detect them.
2. Convert quality-over-coverage into an explicit abstention policy. Estimate per-query competence, serve above a threshold, escalate or decline below it. Reporting accuracy over answered queries while silently answering everything is the failure this prevents, and abstention rate belongs next to accuracy in every eval report.
3. Set numeric exit criteria per rollout phase rather than ramping on a schedule. Accuracy against the intent-sampled set gates pilot to beta. Retention gates beta to GA, and Snowflake's bar was above 70% weekly active return. A ramp without gates is just a slower launch.
4. Separate the two adoption failure modes before spending an engineering sprint. Trial rate and return rate are different diagnoses with different fixes, and low trial is not a product bug. Instrument both from day one, because you cannot reconstruct trial rate retrospectively.
5. Treat skill or tool selection as a retrieval problem with its own recall metric. Under progressive disclosure the description-level trigger bounds system capability, and a miss there is unrecoverable downstream. Test routing separately from end-task accuracy, which is exactly what Snowflake's "routing tests" do.
6. Mine production logs with a cascade, not a frontier model per record. Cheap embedding-based clustering or a small classifier on the head of the distribution, expensive classification only on the uncertain tail. At 40,000 queries a week the difference between these two designs is an order of magnitude in cost for near-identical taxonomy quality.
7. Treat your query log as a distillation corpus, not just an analytics source. Production queries with outcomes attached are the highest-quality task-specific training data available, and the topic categories you extract for analytics are the natural boundaries along which to carve specialist small models out of a monolithic agent.
8. Require a named constraint before adopting a new primitive. Every re-architecture in Snowflake's sequence was preceded by a limit they had actually hit, instruction length, version control, orchestration overhead. That precondition is what separates a 30% re-architecture tax that pays for itself from novelty-chasing that does not.
9. Invest permanently below the agent and disposably above it. Data consolidation, governance and access control survive every rewrite and are where inherited RBAC comes from. Instruction management, orchestration and interfaces churn. Plan the durable layer properly, and keep the churning layer cheap to replace.
10. Budget for change management as an engineering line item. Izmit spends 60 to 70% of his time on demos, sales meetings, adoption dashboards and executive sponsorship, and says the product would be at half its current usage without it. If nobody on the team owns activation, the default outcome is a technically sound system nobody opened.
