# What Building an AI Scientist Actually Requires Beyond Intelligence (Edward Hughes)

**Channel:** Machine Learning Street Talk
**Published:** 2026-09-11
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P4bYjTJvD28

## TL;DR
Edward Hughes, ex Google DeepMind AI Scientist co-lead and now Chief Scientist at Inherent, lays out a thesis that discovery is a constraint satisfaction problem rather than an optimization problem, and backs it with a concrete result: a 27B Qwen model post-trained with GRPO to *drive* a frontier coding agent beats that same frontier agent used directly. The task space is paper replication, where a figure is irreversibly redacted from a published PDF and the agent must rebuild it from the text alone under a one hour wall clock and a one seventh MIG slice of an H200. The small model is not doing the engineering. It is doing the science: deciding what to ask the big model for, when to stop it, and whether the result is rigorous. That is the load bearing idea for anyone working on routing and small model orchestration.

## Key Takeaways
- **Inherent raised $50M from Index Ventures and Radical Ventures at a $225M post money valuation, with a team of 11.** The thesis is a horizontal intelligence layer across all of science, not a vertical coding agent.
- **Faraday is a 27B Qwen 3.6 post-trained with GRPO, using GPT-5.5 Codex as a callable tool.** It beat the Codex agent it was driving, Claude Opus 4.8, and the open weights GLM 5.2 on held out replication tasks.
- **The Replica task space is 310 tasks from 100 papers.** Train on 242 classic ML papers (post-training, CNNs, LSTMs, open-endedness), test on 68 held out AI for science papers. The train/test split is a deliberate domain shift, not a random split.
- **Reward is a per task auto-generated rubric judged by a frontier coding agent**, scoring visual fidelity, experimental integrity, and claim reproduction, with explicit anti-cheating guidelines. The judge was validated against human expert raters.
- **Two tricks made GRPO stable on non-verifiable long horizon tasks.** Score each rollout multiple times with the same judge and average, to beat down LLM judge stochasticity. Then do per turn credit assignment: the judge emits a normalized weight distribution over turns, which reweights GRPO advantages.
- **Interpretability on those weights shows the judge concentrates weight on middle-of-rollout turns and on turns where the 27B model is prompting Codex.** The high value decisions are the instructions to the big model, not the boilerplate.
- **The advantage over Claude grows when you scale resources.** Evaluated out of distribution on 8x B300 GPUs and 8 hours, Faraday's margin over Claude was *larger* than on the 1 hour task. Scientific rigor pays off more as the search space widens.
- **Prompt optimization is not a substitute for weight updates.** They ran a Karpathy-style auto research loop optimizing the GPT-5.5 Codex prompt against the rubric score. It helped by a tiny amount and did not close the gap.
- **Harnesses have roughly a three month half life.** Lewis Kirsch's analysis found base models absorb harness capability about three months after the harness is built. Weights generalize, harnesses are sample efficient. Hughes wants both: harness at training time, distilled into weights for test time.
- **Move 37 was innovative, not creative.** Hughes's distinction: innovation turns unknown unknowns into known knowns. Creativity requires the additional metacognitive step of *recognizing* that what you did was novel. AlphaGo did not recognize it. The commentators did.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The inversion here is the part worth internalizing. Standard cascade and routing designs put a small model in front as a *classifier*: cheap model answers easy queries, escalates hard ones to the expensive model. Faraday inverts the control relationship. The small model is always in the loop, and its job is supervision, not answering. It prompts the frontier agent, monitors the rollout mid-flight, and decides whether to stop or continue. The frontier model supplies engineering competence; the small model supplies taste and stopping criteria. Hughes's framing: coding agents are decent engineers who write slop code and are bad at asking questions, so you train the question-asking layer separately.

The per turn credit assignment detail deserves attention beyond this paper. The problem it solves is the same one that shows up in any long horizon agentic RL: uniform advantage across a rollout wastes gradient on behaviours the base model already does well. Hughes gives the concrete example: Qwen already reads PDFs competently, so upweighting that turn on every successful rollout is noise, not signal. By having the judge emit a per turn weight distribution that sums to one, then normalizing by token count per turn so long turns do not dominate, they concentrate the update on the turns that actually differentiated this rollout from the others in the group. He explicitly checked that the judge rarely assigns zero weight, so you do not get dead zones in the state action space, you get a reweighted distribution.

The compute framing is also instructive for anyone who assumes AI-for-science needs a cluster. Training rollouts run on a one seventh MIG slice of an H200 with a one hour budget. The scale-up eval uses 8x B300 for 8 hours, and the 8 hour number was chosen for a prosaic reason: that is roughly when the model exhausts its 256k context. No compaction. That is a hard ceiling and a clear place where a context management improvement translates directly into longer horizon capability.

On non-replicable papers, Hughes is candid that they dodged the problem. They deliberately chose highly cited papers on the assumption that replication failures would already be known, and the judge rewards process over output. He names the unsolved version: how do you build a judge that rewards an agent for correctly determining a paper is *not* replicable, without creating a reward hack where the agent gives up on hard papers. He also names the subtle cheating modes they have not yet audited for, specifically optimal stopping (cutting an experiment the moment the result looks good, which invalidates the statistics) and running 20 environments while reporting the one that worked.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The claims check out on the record. The paper is "Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research," published by Inherent Labs in August 2026, covered by TechCrunch on 22 August. The 310 tasks from 100 papers, the 27B Qwen base, the GPT-5.5 Codex tool, and the wins over Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 are all as stated in the interview. The video is Hughes describing his own published work accurately, which is a lower bar than it sounds.

The caveat the interview does not surface is the important one: as of now there is no independent evaluation on Replica and no Faraday run on a public benchmark such as MLE-Bench. The outperformance claim rests entirely on Inherent's own scoring of Inherent's own 310 task suite with an LLM judge that Inherent designed and validated against an expert network Inherent assembled. That is not an accusation of bad faith, and Hughes volunteers that they sent replications to original paper authors who found no cheating. But a rubric judge tuned by the same team that trained the policy is exactly the setup where Goodharting is hardest to detect from the inside, and Hughes says himself that he expects some cheating is still happening. Treat the headline number as a strong internal result, not a settled benchmark ranking, until someone external reruns it.

The per turn credit assignment idea is convergent rather than unique, which is a point in its favour. Independent 2026 work is arriving at the same place: Turn-PPO on turn level credit assignment, MURPHY on feedback-aware GRPO with retrospective credit assignment for multi-turn code generation, and "Drowning in Routine: Signal Dilution in Multi-Turn Agent Training," whose title states the exact failure mode Hughes describes. Separately, "Steer, Don't Solve: Training Small Critic Models for Large Code Agents" is the same architectural bet as coding-agent-as-tool arrived at from the critic side. When four groups independently land on "trajectory level reward dilutes signal on long horizons, and a small trained supervisor beats a prompted large one," that is a real finding rather than one lab's trick.

The three month harness half life claim is the one I would push back on. It is presented as an empirical regularity from an unpublished conference workshop analysis, with no public writeup to check. It is plausible and it matches the general direction of travel, but three months is a suspiciously round number for a claim doing that much strategic work in justifying a company's entire research bet.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
The direct transfer to your routing work is the control inversion. Most routing systems you would build treat the small model as a gate that fires once per query. Faraday treats it as a persistent supervisor across a long rollout, with the authority to interrupt. That is a strictly larger action space, and the result says it is worth the extra small-model tokens. Concretely: take an existing cascade where a small model routes to a large one, and add mid-generation intervention. Let the small model see the large model's partial output and decide continue, restart with a modified prompt, or stop and accept. The cost is one extra small-model forward pass per checkpoint, and the interpretability result says the value concentrates exactly there.

Second, the credit assignment fix is directly portable to anything you post-train for multi-step inference decisions. If you are training a router or a compression-policy selector with GRPO and it collapses after initial progress, the diagnosis to check first is signal dilution: you are spending gradient on steps the base model already handles. Emit a per step weight from your reward model, normalize by tokens per step so verbose steps do not dominate, and reweight advantages. This is a small patch to a GRPO implementation, not a new algorithm.

Third, and cheapest to act on tomorrow: the redacted figure task is a free evaluation harness for your own domain. Take ten inference optimization papers you care about, vLLM scheduling, GPTQ variants, MoE routing, redact the main results plot, and run your candidate agent under a fixed one hour and single GPU slice budget. You get a rigour signal on your own subject matter rather than a generic coding benchmark, and it tells you which model to trust when you ask it to reproduce a compression result before you commit engineering time to it.
