# Building GTM AI Agents: Lessons from Deploying to 6,000 Users | Sait Izmit, Snowflake

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DrTdD-ttjCY

## TL;DR
Snowflake's internal go-to-market assistant, built on Snowflake CoWork and launched September 2025, has answered over 1.2 million questions at roughly 40,000 per week across 6,000 sales users. Izmit's thesis is that these projects almost never fail on model quality, they fail on trust economics and activation. His operating rules: pick quality over coverage (answer 50 questions at 95% rather than 100 at 70%), phase the launch, budget 60 to 70% of your own time on change management after GA, and accept that 30 to 40% of every sprint is re-architecting against technology that shipped last month.

## Key Takeaways
- **Trust is asymmetric and that dictates scope.** A free-form chatbot gets judged on the first five questions. If a user dislikes what they see, winning them back costs ten times more than the first impression did. So deliberately restrict the answerable surface rather than maximizing recall.
- **The eval set precedes the system.** Before touching the existing agent, Izmit wrote 150 questions straight off the sales process into a spreadsheet. Engineering objected that the data was not connected. That was the point: it measured the real distribution of user intent, and it scored 50% accuracy. 60% of the data in the agent today was added after launch, driven by that gap analysis.
- **Phased rollout as a trust-preservation mechanism.** Pilot proves accuracy with AI-native volunteers. A 10% beta (600 people) proves you have an MVP by revealing where data requests concentrate, and gates on retention (they exited beta at over 70% weekly-active return rate). Only then GA.
- **The collapsing wow factor.** Delight decays into baseline within months. The observed adoption ladder is: talk to your data, then automate my workflows (via MCP integrations), then team-level tool democratization (users building their own skills, dashboards, alerts), then hyper-personalization. Stop at stage one and you get displaced in a month or two because switching is now overnight-cheap.
- **Ship on today's stack, expect to rewrite.** Launch config to 6,000 users was a nine-page agent instruction file, a couple of Cortex Analyst semantic views, one Cortex Search service, and version control by Google Doc. Everything since (CI/CD, eval infra with unit and routing tests, a skill library, MCP orchestration, progressive disclosure, user memory, task scheduling, Slack surface) was added post-launch. Only 20% of the original architecture diagram survived.
- **Logs are the highest-leverage asset.** LLM-classified logs across 1.2M questions give a real-time topic and subtopic breakdown, which surfaces feature gaps, quality failures (users swearing at the agent or repeating a question), sales enablement gaps that previously required interviewing 100 sellers a week, and cross-team account overlap for matchmaking.
- **Current scale of the agent:** 15 semantic views, 85 tables, 3,000 columns, five to six MCP connections, close to 20 skills. Agent instructions hit a context ceiling, which is what forced progressive disclosure.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
Two things here map directly onto Amit's work. First, the agent-instruction context ceiling and the progressive-disclosure fix. Izmit hit a hard wall where business processes could no longer fit in the instruction block, and the resolution was to stop shipping all instructions in the prompt and instead disclose them on demand via a skill library. That is exactly the context-budget problem: instructions are a fixed per-request prefix tax paid on all 40,000 weekly queries, and most of it is irrelevant to any given query. Progressive disclosure converts a static prefix into a retrieved, query-conditioned payload. The optimization framing is prefix-cache friendliness versus token count: a static nine-page prefix caches perfectly but wastes attention over irrelevant content, while a dynamic skill payload cuts tokens but fragments the KV cache. The right design keeps a small stable prefix (cacheable) and appends a query-conditioned skill slice.

Second, "routing tests" as a named eval category alongside unit tests is a signal worth taking seriously. With 15 semantic views, 85 tables, and 20 skills, the dominant failure mode is not generation quality but tool and view selection. That is a classifier problem, and it is measurable and cheap to fix independently of the generator. This is the same decomposition Amit uses in LLM routing work: separate the router's accuracy metric from the downstream model's quality metric, because a 5% routing error caps end-to-end accuracy no matter how good the generator is.

The 95%-on-50-questions over 70%-on-100 rule is also a coverage-versus-confidence calibration argument. The engineering implementation is an abstention policy: the agent must know its answerable set and refuse cleanly outside it. Abstention is the cheapest quality lever in any deployed system, and it is almost always underused.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Izmit's closing answer holds up precisely. Snowflake Intelligence was renamed CoWork on June 2, 2026 at Snowflake Summit, and Cortex Code became CoCo at the same time. The rename was not cosmetic. Snowflake Intelligence had been one component inside Cortex AI, a conversational interface over governed data. CoWork is now a top-level product at ai.snowflake.com that runs multi-step Deep Research across the data estate, generates artifacts (decks, PDFs), and takes action in Gmail, Jira, Slack, and Salesforce through MCP, with multi-step workflows packaged as reusable Skills (public preview). That expansion is a vendor-side confirmation of exactly the adoption ladder Izmit describes, talk-to-data giving way to automate-my-workflows giving way to user-built skills, which suggests Snowflake productized the internal learning curve his team went through as customer zero.

The stated numbers are self-reported and unaudited, and the "2x efficiency" style claims common in this genre are absent here, which is to Izmit's credit. The one soft spot: 70% weekly retention among a population under active management pressure from sales leadership (he describes shaming managers and recruiting sponsorship) is not clean voluntary retention. Adoption driven that hard is real usage but it is not evidence of product-market fit in the way an unmanaged 70% would be.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Write the eval set before the system, from the process not the data.** Izmit's 150-question spreadsheet, authored in deliberate ignorance of what the system could answer, is the single most transferable move in this talk. For any routing or compression project, enumerate the query distribution first and accept the embarrassing baseline number, because it tells you where to spend.
- **Split evals into unit tests and routing tests.** For Amit's routing work, formalize this: measure router selection accuracy independently of end-task quality, and report the ceiling that routing error imposes on end-to-end accuracy. That decomposition is publishable and it is what most routing papers gloss over.
- **Treat the system prompt as a cost line item.** Audit the fixed prefix on high-QPS agents. Measure tokens paid per request for instructions that are irrelevant to the query, then test a small-stable-prefix plus retrieved-skill-slice design and report both the token delta and the prefix-cache hit-rate delta. That is a concrete inference-cost win with a clean before-and-after.
- **Build an abstention policy, then measure coverage separately from accuracy.** Report accuracy-on-answered and coverage as two numbers, never one blended accuracy. This is the quality-over-coverage rule made quantitative.
- **Instrument logs for classification from day one.** LLM-classified query logs at 40,000 per week are effectively a free, continuously updating eval distribution. For a research workload, the equivalent is classifying which query types get routed to expensive models, which is exactly the data needed to justify a router.
