# Building GTM AI Agents: Lessons from Deploying to 6,000 Users (Sait Izmit, Snowflake)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DrTdD-ttjCY

## TL;DR
Snowflake's internal go-to-market assistant, built on Snowflake Cowork (formerly Snowflake Intelligence), has answered over 1.2 million questions at roughly 40,000 per week across 6,000 sales users. The thesis is blunt: these projects almost never fail on model quality, they fail on trust and adoption. Izmit's operating rules are quality over coverage, phased launch, aggressive change management, and treating your query logs as the primary product-development instrument. Roughly 30 to 40% of every sprint goes to re-architecting on top of whatever primitive shipped that month.

## Key Takeaways
- **Quality over coverage, non-negotiable.** Answer 50 questions at 95% accuracy rather than 100 at 70%. Rationale: a free-form chatbot gets judged on the user's first five questions, and winning a burned user back costs 10x. Trust is earned slowly and lost overnight.
- **Write the eval set before you touch the agent.** Izmit's first act on joining was to sit with the sales process and hand-write 150 questions sellers would actually ask, over engineering's objection that the data was not connected yet. First run scored 50% accuracy. That spreadsheet became the roadmap.
- **60% of the data was added after launch.** Current system: 15 semantic views, 85 tables, 3,000 columns, five to six MCP connections, close to 20 skills. It launched with a nine-page agent-instruction doc versioned in a Google Doc, a couple of Cortex Analyst semantic views, and one Cortex Search service. Ship, then learn.
- **Phased rollout maps to distinct questions.** Pilot proves accuracy with AI-native volunteers. Beta at 10% (600 people) proves you have an MVP, revealed by where data requests concentrate, and proves retention (they exited beta at over 70% weekly-active return rate). Only then GA.
- **Change management is the real failure mode.** Two weeks post-GA, leadership panics at low numbers while only 20% of the org has tried the product at all. Izmit spends 60 to 70% of his time in sales meetings, demos, and adoption dashboards, publicly praising high-adoption managers. Without that, he estimates they would be at half their current usage.
- **The collapsing wow factor.** Once "talk to your data" becomes habit, it becomes baseline, and users defect to whatever is newer. The escalation ladder he observes: talk to your data, then automate my workflows (via MCP), then let teams build their own skills and apps, then hyper-personalization. Stall on step one and you are disrupted within a month or two.
- **Stop shopping for the perfect architecture.** Enterprises that spend nine months evaluating frameworks lose to teams shipping in weeks. Accept permanent re-architecting as a line item.
- **Logs are the goldmine.** LLM-classified query logs give real-time topic and subtopic breakdowns, surface exactly where the agent fails or where users repeat themselves and swear at it, and replace the old ritual of interviewing 100 sellers a week to find enablement gaps. They close the loop by generating battle cards from Confluence, Jira, PRDs, and Slack, then feeding them back into the agent. Log analysis also enables cross-team matchmaking when two teams are approaching the same account blind to each other.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **Progressive disclosure as a context-window strategy.** They outgrew agent instructions twice. First business processes moved out into a skill library, then MCP orchestration instructions blew the instruction budget again, so instructions are now progressively disclosed rather than loaded wholesale. This is the practical answer to context bloat: hierarchical retrieval of instructions, not a bigger prompt.
- **Semantic views as the accuracy substrate.** 3,000 columns of raw schema is unusable for text-to-SQL. The semantic layer encodes business logic, verified SQL patterns, and metadata so the model resolves ambiguity structurally instead of guessing. Accuracy here is a data-modeling problem, not a prompting problem.
- **Eval infrastructure is stratified.** Unit tests on individual tool outputs plus separate routing tests that check whether the agent picked the right tool or semantic view. Routing errors and generation errors need different fixtures. That distinction is directly reusable.
- **Log classification at scale without breaking the bank.** 40,000 questions a week classified by LLM is itself a cost-optimization problem, and exactly the workload where a cheap classifier or a small quantized model beats a frontier call. Izmit notes the data science team enjoys this problem, which is a tell that it is a real one.
- **RBAC inheritance from the data layer.** Because all first-party, third-party, Salesforce, and call-transcript data lands in Snowflake first, agents inherit existing role-based access controls rather than reimplementing authorization in the agent tier.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The public record corroborates the talk closely. Snowflake's own engineering write-up on the GTM AI Assistant reports over 4,600 GTM users, weekly usage consistently above 65%, and more than 35,000 questions per week, which brackets the 6,000-user and 40,000-question figures Izmit quotes from a later vantage point. The naming is confirmed too: Snowflake Intelligence was rebranded Snowflake Cowork around the mid-2026 summit, which is why searching the old name surfaces the older blog posts. One architectural detail has already moved since the talk: as of the April 13, 2026 release, Cortex Agents using Cortex Analyst semantic views generate SQL directly rather than delegating to Cortex Analyst as a separate service hop, which Snowflake reports as both an accuracy and a latency win. That is a concrete instance of Izmit's "you will constantly re-architect" point, and it means anyone copying his original architecture should skip the extra hop. Worth noting the counterweight the vendor blogs do not emphasize: independent analyses of Cortex Analyst flag real limits on complex multi-hop analytical questions, which is consistent with Izmit conceding that roughly 20% of questions remain too strategic for the system.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Write the 150-question eval set before building anything.** This is the single most transferable move in the talk and it applies verbatim to routing work: enumerate the query classes a router must handle, hand-label the correct model tier for each, and measure before optimizing. Amit should treat a routing-decision eval set as a prerequisite artifact, not a follow-up.
- **Split routing evals from output evals.** Snowflake tests tool selection separately from answer generation. A router should be scored on "did it pick the cheapest model that met the quality bar", independent of whether that model's answer was good. Conflating the two hides which component regressed.
- **Apply progressive disclosure to system prompts.** If any internal agent has a monolithic instruction block, the fix is hierarchical: a thin router prompt that pulls in only the relevant skill or policy document. This directly cuts prefill tokens per call, which is the cheapest inference win available and needs no model changes.
- **Use a small quantized classifier for log mining.** Classifying tens of thousands of queries per week is the canonical case for distillation. Label a few thousand examples with a frontier model, distill into a small classifier, and run it continuously. That is Amit's day job pointed at his own telemetry.
- **Budget 30% of capacity for re-architecting, explicitly.** Not as slippage, as a planned allocation.
