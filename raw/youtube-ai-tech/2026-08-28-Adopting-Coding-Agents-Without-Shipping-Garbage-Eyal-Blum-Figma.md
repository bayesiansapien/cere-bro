# How to Get Your Org to Adopt Coding Agents (Without Shipping Garbage) (Eyal Blum, Figma)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Bn0xro2ol8

## TL;DR
Blum's three-act model of AI adoption: act one, you pick up a tool and simple things work 10x better. Act two, you apply the same practices to bigger problems, get bad code and bugs, and the trust you built collapses. Act three, you learn the actual skill of guardrails, context, and prompting. Adoption is uneven, so teams in different acts have to ship a product together, and the organisational problem is getting everyone to act three without pretending act two did not happen. His three most useful contributions are the inversion on skeptics (your most senior engineers are the slowest adopters because they can see every failure, so make them own the safety roadmap rather than trying to convert them), attention-aware communication (mark what you wrote versus what the AI wrote, because human attention is now the scarce resource), and plan-first development, where a week spent on a detailed plan yields overnight implementation and restores the craft satisfaction that prompt-cycling destroys.

## Key Takeaways
- **The three friction points he names are all human, not technical.** Reduced developer agency killing job satisfaction, because pride in writing code and getting into flow has been replaced by waiting on output and talking to a model. Best engineers becoming bottlenecks. And communication inflation: design docs and Slack messages three to four times longer, two to three times as many emails, conveying the same information, with the old signals of quality and importance no longer readable.
- **The best-engineer paradox is the sharpest observation in the talk.** The engineers holding all the institutional context in their heads are the ones who know where every pitfall is. They hold together with "mental duct tape" all the places agents fail, they catch the genuinely bad output, and consequently they carry the most burden, become bottlenecks, get frustrated, and adopt slowest. They are not resisting out of ignorance. They are the only people seeing the failure rate honestly.
- **The inversion: skepticism is a roadmap, not an obstacle.** Their objections are precisely a list of where verification is missing and where tooling fails. Put them in charge of making AI safe in the organisation rather than trying to make them use it. They will come along once the improvements they built make their own lives better, and they will not be shy about telling you what to fix.
- **Verification is the highest-value investment in the codebase.** Every time something moves from requiring a human to being agent-verifiable, that is the win. His concrete example: when Playwright plus MCP arrived, agents could explore the application instead of humans navigating it, which unlocked productivity across teams.
- **Encode discovered behaviour into deterministic flows.** When you find something the agent reliably does well, convert it into a repeatable deterministic step. Saves tokens, saves time, and reserves the LLM for the parts that actually need reasoning.
- **TDD ordering measurably improves agent output.** Have the agent write the test first and work red-green, because if it writes code first and tests afterwards it will fit the test to the code rather than fitting the code to the verification criteria. This is a one-line process change with a real quality effect.
- **The review pyramid mirrors the testing pyramid.** Push everything possible down into deterministic analysis (linting, compiler, unit tests). Above that, agent review against encodable criteria such as architectural standards. Reserve human review for the top: is this the right functionality, is this the right thing to build.
- **Plan-first restores craft and delivers the speedup.** It is not unusual to spend a week writing a detailed plan, making every decision, iterating, and circulating it for teammate review, then hand it to the agent as a mechanical implementation. His worked example: a plan that took a week to write plus a week aligning with three other teams, sent to an agent overnight, returned as roughly 20 PRs ranging from 10 to 100 lines. Six weeks of coding work in one week including the review cycle, so roughly 5x.
- **What makes a good plan.** Start with a "why" as a bold executive summary, which prevents agent drift and stops the agent from revising the goal because it feels like it. Break into small parts, each independently verifiable, each with a validation gate or acceptance criteria so phase four is not built on unvalidated phase one assumptions. His sizing heuristic is excellent: if the corresponding PR is big enough that you would want a coffee before reading it, break it down further.
- **Do not centralise on one workflow.** Diminishing returns. Anyone can set up the loop that suits them as long as others can iterate with them.
- **Attention-aware communication, with a real cost paid to learn it.** Mark AI-generated versus human-written text so readers know how much attention to spend and how much slop to expect. His team's convention: every PR description opens with a short hand-written summary of what the change does, followed by the AI description. Blum learned this by sending an AI-generated PR-comment analysis to senior skeptics without marking it, and being told they did not expect someone they respected to send something so obviously sloppy. He apologised and changed the convention.
- **Let people use AI where they already are.** The single highest-leverage adoption tactic is tagging an agent in a Slack thread alongside a colleague and letting it close the loop in-thread. Non-confrontational, and a good experience there leads people to try it themselves elsewhere.
- **He does not claim to be finished.** Automation story not fully there, still working out how to use agents effectively given dependencies in older systems. Fifteen years in the valley and he calls this the biggest cultural and technological change by orders of magnitude.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **The verification pyramid is a cost-tiering scheme, and that is the right way to read it.** Linting and compilation cost approximately nothing per run. Agent review costs tokens. Human review costs the scarcest resource in the organisation. Pushing checks downward is straightforward cost optimization, and it improves latency because deterministic checks return in milliseconds while an agent review round-trip takes minutes.
- **Deterministic encoding of discovered behaviour is the same move as caching a hot path.** Once you know the answer, stop paying a model to rediscover it. Every LLM call replaced by a script is a permanent reduction in both token spend and variance. This is the most underused optimization in agentic systems, because teams keep paying for reasoning on solved problems.
- **TDD ordering works because it fixes the reward specification before generation.** Test-after lets the model optimise against a target it authored, which is reward hacking in miniature. Test-first pins the objective outside the model's control. The lesson generalises to any generate-and-verify pipeline: the verifier must not be produced by the generator.
- **Plan-first is cheap iteration on the expensive decisions.** Editing a document costs a few thousand tokens and a few minutes of human attention. Editing an implementation spread across a codebase costs a full regeneration plus a full review. Front-loading decisions into a reviewable artifact is the same principle as compile-time versus runtime error detection.
- **"Why" at the top as drift prevention.** In a long agent run, the goal statement competes with accumulating intermediate context. An explicit, prominent, protected objective statement is effectively an anchor against context dilution, and instructing the agent not to revise it closes the loop where a model rationalises a changed goal to match what it already built.
- **Independent per-phase validation gates bound error propagation.** Without them, an unvalidated early phase silently becomes an assumption for everything downstream, and the debugging cost is superlinear in the number of phases. Same reasoning as checkpointing a long training run.
- **Authorship marking is a signal-to-noise intervention on a genuinely fixed resource.** Compute scales, human review attention does not. Making provenance explicit lets readers allocate scrutiny proportionally, which is the only lever available once volume has tripled.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Blum's framing of Figma as an AI-first company is consistent with what the company shipped in 2026. At Config 2026 Figma announced code layers, bringing executable code directly onto the design canvas so teams can clone repositories and extract flows from code into design layers, explicitly collapsing the design-to-development handoff. They also shipped a Figma Design Agent, opened the canvas to external agents, launched generative plugin creation, and released the Figma MCP server in beta so Claude Code, Codex, and other MCP clients can generate and modify design assets against a design system. Figma's own third annual AI and Design Report found respondents six times more likely than two years ago to say AI significantly changed how they collaborate rather than just individual productivity, which is precisely the shift Blum is describing internally.

Two honest notes. First, the 5x figure is a single self-reported instance from one engineer's own plan, and it includes the two weeks of planning and cross-team alignment in the numerator's baseline while attributing the compression to the agent. That is a defensible way to count, but it is one data point and it is not a controlled comparison. Read against METR's finding that developers overestimated their AI speedup by roughly 39 percentage points, treat any single-instance multiplier with caution. What is more credible than the number is the mechanism: the work that compressed was mechanical implementation of already-made decisions, which is exactly the class of work that should compress.

Second, the friction points he lists are more consequential than the productivity claim and are underreported elsewhere. Communication inflation in particular is a genuine organisational tax that nobody has measured, and his response, provenance marking, is a norm rather than a tool, which means it can be adopted immediately by anyone at zero cost. The anecdote about being told his own AI-generated analysis was insultingly sloppy is the most instructive thing in the talk, and it is to his credit that he tells it against himself.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Adopt provenance marking in your own writing, starting with the next PR or design doc.** Two or three hand-written sentences at the top stating what this is and what it does, then the generated detail below. This costs a minute and it tells reviewers where to spend scrutiny. Given how much research and optimization work now involves generated analysis, this is the highest-value zero-cost norm in the entire batch of talks.
- **Flip the test-writing order for every agent task.** Specify the acceptance check before the implementation, always. For Amit's work that means writing the accuracy-delta assertion and the latency threshold *first*, then asking the agent to make them pass. Otherwise the agent will produce a benchmark that its own kernel passes, which is precisely reward hacking on a metric you were relying on.
- **Give the loudest skeptic on your team ownership of the verification roadmap.** This is the organisational recommendation worth acting on. The senior engineer who does not trust agents on kernel or quantization work has, in their head, a precise list of every place the tooling lacks validation. Ask for that list and fund it. It is both the correct engineering backlog and the only route to their buy-in.
- **Convert every reliably-working agent behaviour into a script.** Audit which agent tasks now succeed consistently and replace each with deterministic code. Every one removed is a permanent cut to token spend and to variance, and this is the specific discipline that keeps agentic pipeline costs from growing without bound.
- **Use the coffee test for task decomposition.** If the resulting change is too large to review in one sitting, it is too large to hand to an agent as one unit. This is a simpler and better heuristic than token counts or line limits.
- **Write the "why" as a protected header in every plan or steering file**, and instruct the agent not to revise it. Cheapest available defence against long-run goal drift.
- **Expect and plan for act two.** The trust collapse when agents fail on a large problem is predictable, and pretending it will not happen is how teams end up permanently in act one. Say out loud that the first big attempt will go badly, so that when it does, it reads as expected rather than as proof the whole approach is broken.
