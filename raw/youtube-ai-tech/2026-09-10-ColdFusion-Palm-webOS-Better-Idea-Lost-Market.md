# Before the Foldable, Apple Copied This Phone

**Channel:** ColdFusion
**Published:** 2026-09-10
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0Hvt-xhS65k

## TL;DR
Palm's webOS shipped card-based multitasking and edge-swipe gestures at CES in January 2009, eighteen months before the iPhone could run two apps at once and eight years before iOS 7 and the iPhone X made those gestures the default navigation for the entire industry. The technology was demonstrably first and demonstrably better. Palm was sold, dismembered, and gone inside two and a half years. The distribution stack beat the product: Palm launched on Sprint alone, opened its store with **18 apps** against Apple's tens of thousands, ran advertising that never showed a feature, and put a software vision on hardware its budget could not support. Steve Jobs stated the actual mechanism in writing to Palm's CEO in 2007: "I'm sure you realize the asymmetry in the financial resources of our respective companies." webOS survived anyway. LG bought the corpse in 2013 to fix its terrible smart TVs, and it now runs on over 130 million of them.

## Key Takeaways
- **The invention was real and the lead was enormous.** Matias Duarte's card switcher demoed January 2009. iOS did not get a card-style switcher until iOS 7 in 2013, and even then it required a double-click of the home button. The gesture itself waited for iPhone X in 2017. Palm was roughly a decade early on the interaction billions now perform daily.
- **Apple's public response was a legal threat, which is the tell.** Two weeks after CES, Tim Cook warned that "if others rip off our intellectual property, we will go after them," and refused to name the Pre when asked directly. Silicon Valley had already written Palm off. Apple had not.
- **The Jobs-Colligan exchange is the most instructive artefact in the story.** Jobs proposed a mutual no-hire agreement in August 2007 and implied a patent suit if Palm refused. Colligan refused **in writing**, telling Jobs the proposal was probably illegal. He was right, and that correspondence later became evidence in the tech industry's anti-poaching antitrust litigation.
- **Palm's original method was the block of wood, and abandoning it was the beginning of the end.** Jeff Hawkins carried a whittled wooden block and a chopstick stylus in his shirt pocket for weeks, pretending to check a calendar, before a line of code was written. The Palm Pilot did four things extremely well instead of forty badly, and outsold the Sony Walkman's launch pace.
- **The organisational wound preceded the market one.** In 1998 Hawkins, Dubinsky, and Colligan asked 3Com to spin Palm out. Refused. They left, founded Handspring, licensed Palm's own OS, and took roughly 20% market share almost instantly. Palm handed its founders to a competitor it created, then bought them back years later.
- **Palm peaked at a $53B valuation in March 2000, above GM and McDonald's, and lost 98% of it by 2002.** The dot-com bubble burst eight days after the IPO.
- **Colligan's November 2006 dismissal of Apple is the industry's canonical famous-last-words.** PC makers "are not just going to walk in." Seven weeks later Jobs walked in with the iPhone.
- **The execution failures were all distribution, not engineering.** One carrier with the weakest reach. Developer tools locked away for months so the catalogue opened at 18 apps. Ads about a phone that "reads my mind" that demonstrated nothing. Hardware too slow for the software, because the OS was built for the phone they wanted and the phone was built for the budget they had.
- **The asset outlived every company that owned it.** HP bought Palm for $1.2B in April 2010 and killed webOS **49 days** after the TouchPad launched, announced in the same briefing as a $10B enterprise acquisition. Apotheker was fired 35 days later. LG bought the remains in 2013 for undisclosed terms.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The technical decision that made webOS both remarkable and doomed was writing the entire interface in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on a Linux kernel in 2008. The strategic logic was sound and ahead of its time: any web developer on Earth becomes a potential app developer, which is a direct assault on the platform's hardest problem, developer supply. The execution cost was that a web rendering layer on 2009 ARM hardware could not hold frame rate, and the cards stuttered. Palm chose the abstraction that maximised future developer throughput and paid for it in present-day latency on the only hardware it could afford. That is a recognisable tradeoff and the lesson is not that the abstraction was wrong, it is that **an abstraction whose cost lands on hardware you do not control is a bet on someone else's roadmap arriving before your runway ends**. It did not.

The broader pattern is the one Amit's wiki already has from a different angle in [Why The Harness Matters More Than The Model](2026-09-07-YC-Paper-Club-Why-The-Harness-Matters-More-Than-The-Model.md), and the two videos are the same claim separated by seventeen years. Palm had the better model. Apple had the better harness: carrier deals with AT&T, retail stores, an App Store with tens of thousands of SKUs, a developer toolchain that shipped on time, and the balance sheet to absorb a bad quarter. The core artifact lost to everything wrapped around it. For optimization work this is the standing correction to a bias most researchers carry: the measured superiority of a technique on its own axis is close to uncorrelated with whether it gets adopted, because adoption is decided by the surrounding system. A routing method that needs a bespoke serving stack loses to a worse method that drops into vLLM unmodified. An inference kernel that requires a custom compiler loses to a slower one that runs under torch.compile today.

The webOS-to-LG third act is the part worth studying hardest, because it is the rare case where the invention won on a completely different substrate. Duarte's card interface was designed for one-handed thumb interaction on a phone, and it turned out to be near-optimal for a glanceable screen driven from a couch by a remote. **The design was right; the market was wrong; the design survived by finding the market it was accidentally correct for.** The transferable read for research work: when a technique underperforms in its intended setting, the disciplined next question is which regime its inductive bias actually suits, not whether to abandon it. Structured sparsity that disappoints on dense GPU inference may be exactly right for an edge NPU with different memory economics. The technique was not wrong, the deployment target was.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
ColdFusion's framing device checks out and is better-timed than the video makes clear. Apple unveiled the **iPhone Duo** on 9 September 2026, its first foldable, opening from a 5.4-inch exterior display into a 7.6-inch internal one that Apple describes as its largest iPhone display ever, with 50% more screen area than the iPhone 18 Pro Max. It starts at **$1,999 for 256GB** and ships 23 October 2026. So the video's opening premise, millions upgrading without a thought for competitors, lands against a product Apple shipped years after Samsung established the category, which quietly reinforces the thesis rather than undercutting it. Apple is once again not first and once again likely to take the market.

The webOS afterlife is more successful than the video's melancholy tone suggests, and the numbers are worth having. LG's webOS now powers **over 130 million smart TVs** with roughly a **16% smart TV platform share**, and LG holds 15.2% of global TV revenue, second to Samsung, plus a 52.4% share of OLED for the twelfth consecutive year. More pointed: LG generates approximately **$2.27 billion from webOS TV data and subscription revenue**. The operating system that could not sell a phone became a high-margin advertising and data business attached to hardware. That reframes the story's ending considerably. Palm's software did not merely survive as a curiosity, it became a platform monetised through exactly the mechanism, owning the interface layer and harvesting what flows through it, that Palm failed to achieve on phones.

Which makes ColdFusion's own aside the most important caveat in the video, and he undersells it. He notes recent webOS TVs are laggy, ad-filled, and that LG TVs have been found spying on users. That is not a footnote, it is the resolution: the $2.27B is *from* the ads and the data. The elegant, user-respecting interface that reviewers praised in 2009 now funds itself by surveilling the living room. The idea won. What it was made to serve did not survive the transition.

One correction to the video's implicit narrative. It presents Palm's death as primarily a distribution failure, which is mostly right, but under-weights that Palm OS had not been meaningfully rebuilt in years before webOS, that the company split into PalmOne and PalmSource in 2003, lost the rights to its own name, sold its OS to Access, and spent until 2006 buying itself back. webOS was a sixteen-month sprint by a team assembled in late 2007 to replace a decade of deferred platform work. It was never a fair fight on time, and the CES demo's brilliance obscures that Palm was executing a rescue, not a strategy.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
**Run the harness audit on your own work.** Take the optimization technique you are currently most confident in and score it separately on two axes: measured advantage on its own benchmark, and integration cost into the stack a team would actually deploy on. If the second number is high, the first number will not save you, and Palm is the proof. Concretely, before investing further in any routing or compression method, write down what a colleague would have to change in their existing vLLM or TensorRT-LLM deployment to adopt it. If the answer involves a custom kernel, a forked serving layer, or a non-standard checkpoint format, budget as much effort for the harness as for the method, or expect the same outcome.

**Apply the wrong-substrate question to your own dead projects.** Pull up one technique you shelved because it underperformed. Instead of asking whether it can be improved, ask which deployment regime its inductive bias is actually suited to: different memory bandwidth, different batch size, different latency budget, edge rather than datacentre. Duarte's card switcher was not a failed phone interface, it was an unrecognised TV interface. At least one shelved result probably has an LG waiting for it.

**Adopt the block of wood.** Hawkins' rule was proof of use before a line of code. The research equivalent is a cheap mock of the end-to-end system before optimising any component of it: stub the router with a hardcoded policy and measure whether the cost curve even moves, before building the learned version. Most optimization effort is spent making a component faster inside a system whose bottleneck is elsewhere, and a week of carrying a wooden block would have revealed that.
