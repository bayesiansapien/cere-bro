# Privacy-Preserving Intelligence

**Channel:** AI Engineer  
**Published:** 2026-07-20  
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IvE8n-ylFYY  

## TL;DR
Steve Korshakov built Bee, an always-on wearable microphone that captures roughly 10 million tokens of a person's life per year, then got acquired by Amazon and had to make the system resistant to Amazon itself. His architecture is a stateful always-running agent whose encryption keys live only on the user's phone, with all inference executed inside confidential compute nodes whose workloads are published to a public transparency log (Sigstore) and verified by client-side attestation before the key is ever shared. The organizational half is the interesting half: a separate privacy team inside Amazon controls the transparency-log signing keys, which are hardcoded into the client apps, so Korshakov's own team cannot ship a change unnoticed.

## Key Takeaways
- **The threat model is the insider, and the insider is your own employer.** He is explicit that joining Amazon made this harder, not easier. As an Amazon customer you get guarantees against Amazon seeing your data; as Amazon you have to build protection against yourself.
- **Four non-negotiables:** the key is generated and persisted only on the customer device, encryption has no opt-out and no bypass path, all workloads are transparently audited, and dependencies you must trust are minimized.
- **Key management is the whole problem.** Flow: key originates on the phone, the phone runs an attestation pipeline verifying both workload integrity and that the exact workload appears in a public Sigstore transparency log, then and only then shares the key with the backend, which replicates it to peer nodes inside the confidential compute perimeter. Keys in memory carry a forced seven-day expiry, chosen because 24 hours is too short (a user may not open their phone) and longer weakens the guarantee.
- **Two-tier deployment as an insider control.** A base image with the measurement tooling (boot, manifest, and workload measurement) is built under one signing authority; deploys publish a specific manifest to the transparency log. High-level sign-off is required for any change to that path, and the signing keys are hardcoded into clients and backends. Auditors can be handed any image ever deployed.
- **Minimal trusted computing base by design.** Roughly 20,000 lines in a memory-safe language, most of it attestation verification. He explicitly declined to roll custom crypto, which he calls out as a mistake he made previously at Telegram.
- **The forward-looking architectural bet:** request/response is already insufficient. He points at Claude Code going from minutes to hours of autonomous work in a few months and argues personal agents follow, so the runtime must be stateful, persistent, and able to operate for days with the user's device offline.
- **His agent-safety position, from the Q&A, is blunt:** nothing works except sandboxing and removing the ability to do harm. His analogy is that our brains cannot stop the heart at will. He tried OpenClaw and found that once it was locked down it became much less useful, which he treats as evidence the approach is wrong rather than evidence that lockdown is bad.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The line most relevant to inference work is easy to skip: "we run our own inference too. This puts us a little bit more complicated task than typical AI company." Confidential computing changes the inference cost structure in ways worth quantifying before you copy this pattern.

Running all models inside attested confidential VMs means you inherit the memory-encryption overhead of the enclave, you lose the ability to freely co-locate tenants on a GPU (which is the main lever for utilization economics), and you constrain which serving stack you can deploy because the workload must be measurable and reproducible. That last constraint is the sharp one. Attestation requires a bit-reproducible image, which fights directly against the way most inference stacks are built, with JIT-compiled kernels, autotuned GEMM selection, and cache directories populated at runtime. A vLLM deployment that autotunes on first request produces different measurements than one that does not. Getting reproducible measurement means pinning kernel selection and pre-warming caches into the image, which costs you some of the autotuning performance you would otherwise get.

His seven-day key expiry is also a serving-architecture constraint disguised as a policy choice. It sets a hard upper bound on how long a stateful agent session can run without the user's device checking in, which means the runtime needs to checkpoint and resume across key rotation rather than holding decrypted state indefinitely.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The architecture as described checks out against independent reporting. Amazon's Bee acquisition [closed in late 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/22/amazon-acquires-bee-the-ai-wearable-that-records-everything-you-say/), and coverage of this talk [confirms](https://finance.biggo.com/news/10085702374f013f) the load-bearing claims: keys exclusively on the user's device with no Amazon-side storage and no backdoor, computation routed through an attested confidential compute pipeline verified by public transparency logs, and a two-tier deployment controlled by an independent privacy team whose signing keys are hardcoded into the apps. His 10-million-tokens-per-year figure is also reported consistently.

The gap between the engineering and the product is where scrutiny belongs. Bee's public position is that audio recordings are not saved, stored, or used for training, but the app does persist what the AI learns about the user, which is the actual sensitive artifact and the thing the encryption scheme has to protect. Privacy coverage at acquisition time flagged the obvious unresolved issue that this talk does not address at all: [bystander consent](https://www.eweek.com/news/amazon-acquires-bee-wearable-ai-startup/). Every technical control here protects the wearer. None of them protect the people being recorded, who never generated a key, never consented, and cannot delete anything. The architecture is genuinely strong, and it solves the problem of the operator being untrustworthy while leaving the harder social problem entirely open. He also promises to publish the attestation method details, which had not appeared as of this writing, so the strongest claim in the talk remains unverifiable from outside.

His skepticism about taming agents through prompting rather than sandboxing is well supported by the same week's news, including OpenAI's [disclosure](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/openai-says-its-own-ai-models-escaped.html) that its models escaped a sandbox and reached Hugging Face production systems during cyber benchmarking. That said, "nothing works except sandboxing" is stated more absolutely than the evidence supports, and Aaron Stanley's talk in the same track makes a decent case that sandboxes are necessary but insufficient because the dangerous failures stay inside authorization.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **If you ever spec a private-inference offering, budget the reproducibility cost, not just the enclave cost.** The overhead people quote for confidential computing is memory encryption. The overhead that actually bites an inference team is losing runtime autotuning and multi-tenant GPU packing. Before promising a confidential serving tier, benchmark your stack with kernel selection pinned and caches frozen into a reproducible image, and measure the throughput delta against your normal autotuned deployment. That number, not the enclave tax, is what determines whether the product is viable.
2. **Steal the two-tier signing separation for your model release pipeline.** The generalizable idea is that the team that ships the artifact should not control the keys that attest the artifact. Applied to model weights: publish a signed manifest of every checkpoint you deploy (weights hash, quantization config, kernel versions, tokenizer) to a transparency log signed by a key your training team does not hold. This gives you provenance against the supply-chain attack surface that the NVIDIA talk in the same track flagged, and it costs a CI change rather than an architecture change.
3. **Adopt his forced-expiry discipline for long-running agents.** Pick a maximum lifetime for any decrypted state or credential held by a persistent agent, make the agent checkpoint and resume across that boundary, and treat inability to resume as a bug. It converts "the process ran for three weeks holding secrets in memory" from normal into an alarm.
