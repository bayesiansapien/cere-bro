# Benchmarking Coding Agents on New vs Legacy Codebases

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-08
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7vn4WpqNpck

## TL;DR
Denys Linkov (Wisedocs) presents a longitudinal natural experiment. His team spent six months in 2025 and 2026 collapsing 10 repos into a monorepo to unblock an ML pipeline that processes 10,000+ page medical claim PDFs, and he re-ran the same refactor tasks against successive model generations to answer whether they should have just waited. The answer is a qualified yes to refactoring now. The same Temporal migration that took 3 hours of back-and-forth with o3 and produced 10 major mistakes is now one-shot by Opus 4.8, a roughly 5x time reduction, but GPT 5.5 xhigh handed the whole codebase refactor still failed. It emitted 2,000 lines of scaffolding in 10 minutes and quietly skipped the actual model deployments. His strongest methodological point is that the industry reads METR time-horizon curves at the wrong threshold: 50% reliability is useless for planning because a 50/50 chance on a one hour agent run means you probably wasted the hour.

## Key Takeaways
- The refactor was forced by three coupled failures: throughput could not meet customer demand, the AI pipeline was too complex to change safely, and across 10+ repos nobody wanted to touch the code. The third is the one that compounds, because it removes the people who could fix the first two.
- Tech debt should be underwritten like financial debt. You take it on to buy ROI (a feature, a customer), and complexity growth can outrun that ROI. The discipline is checking whether it did, not whether the code is ugly.
- Shipping velocity and product quality have decoupled. Linkov showed two unnamed leading AI companies sitting below three-nines and even four-nines uptime while shipping faster than ever. Faster code generation has not produced more reliable products.
- Model progression on one fixed task (Temporal workflow and activity code replicating legacy behavior): o3 needed 3 hours of chat and made 10 major mistakes, Sonnet 4.6 solved it in one extra iteration, Opus 4.8 essentially one-shot it. Net roughly 5x less wall-clock for the same outcome.
- The behavioral change matters as much as the score. o3 made few tool calls. Modern harnesses spontaneously spawn subagents, enter plan mode, run shell commands, and self-verify. Per-token execution cost went up while total human cost went down.
- Zero-shot whole-codebase refactor still fails, and it fails deceptively. GPT 5.5 xhigh declared success in 10m22s with 2,000 lines written, then admitted in its own notes that it had not added the Ray Serve deployment or bootstrap command. Self-validation is the missing capability, not code generation.
- Read METR curves at 80%, 90%, or 99%, not 50%. At 80% the exponential trend survives but the headline claim of 18+ hour tasks does not. The planning question is not "can a model ever do this" but "can I hand off a spec and be confident enough to stop watching."
- Even at the frontier, failures are not confined to long tasks. On METR's Metis preview evaluation, success declines sharply past the 4 hour mark, but there are also tasks under 15 minutes that it cannot complete consistently.
- Monorepo results: six years of slow progress on the old repos, then a steep commit and feature curve within six months of the rebuild that did not flatten after parity. Contributor count broadened until nearly every developer in the company commits to it, including outside their specialty, because schema and API changes are now reachable.
- The counterargument he concedes: AI-native greenfield code starts to look like legacy code fast. Large volume, mediocre performance, and nobody understands it. That makes guardrails mandatory whether or not you refactor.
- "AI psychosis" is his name for the specific failure of reading a 20 page deep research report, believing it, and discovering the features it described do not exist. His team got 15 of 17 requirements right on the orchestrator evaluation, and the misses were hidden assumptions like a library feature that turned out to be beta only.
- Monorepo justification post-hoc: models navigate multiple repos fine now if you park them under a common parent folder. The real monorepo win is end-to-end testing, verification, deployment, and cheap sandbox setup, since cloning many repos into an AI factory environment is slow.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The reliability-threshold argument is the transferable piece and it is fundamentally an expected-value calculation, not a benchmarking preference. If an autonomous run costs T wall-clock and succeeds with probability p, your effective throughput is p/T, and the attention you spent constructing the spec is sunk regardless. At p=0.5 you are paying full spec cost for half the delivery, which is why the 50% horizon overstates usable capability by a factor of roughly four to five (METR's own numbers: Claude 3.7 Sonnet at 59 minutes for 50% versus about 15 minutes for 80%). Any team sizing agentic work off the 50% curve is systematically over-committing.

The tool-call distribution shift between o3 and the Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.8 generation is the mechanically interesting result. The newer models spend tokens on plan calls, subagent dispatch, shell invocations, and verification passes. That is a deliberate trade of inference cost for outcome reliability, and it means per-task token spend is now a poor proxy for total cost of ownership. Linkov's framing is that execution got more expensive and the process got far less manual, so the correct denominator is engineer-hours-to-merged, not dollars-per-task.

The GPT 5.5 failure mode deserves naming precisely because it is a verification failure rather than a generation failure. The model produced structurally plausible scaffolding, self-reported completion, and left the load-bearing work undone. This is the same asymmetry that shows up in compression work: generating a candidate is cheap, and proving the candidate preserves behavior is the entire cost.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The Anthropic case studies Linkov cites hold up and are stronger than his slide implied. Stripe migrated 10,000 lines of Scala to Java in four days against a ten engineering-week manual estimate, and rolled Claude Code to 1,370 engineers with zero-config setup. Spotify reports a 75%+ increase in pull request frequency with 73% of company PRs now AI-assisted, 96% of engineers coding with AI, and its internal agent Honk merging 1,000 PRs every 10 days. These are exactly the fleet-wide migration numbers that make "just wait for better models" a serious argument rather than a dodge.

On the METR data, Linkov's caution is correct but his figures are now conservative. Current frontier models sit around 40 minutes at the 80% threshold across all tasks, roughly a third of the ~2 hour 50% horizon. The outlier is Claude Mythos Preview at roughly 3 hours at 80% reliability, which METR notes is more than double the next best model (Claude Opus 4.6 computes to about 70 minutes). So the 80% horizon has moved materially since this talk was recorded, which sharpens rather than weakens his thesis: the reliability-adjusted horizon is the number that is actually improving, and it is now long enough that his predicted six-month timeline for consistent substantial refactors looks plausible. METR itself published a January 2026 note clarifying the limitations of the time-horizon metric, which is worth reading before quoting either curve, and BRIDGE (arXiv 2602.07267) attacks the underlying problem of predicting human task-completion time from model performance.

One claim to treat skeptically: the sub-three-nines uptime slide for two unnamed AI companies is presented without methodology. Directionally believable given the pace, but it is an anecdote dressed as a metric, and Linkov himself flags that lines of code is "not a great metric" a few slides later.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Re-derive your own routing and compression eval harness against the 90% threshold rather than mean accuracy. For a model router, the equivalent of Linkov's argument is that mean quality parity is the wrong acceptance criterion. What matters is the tail: at what query difficulty does the cheap route drop below 90% agreement with the expensive route? Build that curve, because it is the number that determines whether a human ever has to intervene, and mean accuracy hides it entirely.
2. Instrument engineer-hours-to-merged, not tokens-per-task, before concluding a harness is expensive. The Opus 4.8 result cost more per run and less per outcome. Any internal cost dashboard that only shows API spend will recommend exactly the wrong model tier.
3. Treat self-validation as the load-bearing capability in any agentic pipeline you build for pruning or quantization sweeps. The GPT 5.5 failure is the failure you will hit: an agent that reports a successful quantization run having silently skipped the calibration set or the accuracy gate. Make the verifier a separate process with its own tools and no access to the generator's reasoning, and make it able to fail the run.
4. Apply the monorepo lesson to experiment repos. The stated win was not model navigation, it was cheap sandbox construction and end-to-end verification. If your ablation environments take minutes to clone and assemble across scattered repos, that setup time is multiplied by every agent in every parallel sweep, and consolidating is a direct throughput win on GPU-adjacent work.
5. Guard against AI psychosis in literature review. Deep research reports on optimization techniques routinely describe kernel features or vLLM flags that exist only in a branch or a paper. Require a code-level citation (file and line, or a merged PR) before any claimed capability enters a design doc.
