# The Messy Reality of Scale: Synthetic Data and Pre-Training — Marah Abdin & Robert McHardy, poolside

**Channel:** AI Engineer  
**Published:** 2026-07-26  
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE  

## TL;DR
Two halves of one argument: data and training-code correctness have to be treated as a single system, because either one being broken produces the same symptom of a model that will not train. Abdin covers synthetic data. Poolside hit non-optimal repetition on their high-quality corpus once the training budget scaled, which saturated the model early, and their fix was rephrasing to restore token uniqueness, now at 13% of the pre-training mix drawn from a continuously growing six trillion token synthetic corpus. Her framing is that synthetic data complements rather than replaces organic data, because organic data contains implicit rationale, planning, and structure that is present but not optimally presented, and synthetic generation is a way to project those features onto new planes. She decomposes every synthetic pipeline into six components and four reusable shapes, with the governing rule of thumb that if a task is too hard for your generator model it loses correctness and diversity, so break it down. McHardy covers pre-training, and his team's stance is that they trust nothing: they hash model weights across data-parallel replicas and crash training on mismatch. Two previously unpublished failure stories follow. A single broken GPU caused silent data corruption, producing spiky loss and huge gradient norms in a run otherwise identical to a healthy one. Then at around 50,000 steps the model stopped converging entirely because activations before the unembedding grew large enough that BF16 accumulation in the tensor-parallel LM head ran out of precision; moving that accumulation to FP32 resumed convergence and reversed the gradient norm trend. Scaling the fixed recipe to Laguna S, 118 billion total parameters with 8 billion active on 30 trillion tokens across 4,000 GPUs, surfaced a third bug: a race condition in DeepSeek's FP8 kernels silently corrupting about 0.5% of gradients, which their hash checks structurally cannot catch.

## Key Takeaways
- **The BF16 accumulation failure in the tensor-parallel unembedding is the single most valuable engineering detail in the talk.** Activations grew through training, the tensor-parallel LM head requires an accumulation across shards, that accumulation defaulted to BF16, and eventually there was not enough precision to do it accurately. The model flatlined at roughly 50,000 steps. The fix was one dtype change to FP32, applied to the existing checkpoint, and convergence resumed with gradient norms turning from increasing to decreasing.
- **The location of that failure is what makes it so dangerous.** The unembedding is the last layer, so precision loss there back-propagates into the entire model trunk. A numerical fault at the output boundary corrupts everything upstream, which is the worst possible place for it and the reason it presented as total convergence failure rather than as degradation.
- **Weight hashing across data-parallel replicas is a cheap, high-yield invariant that most teams do not implement.** All replicas must hold identical weights, so hash them periodically and crash on mismatch. That check caught a broken GPU causing silent data corruption in a run that was otherwise byte-identical in config, data, and code to a healthy one.
- **McHardy names the structural blind spot in his own method, which is the most credible part of the talk.** Hash comparison verifies that replicas agree, but real training runs have no redundancy where the same weights see the same data, so you can never verify that forward and backward passes are themselves correct. A race condition inside a kernel is invisible to replica hashing. They are building a dry-run hash checker to close this, and until they do, roughly 0.5% of gradients were being silently replaced by random values with no alarm firing.
- **The repetition-versus-quality tradeoff reverses as you scale the training budget, and this is the non-obvious data lesson.** Starting from quality over quantity is correct at small scale. Once the budget grows, the same high-quality corpus gets repeated past the useful point and saturates the model early. The constraint that binds is unique tokens, not quality, and rephrasing is the cheapest way to manufacture uniqueness from a corpus you already trust.
- **The six-component pipeline decomposition is genuinely reusable: seeds, primary inputs, metadata, secondary inputs, generator function, and supplementary functions like filters and validators.** Everything from a trivial rephrase to a heavily orchestrated agentic pipeline composes from the same parts, which is what makes a configurable infrastructure layer possible rather than one bespoke script per dataset.
- **The four generation shapes are worth memorising: rephrasing, multi-stage decomposition, cross-domain porting, and multi-turn iteration.** Her novel-writing example makes the second concrete: generating setting, characters, style, plot, and twists first, then chapters one at a time, produces a better novel than generating chapters directly. Cross-domain porting is their math-problems-to-code conversion. Multi-turn covers two agents in dialogue or a judge-and-evolver loop.
- **The complexity axis is a cost decision, not a quality ranking.** Cheap seed-heavy pipelines with small models handle rephrasing at scale. Expensive orchestrated pipelines are reserved for data worth the spend, and their explicit purpose is to avoid being capability-limited by whatever a single teacher model can do in one shot. Decomposition is how you generate data above your generator's single-pass ceiling.
- **Poolside is candid about a deliberate capability gap, which is refreshing.** Laguna S trails Nemotron and DeepSeek on MMLU-Pro, McHardy attributes this squarely to a data gap they could plug if they chose to, and says they do not care about knowledge benchmarks relative to agentic coding. Base-model evals are also explicitly caveated as only partly predictive of post-training outcomes.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
This is the most directly applicable talk in the batch for inference and numerics work, and there are four things worth extracting precisely.

The first is that the BF16 accumulation failure is a training-time instance of the exact failure mode that quantisation work fights at inference time, and the mechanism is identical. Reduced-precision formats fail when the dynamic range of the values being accumulated exceeds what the format can represent, and the failure is silent because there is no exception, just a wrong number. What makes their case instructive is that the same code trained fine for 50,000 steps before breaking, because activation scale grew gradually until it crossed the threshold. That is the characteristic signature of a precision fault: works, then stops working, with no change to the code. The transferable rule is that any accumulation, anywhere, is a precision risk independent of the precision of the values being accumulated, and this is why FP32 accumulation is standard practice inside otherwise low-precision GEMMs. If you are evaluating a quantisation scheme, the question to ask about it is not what format the weights and activations are in but what format the accumulator is in and whether the reduction width scales with the tensor-parallel degree. Poolside's bug existed specifically because tensor parallelism turned a local accumulation into a cross-shard one, which is a coupling between parallelism strategy and numerical safety that is easy to miss when the two are owned by different people.

The second is the monitoring lesson, which generalises past training. Their two visible failures both showed up as gradient norm anomalies before or alongside the loss anomaly, and in the BF16 case the gradient norm trend flipped from increasing to decreasing when the bug was fixed. That makes gradient norm trend a leading indicator, and loss a lagging one. The inference analogue for a compression practitioner is that aggregate quality metrics are lagging indicators of numerical trouble, while distributional statistics on intermediate activations are leading ones. If you quantise a model and only watch task accuracy, you will notice the problem after it is expensive. Watching activation magnitude distributions per layer, and specifically watching for growth in the layers feeding a reduction, catches it earlier and tells you where.

The third is the epistemics of silent corruption, and this is the part I would actually change practice over. Poolside has three failures in this talk and all three were silent: a broken GPU corrupting data, BF16 losing precision, and an FP8 kernel race condition randomising 0.5% of gradients. None threw an error. Two were caught by an invariant deliberately constructed to be checkable, and the third was caught only because it also happened to produce illegal memory accesses and NaNs, which is luck. The general principle is that you cannot detect silent corruption by looking harder at your outputs, you detect it by constructing invariants that must hold and asserting them. Replica weight hashing is one such invariant. For compression work, the analogous constructible invariants are worth enumerating: a quantise-then-dequantise round trip must reproduce weights within a known error bound, a compressed model's outputs on a fixed probe set must be bitwise reproducible across runs on identical hardware, and layer-wise activation statistics must match a recorded reference within tolerance. Each of these is a few lines, and each converts a class of silent failure into a loud one.

The fourth is the FP8 detail, which has immediate practical consequence. Poolside adopted DeepSeek's open FP8 kernels for a 4,000-GPU run and found a race condition that silently randomised a fraction of gradients, with a fix in an unmerged pull request at the time of the talk. FP8 training is where the throughput gains are, and this is a concrete instance of the risk: the kernel ecosystem is young, the bugs are concurrency-dependent, and concurrency-dependent bugs do not reproduce in small tests. If you are considering FP8 for any production path, the operational conclusion is to pin kernel versions, read the open issue tracker before adopting, and build the numerical invariant checks first rather than after. The same caution applies to FP4 and low-bit inference kernels, where the public issue trackers show a similar pattern of concurrency-dependent corruption at specific batch sizes and scale formats.

One more mechanic worth noting from the data half, because it maps onto distillation. Abdin's rule that a task too hard for the generator produces loss of both correctness and diversity, and that the answer is decomposition, is the same insight as staged distillation. You do not ask a teacher to produce the hard artefact in one shot, you decompose the artefact into steps each of which is within the teacher's reliable range. Their novel example and their orchestrated educational-data pipelines are both instances. For anyone generating synthetic data to distil a capability into a smaller model, the practical version is that pipeline depth substitutes for teacher capability, and it is usually cheaper to add a stage than to upgrade the teacher.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The model releases are confirmed and the transcription garbles several names worth correcting. Poolside released [Laguna XS 2.1 on 2 July 2026, a 33B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model that runs on a single GPU and ships with DFlash speculator models](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/319676/20260704/poolside-releases-free-open-weight-coding-model-july-9-upgrade-deadline.htm), free on Hugging Face and OpenRouter. The preview McHardy gave is no longer a preview: [Laguna S 2.1 was released on 21 July 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/07/21/3330818/0/en/Poolside-releases-Laguna-S-2-1-the-West-s-most-capable-open-weight-model.html), five days before this video went up. The architecture matches his description exactly: [118 billion total parameters with 8 billion activated per token, a context window up to 1 million tokens, and weights on Hugging Face under an OpenMDW-1.1 licence](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/07/21/poolside-releases-laguna-s-2-1/). It [scores 70.2% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 with thinking enabled, first among open models of disclosed size on poolside's own leaderboard](https://explainx.ai/blog/poolside-laguna-s-2-1-open-weights-coding-model-july-2026), and [VentureBeat's framing is that it beats rivals ten times its size](https://venturebeat.com/infrastructure/poolside-drops-laguna-s-2-1-an-open-weight-coding-model-that-beats-rivals-10x-its-size). Note one thing the talk does not mention that matters for interpreting the results: the released model uses Reinforcement Learning from Code Execution Feedback, poolside's method of learning from actual execution results rather than human preference labels. McHardy correctly caveated that his numbers were base-model evals, and RLCEF is a substantial part of what happens afterwards, so the released model's agentic performance is not attributable to the pre-training recipe alone.

On the rephrasing approach, Abdin's reference to "Beyond Web" is DatologyAI's [BeyondWeb](https://www.datologyai.com/blog/beyondweb), and the published findings support her framing closely. The [paper's results show that repetition degrades performance and reduces the effective per-token quality of a dataset](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10975v1), which is precisely the saturation problem poolside hit, and that rephrasing high-quality classifier-selected inputs with diverse prompts into dense, task-aligned formats [beats a full-data baseline by 4.2 percentage points](https://ritvik19.medium.com/papers-explained-557-beyond-web-eb0483a21dff), exceeding the ceiling set by natural data alone. It also explicitly contrasts this with naive repetition and simple continuation, which yield only modest gains. So the trend Abdin describes as trendy has real published support, and her caution to treat her own ablation numbers with a grain of salt is appropriate but the direction is well established.

On the FP8 kernel bug: the library is DeepSeek's [DeepGEMM](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM), transcribed as "Deep Chem." I could not independently verify the specific race condition or the 0.5% gradient corruption figure, and the fix was described as an unmerged pull request at the time of the talk, so treat that number as poolside's own unpublished measurement. What is independently visible is that the surrounding ecosystem has a consistent pattern of concurrency-dependent FP8 and FP4 faults: serving-side issues report [token-stream corruption in a W4A16 MoE decode kernel at concurrency two or above](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/issues/20775) and [CUDA crashes from DeepGEMM at concurrency 64 or above when checkpoint scale format is not ue8m0](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/issues/20776). That corroborates the class of bug even if not the specific instance, and it reinforces the practical point that these faults only appear at concurrency and scale.

Two caveats on the talk. First, comparisons were made against GLM-4.5-Air, which McHardy himself concedes is older, alongside more recent DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Nemotron models. Given that GLM-5.2 shipped as a 753B open-weights model beating GPT-5.5 on several long-horizon coding benchmarks, the open-weights bar moved considerably between the pre-training work described here and the talk. Second, all the eval results shown were base-model evals on a model that has since shipped post-trained, so the numbers in the talk are not the numbers you would reproduce by downloading Laguna S 2.1.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **Audit every accumulation in your inference and training path for accumulator dtype, and pay particular attention to reductions introduced by tensor parallelism.** Poolside's bug lived in a cross-shard accumulation that only existed because the unembedding was tensor-parallel. Concretely: list every reduction in your serving path, record the accumulator precision for each, and confirm FP32 accumulation anywhere the reduction width scales with parallel degree or vocabulary size. This is an afternoon of reading and it is the highest-value numerical audit available.
2. **Add gradient norm trend, or the inference equivalent of per-layer activation magnitude, to your standing dashboards as a leading indicator.** Loss and task accuracy are lagging. In both of poolside's visible failures the norm behaviour was diagnostic, and the fix reversed the trend. For compression work, record per-layer activation magnitude distributions on a fixed probe set for the full-precision model, then compare after every quantisation change. Growth in a layer feeding a reduction is the specific pattern that precedes a precision fault.
3. **Construct at least two checkable invariants for your own pipeline this week, because silent corruption is not detectable by inspection.** The candidates that cost almost nothing: a quantise-dequantise round trip bounded by a known error, and bitwise reproducibility of outputs on a fixed probe set across runs on identical hardware. Assert them in CI and fail loudly. Three of poolside's three failures were silent, and only the ones with a constructed invariant were caught deliberately.
4. **Pin FP8 and low-bit kernel versions, read the issue tracker before adopting, and test at production concurrency rather than at unit-test scale.** The failure class here is concurrency-dependent by nature and will not appear in a small reproducible test. If you are evaluating FP8 or FP4 paths, the acceptance test has to run at the batch sizes and parallel degrees you intend to serve at, and you should assume the kernel library has open correctness bugs until you have checked.
5. **Reframe your data constraint from quality to unique tokens once your compute budget grows, and use rephrasing to manufacture uniqueness.** The counterintuitive result is that a smaller high-quality corpus becomes a liability at larger training budgets because repetition saturates the model early. If you fine-tune or continue pre-training on a curated internal corpus, compute your effective epochs over unique tokens, and if that number is climbing past two or three, rephrasing your existing trusted data is cheaper and safer than sourcing new data of unknown quality. BeyondWeb's 4.2-point gain over a full-data baseline is the published evidence that this can exceed rather than merely approximate the natural-data ceiling.
6. **Use pipeline depth as a substitute for teacher capability when generating distillation data.** Abdin's rule is that a task too hard for the generator loses both correctness and diversity, and the fix is decomposition. If your synthetic data quality is limited by the teacher, add stages (plan, then draft, then verify, then refine) before you reach for a more expensive teacher. This is the same economics as your routing work: structure bought with cheap calls beating capability bought at frontier prices.
7. **Steal the six-component pipeline decomposition as the schema for your own data generation code.** Seeds, primary inputs, metadata, secondary inputs, generator function, supplementary functions. If your synthetic data generation is currently a set of ad hoc scripts, expressing them against this schema is what makes a configurable orchestration layer possible instead of a script per dataset, and it is the precondition for being able to reproduce or vary a dataset later.
