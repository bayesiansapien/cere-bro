# Circleback CEO Ali Haghani: Why Your Company Should Be Recording More Meetings

**Channel:** Y Combinator
**Published:** 2026-08-11
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4YWO4sSRrTE

## TL;DR
A workspace-tour interview with Ali Haghani (Circleback, YC W24) that turns out to contain three genuinely substantial ideas underneath the keyboard talk. The thesis in the title is an argument about agent context rather than about note-taking: as agents do more work, an agent operating without knowledge of what was said earlier today or what a customer asked is working in isolation, so the opportunity cost of not recording is already high and rising. His eval discipline is the best technical content in the piece and is unusually concrete, including a negative example (if a task was completed during the meeting it should not become an action item) and a style constraint (their notes should never contain the word "discussed" because it adds nothing). His stated reason for having evals at all is the one most teams learn too late: without systems for incremental improvement you play whack-a-mole, improving the overview while the topics get too long. And his list of what agents are not allowed to do is the sharpest governance statement I have seen from a founder: never send an email autonomously, never ship production copy, nothing touching data access or security, and critically, the engineer architects and the agent builds. The context the video omits entirely is legal. A record-everything default runs into twelve all-party consent states, four class actions against Otter.ai as of April 2026, and a GDPR position where consent is not an available basis in employment relationships.

## Key Takeaways
- What the product is: an AI notetaker that records and transcribes meetings, writes notes, assigns action items, runs automations to pull custom information into a CRM, issue tracker, or Slack, answers questions across all of a team's conversations, and connects to agents. His framing is a company brain that remembers everything.
- The recording thesis, stated as a claim about agents rather than about productivity: as LLMs get more capable and agents do more work, the context of everything going on becomes critical, because otherwise your agent does not know what was said earlier today or what a customer said. The opportunity cost of not recording is already high and getting higher, so companies will default to recording everything and to sharing what can be shared.
- The counterweight he names himself, and it is the correct one: customers need confidence that captured content reaches the right people and nothing is shared inadvertently, so that a one-on-one does not end up visible company-wide. He treats that as a precondition for the record-everything default rather than an afterthought.
- Unexpected internal uses. He runs Circleback as his applicant tracking system, with a saved view of every open action item belonging to a Circleback employee that came from an interview, so he can see who owes a candidate a response and who is interviewing whom. For candidates he is excited about, a person page gives the full timeline of touchpoints across emails and meetings.
- The same pattern applied to organisations: a company page for Y Combinator showing everyone they have spoken to there, what action items are outstanding, and a full conversation timeline plus upcoming meetings.
- The recursive example, which is the most convincing demo in the piece: he asked Circleback to prepare him for this interview, and it worked through prior emails and past conversations with the interviewer, pulled location from his calendar, extracted things to prepare for from emails, and surfaced things worth showcasing given the questions that had been shared.
- Capture ergonomics as a real design concern. He maps the iPhone action button to start a recording so capture is one press without fiddling, and a watch app in beta lets him start a recording from a complication when walking around the office without his phone.
- Agent setup: a main agent plus separate ones for finance, customers, and people ops, running in Telegram via OpenClaw, occupying maybe 10 to 20% of his day because they are always on and mostly just working, which he says is the state he wants them in.
- The migration he is midway through, and it is the important architectural move: shifting those processes out of his personal agent setup into actual company Slack channels, so a customer email spawns a thread with the investigation visible to everyone. His reason is that it makes it a company effort and gives everyone visibility into what customers are asking about, what kinds of prospects are inbound, what they care about, and why deals do not close.
- Customer base split between traditional businesses where the only known software is Microsoft and cubicles are the norm, and growth-stage startups and YC companies. They run a weekly rotation where an engineer handles all incoming support and talks to customers.
- Search-and-act over a recorded conversation: from a June customer fireside chat he can jump to a specific part of the imported recording, ask what was on a particular person's wish list, get a rundown of a 30-minute conversation, then follow up with whether any of it shipped and draft an email saying so.
- The eval discipline, quantifiable where there is a right answer. His example: if during a meeting someone says they will send something right now and there is signal it was done during the meeting, that should not be an action item, because it is redundant and unnecessary noise.
- Style as an eval target, which is the unusual part. Getting notes to read as though written by a person, and being deliberately opinionated about what good looks like since it is a primary surface area of the product. Their specific rule: never let notes say "discussed," because obviously things were discussed, it is a meeting, and the word adds no value.
- Why the evals exist, and this is the line to keep: if you do not have good systems for making incremental improvements you are constantly playing whack-a-mole, where maybe the overview got better but now the topics are too long. The evals run whenever they update models or change prompts.
- On token maxing: a culture of not just doing the task but doing it so that the next person doing it has an easier time or a better output, extensive AI use, and automating ops tasks nobody should be doing. Currently an unlimited AI budget with the team's judgment trusted, which he acknowledges may not scale at ten times the size. Regular show-and-tells where people demo tools they started using that week.
- His own workflow change over six months: mostly out of the editor, using a lightweight one only to review diffs and make small changes, with most code written through agents. He still hand-edits copy so he can see it in context, and small styling changes, because direct editing is quicker. Everything else runs end to end because they have built enough scaffolding and infrastructure.
- The bottleneck migration, stated twice. Code writing stopped being the constraint, so review became it, and they now spawn different reviewer agents depending on the PR, checking not only code quality but architecture, product, and even copy, to keep everything consistent with how they usually do things. Then at company level the constraint moved again, to hiring more good people to increase throughput of product decisions.
- What agents are not allowed to do, and this is the cleanest governance list in the piece. Never send an email on its own, though a draft is fine. Never ship production copy, though a first pass is acceptable. Nothing involving data access or security, where an agent can review as a sub-agent pass but not decide. And the general rule: the agent does not architect, the engineer or whoever owns the work architects and the agent builds it out.
- Thursdays are his no-meeting day, headphones on, aiming to ship something end to end within the day.
- His view on where software engineering is heading: the bound on what is possible is now imagination rather than your team's skills or bandwidth, and as the cost of writing software falls what matters is not how fast you ship but what you decide to build and how, so that the systems you build keep composing and interoperating.
- One incidental note on his own leverage change: he is doing less of what he disliked about founding, the ops work like logging into payroll software, which he automated, and consequently more of what he is high leverage at because he is not constantly underwater in the miscellany of running a company.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The recording thesis is worth taking seriously because it is the same claim Fei-Fei Li makes from an entirely different direction, and the convergence is informative. Her accessibility criterion is that a system can only learn what some sensor captured and some pipeline retained, so the frontier of any model is bounded by instrumentation rather than by architecture or scale. Haghani is applying that at organisational scale: an agent cannot act on a decision made in a meeting that produced no artifact, so the binding constraint on agentic usefulness inside a company is capture coverage rather than model capability. That reframes note-taking from a convenience into an instrumentation project, and it explains why the product's positioning as a company brain is the right one commercially. It also gives a sharper version of his opportunity-cost argument than he states: the value of capture is not linear in coverage, because an agent that has 60% of the context will confidently act on an incomplete picture, which can be worse than declining to act. Partial organisational memory has a failure mode that no memory does not.

This is also the third convergence on externalised durable state in as many days, and by now the pattern is worth treating as established. Anthropic's [managed agents](2026-08-11-Anthropic-Evolution-Of-Agentic-Surfaces-Managed-Agents.md) architecture logs everything to a durable session resource and explicitly sketches an emerging organisational-scale memory holding a team's runbooks. Circleback is building precisely that layer from the conversation side rather than the agent-execution side. The [Velocity Sickness](2026-08-09-Velocity-Sickness-Team-10x-Faster.md) argument was that decisions made inside ephemeral chats vanish, leaving only code with invisible reasoning. Three independent products and arguments, all concluding that the durable record is the load-bearing component and the agent is the replaceable part. If you want to predict where this goes, the interesting question is who owns that record, because the company brain and the agent platform's session log are the same asset viewed from two ends.

The eval discipline is the most directly transferable thing here and his whack-a-mole framing is the correct diagnosis. What he is describing is multi-objective quality with no single scalar, where prompt or model changes trade one dimension against another invisibly. His two example dimensions are instructive because they are different kinds: action-item extraction is a classification problem with ground truth, and note style is a preference with no right answer that they nonetheless made testable by encoding specific opinions as rules. Deciding to be opinionated about what good looks like is what converts an unmeasurable dimension into a measurable one, and that is the move most teams skip.

The compression parallel is exact and it is the thing I would act on. Quantisation and pruning are multi-objective in the same way: perplexity, task accuracy, long-context coherence, instruction following, code generation, and non-English performance all move independently under a recipe change, and it is entirely routine to improve the headline metric while silently degrading a dimension nobody measured. That is compression whack-a-mole and it is why quantised models often "feel worse" in ways no benchmark shows. His fix is the right one: an eval suite covering every dimension you care about, run on every recipe or model change, with the dimensions chosen because you decided to be opinionated about them rather than because a benchmark exists. His negative-example approach is also worth copying directly. Most eval suites test that good behaviour is present. Testing that a specific bad behaviour is absent, his already-done-during-the-meeting case, catches a different and often more damaging class of regression.

The bottleneck migration he describes is the clearest statement of it I have seen from a practitioner, and it happens twice in one interview. Writing code stopped being the constraint, so review became the constraint, so they built multiple reviewer agents specialised by PR type and by review dimension. Then at company scale the constraint moved off engineering entirely to hiring and product decision throughput. That is Amdahl applied to an organisation, and the operationally useful part is his response to the second bottleneck: reviewer agents covering architecture, product, and copy consistency alongside code quality. Note that this is exactly the automated verification layer the Velocity Sickness talk said was needed and did not supply, and it lines up with the DORA finding of median PR review time up 441% and 31% more PRs merging with no review at all. Circleback's answer to that data is more review capacity from agents rather than more discipline from humans, and given the evidence that human review of confident AI output is unreliable, that is probably correct.

His governance list is where I would push back least and quote most. "The agent does not architect, the engineer architects and the agent builds" is a genuinely good line because it locates the boundary at the point where decisions become expensive to reverse. Implementation is cheap to redo and architecture is not, so keeping the irreversible decisions human is the right allocation regardless of model capability. The email rule follows the same logic, since a draft is reversible and a sent message is not. Applied to research work the principle is identical: an agent can run the sweep and write the analysis script, and the human decides what the sweep is testing and which result to believe.

One tension worth naming across this week's talks. Haghani runs an unlimited AI budget on trust, and volunteers that it may not scale at ten times the size. The [Amazon AGI Lab talk](2026-08-11-Amazon-AGI-Lab-Agents-Codebases-Teams.md) describes the industry arc as ending with budgets bolted on after money got lit on fire. Both are probably right for their own scale, and the useful reading is that unlimited budgets work when everyone on the team can see the aggregate spend and feels ownership of it, and stop working when that visibility breaks. That is an argument about observability rather than about frugality, which suggests the thing to build before you grow is per-team spend attribution, not a budget.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The company's numbers are extraordinary and the video does not mention them, which is worth knowing because they are the strongest evidence for his own thesis about leverage. Circleback was founded in 2023 by Ali Haghani and Kevin Jacyna, went through YC W24, and raised a $2.5M seed in November 2024 from Y Combinator, Rebel Fund, Pioneer Fund, and Transpose Platform, for about $3M total across two rounds. Reported annual revenue is around $16.8M with roughly four employees, cash-flow positive, grown primarily organically, with thousands of paying customers. Whatever the precision on those figures, the revenue-per-employee ratio is the concrete instance of his claim that ops automation frees a founder to work on high-leverage things, and it is a considerably better argument than anything he says in the interview. It also puts his "unlimited AI budget" in context: at that revenue with four people, AI spend is not a meaningful line item.

The competitive frame matters for reading his eval emphasis. Circleback competes with Granola, Fireflies, and Otter on note quality and automation depth, which explains why he treats note style as a primary product surface and encodes opinions like never using the word "discussed" into evals. In a category where the underlying transcription and summarisation are commodity model capabilities, the differentiator is exactly the taste layer he has made testable. That is a good lesson for anyone building on foundation models: the defensible part is the opinion, and the opinion is only defensible if it is enforced by a regression suite.

The legal context is the video's real gap and it is substantial. In the US, 38 states are one-party consent while 12 require all-party consent: California, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Oregon, Pennsylvania, and Washington. Legality turns on where participants are located, how the tool obtains consent, and whether a court treats the AI bot as a recording device or as an unauthorised third-party interceptor, which is an unresolved question. Consent under both wiretapping statutes and GDPR requires informed agreement rather than mere awareness, meaning the participant must understand what is recorded, how it will be used, who can access it, and how long it is retained. Under GDPR, consent is not available as a lawful basis in employment contexts because of the power imbalance, so organisations rely on legitimate interest with a documented assessment. Federal penalties run to five years imprisonment, $250,000 fines, and civil damages of $10,000 or more per violation.

This is live litigation rather than theoretical risk. As of April 2026 four class actions challenge Otter.ai's consent practices, alleging that default auto-join and limited participant notification do not constitute valid consent under federal and state wiretapping law. A 2026 Fortune investigation documented AI meeting assistants remaining on calls after participants dropped, capturing off-the-record conversation, which is a distinct and worse legal exposure than recording the meeting itself. To be fair to Circleback specifically, they publish guidance on recording consent for AI meeting notes, so this is not a company ignoring the issue. But the thesis as stated in the video, that companies will default to recording everything, is being tested in court right now and the default-on auto-join pattern is the exact practice under challenge.

Two further consequences of record-everything that nobody in this category discusses and that anyone adopting it should price. A complete conversational archive is a discovery surface in litigation and subject to legal hold, which means the cost of the archive includes producing it to an opposing party. And it is a concentrated security target, since one credential compromise on the company brain yields every conversation the company has had, including the one-on-ones Haghani correctly identifies as the sensitive case. His framing of that as a permissions problem is right and incomplete: permissions govern who can query, and a breach bypasses the query layer entirely.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Build the multi-dimensional eval suite for your compression work and treat his whack-a-mole framing as the justification. Perplexity, task accuracy, long-context coherence, instruction following, code generation, and non-English performance move independently under a recipe change, and improving one while silently degrading another is the normal case rather than the exception. Run the whole suite on every quantisation or pruning change, exactly as he runs his on every model or prompt change.
2. Add negative test cases, which is the specific technique most eval suites lack. His already-completed-task example tests for the absence of a known bad behaviour rather than the presence of a good one. The compression analogue is a fixed set of prompts where you know the uncompressed model refuses, hedges appropriately, or declines to hallucinate, and you check that the compressed model still does. Degradation frequently shows up as increased confidence rather than decreased accuracy, and accuracy metrics do not see it.
3. Be opinionated in order to be measurable. His never-say-"discussed" rule converts an unmeasurable style preference into a regression test. Pick the qualitative properties you actually care about in a compressed model, state them as rules, and encode them. Unmeasured dimensions are the ones that silently degrade.
4. Adopt the architect-versus-build boundary as your own agent policy, because it locates the line at reversibility rather than at difficulty. Agents run sweeps, write analysis scripts, and produce implementations. Humans decide what is being tested, which result to trust, and anything expensive to reverse. This is a cleaner rule than capability-based delegation and it does not need revising when models improve.
5. Copy the specialised-reviewer pattern for your research artifacts. Different reviewer agents by change type, checking dimensions beyond correctness: does this configuration match what the paper claims, is the calibration set appropriate for the deployment distribution, does the results table mix pipeline versions. Review is the bottleneck after generation stops being one, and more human discipline is not the lever that works.
6. Take the capture argument into your own work as an instrumentation question. If an agent is going to help with your optimisation work, the constraint is what has been recorded about prior experiments: which configurations were tried, what was ruled out and why, what the failure looked like. That is the same durable-state conclusion three separate talks reached this week, and it is cheap to start.
7. Do not adopt a record-everything default without resolving consent first, and resolve it by jurisdiction rather than by policy. Twelve US states require all-party consent, GDPR does not permit consent as a basis in employment contexts so you need a documented legitimate-interest assessment, and there are four active class actions against a direct competitor over exactly the auto-join pattern this thesis implies. Read the vendor's own consent guidance and verify that bots leave when the meeting ends.
8. Price the archive as a liability alongside its value. A complete conversational record is discoverable in litigation, subject to legal hold, and a single high-value target whose compromise yields every conversation the company has had. Permissions govern querying and do nothing about a breach, so retention policy is the actual control and the record-everything framing argues against having one.
