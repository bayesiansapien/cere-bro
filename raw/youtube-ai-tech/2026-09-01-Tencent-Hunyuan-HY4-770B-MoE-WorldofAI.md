## TL;DR
Tencent released Hunyuan HY4, a 770 billion parameter mixture-of-experts model with 49 billion active parameters, a 1 million token context window, and an Apache 2.0 licence. It targets long-horizon workflows, software engineering, document-heavy office tasks, agents, and scientific research. On the reviewer's own benchmark platform it lands around sixth, just below Kimi K3, and around fifth on CodeArena, with web development quality he describes as comparable to GLM 5.3 Flash and in some cases close to Opus 5 High at dramatically lower cost. API pricing is roughly 83 cents per million input tokens and $2.50 per million output. The head-to-head qualitative tests are the bulk of the video: a Spider-Man game built in about six hours across nine iterations for roughly $13, a Unity 3D penguin game, a Minecraft clone, and an ISS orbital simulation where HY4 beat GLM 5.3, DeepSeek v4, and Qwen at $1.30 and one hour fifty-two minutes. His own verdict is that Kimi K3 remains the better choice for visual design taste, while HY4 is faster at similar quality and offers the best quality-per-dollar among open models.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
This is the most directly relevant model release in the corpus for Amit's work, and the video omits the two most important details.

**The attention mechanism is the real contribution.** HY4 uses Gated DeepSeek Sparse Attention (Gated DSA) with an IndexCache that lets the model reuse sparse attention indices across layers. That is the mechanism that makes a 1 million token context economically viable, and it is worth understanding precisely. Sparse attention selects a subset of keys per query, and computing that selection is itself expensive at long context. Caching the selected indices and reusing them across layers amortises the selection cost over the depth of the network, on the assumption that which tokens matter is broadly stable layer to layer. That is a strong assumption and it is exactly the kind of thing worth testing empirically: measure how much the selected index set drifts across layers at various context lengths, because the accuracy cost of the reuse is where the technique either holds or breaks. The model has 78 layers, so the amortisation factor is large if the assumption holds.

**The activation ratio deserves attention as a design point.** 49B active out of 770B is roughly 6.4%, which is sparser than most deployed MoE models. Sparser routing means better compute efficiency per parameter and worse memory efficiency, because all 770B parameters must be resident or paged even though only 49B are used per token. That trade is the central MoE serving problem: you pay dense memory cost for sparse compute benefit. It is why the weights are the binding constraint on deployment rather than the FLOPs, and it is why the quantization story below matters so much.

**The cached-input price is the most useful number in the whole release and the video does not mention it.** Published pricing is roughly $0.834 per million input tokens, $2.501 per million output, and $0.042 per million cached-input tokens. That is a cached-input discount of roughly 20x. At 1 million token context, the difference between a cache hit and a cache miss on the prefix is enormous, which makes prefix-cache hit rate the single most important operational metric for anyone serving this model. Every architectural decision that fragments the prefix (dynamic skill loading, per-caller prompt variation, hedged requests landing on different instances) has a directly quantifiable cost here, and it is the concrete number that several other talks in this corpus needed and lacked.

**The correction on quantization is the most important fact-check in this entry.** The video claims the model was "compressed from roughly 1.5 terabytes down to around 200 GB" with benchmark scores that "barely even moved an inch." The 1.5 TB figure is right, since 770B parameters at BF16 is roughly 1.54 TB. But the official release ships alongside an FP8 quantized variant whose weights alone require approximately 924 GB. FP8 on 770B parameters is roughly 770 GB before overhead, which is consistent. Getting to 200 GB would require roughly 2 bits per parameter, which is a community low-bit quantization rather than the official release, and a claim that benchmark scores barely move at 2 bits on a sparse MoE is a strong claim requiring evidence the video does not provide. For Amit this is precisely the kind of assertion worth testing directly: MoE models are known to be more quantization-sensitive in the router and in rarely-activated experts than dense models are, because an expert that sees few tokens has less redundancy to absorb quantization error. Expect degradation concentrated in low-frequency experts and in routing decisions, which is a failure mode that general benchmarks systematically fail to surface because they under-sample the tail.

The practical deployment consequence: at 924 GB for FP8 weights, this needs multiple 80 GB accelerators for weights alone before KV cache, and at 1 million token context the KV cache is itself substantial. Anyone evaluating this model should compute total memory as FP8 weights plus KV cache at target context, and the sparse attention is what keeps the second term tractable.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The specifications check out and the published benchmarks are more specific than the video's leaderboard positions.

HY4 Preview was released on August 28, 2026 under Apache 2.0, four days before this video. It is a 770B MoE with 49B active parameters across 78 layers and 1M context, using Gated DeepSeek Sparse Attention with IndexCache for cross-layer index reuse. Published results include Terminal-Bench 85.4 and DeepSWE 64.3, which are the agentic and software-engineering benchmarks that matter for the stated positioning and which the video does not cite. In an internal blind evaluation on engineering tasks it edged out both GLM 5.3 and Kimi K3, which is a stronger claim than the video's "Kimi wins most benchmarks" and is also Tencent's own evaluation, so both framings deserve their respective discounts.

Pricing is confirmed at $0.834 per million input, $2.501 per million output, and $0.042 per million cached input. The FP8 quantized variant is part of the official release and its weights require roughly 924 GB.

**Caveats on the video as a source.** Several of the rankings cited (the sixth-place position, the fifth on CodeArena in one framing, the front-end comparisons) come from the presenter's own benchmark and vibe-coding platform, which he promotes and links. That is not disqualifying, and it does mean the evaluations are neither independent nor documented in methodology. The qualitative comparisons are single-run, single-prompt, one-shot tests with visual assessment, so they are demonstrations rather than measurements. Note the internal inconsistency in the transcript, which alternates between "HY4," "H4," "H 5.4," and "HY 5.4," suggesting the model naming was not carefully checked.

The one claim to actively distrust is the 200 GB compression with unchanged benchmarks. It does not match the official FP8 release size, no source is given, and it is the kind of claim that is both highly consequential (it would determine whether the model fits on a single 8x80GB node) and easy to get wrong.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Test the IndexCache assumption directly, because it is the paper's real contribution and it is testable.** Instrument the sparse-attention index selection per layer and measure how much the selected set drifts across the 78 layers, at 32k, 256k, and 1M context. If drift is low the technique is sound and generalisable to other long-context models. If drift rises with context length, the accuracy cost of reuse is concentrated exactly where the 1M claim matters most. This is a well-defined experiment with a publishable answer.
- **Use the $0.042-versus-$0.834 cached-input spread as the reference number for prefix-cache economics.** A 20x discount on cached prefix tokens is the concrete figure that quantifies why prefix-cache hit rate matters, and Amit should use it whenever arguing for stable-prefix design against dynamic prompt assembly. It also prices the hidden cost of request hedging, which lands on a different instance and loses the cache.
- **Do not trust the 200 GB quantization claim, and design the test that settles it.** Official FP8 weights are approximately 924 GB. Any sub-400 GB variant is aggressive low-bit quantization, and MoE models degrade differently from dense models: expect error concentrated in the router and in rarely-activated experts. The right evaluation stratifies by expert activation frequency rather than reporting an aggregate benchmark, because aggregates under-sample the tail where the damage is.
- **Compute deployment memory as FP8 weights plus KV cache at target context before evaluating anything.** At 924 GB of weights this is a multi-node or high-density-node deployment, and the sparse attention is the only reason the KV term stays manageable at 1M context. That total, not the parameter count, is the number that determines whether this is usable.
- **Adopt the ISS-test structure as an evaluation template.** Same task across four open models, reporting quality, wall-clock time, and dollar cost together. HY4 best quality at $1.30 and 112 minutes, Qwen close at 30 cents, GLM $3.20, DeepSeek fastest at 57 cents with lower quality. A three-axis frontier on one task is far more decision-useful than a leaderboard rank, and it is exactly the shape of evaluation Amit should produce for routing decisions.
- **Note state-consistency-across-generation as a distinct capability to measure.** The orbital salvage result (GLM losing track of scene state when the camera moved, HY4 maintaining it) is a long-horizon coherence property that no standard benchmark captures and that determines whether a model is usable for agentic work. Worth building a targeted eval for, since it is the property the 1M context is supposed to buy.
