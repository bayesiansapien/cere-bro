# Velocity Sickness: What Happens When Your Whole Team Gets 10x Faster

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-09
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kz4QJmNrVXU

## TL;DR
Matt Dailey (CEO of Ref) names the condition where individual engineers get much faster with AI while the team does not: velocity sickness, defined as stress caused by sudden output increases resulting in output without impact. He identifies four symptoms, of which he argues the fourth is the only one that really matters. Critical decisions being made by agents, because an engineer who lets an agent make a critical decision no longer owns that code, and at company scale that means you no longer own your product. His structural argument is that the shape of engineering work changed (planning, then agent implementation, then polish) while our primary tool, the IDE, was built for the implementation phase that is no longer ours. His proposed answer is a decision layer built on durable shared docs rather than chats, with a specific architectural claim: separate the agent as action from the doc as state, so agents become stateless and start from the same shared context. The best independent validation of his diagnosis is DORA's 2026 data, which he does not cite: median PR review time up 441%, PR size up 51.3%, and 31% more PRs merging with no review at all.

## Key Takeaways
- Velocity sickness, defined: stress caused by sudden output increases thanks to AI, affecting individuals and teams, producing output without impact. The felt experience is moving very fast, expecting that to feel good, and it not feeling good.
- Symptom 1, too many PRs to merge. The classic first problem an individual hits, magnified at team scale into merge conflicts and a merge queue that breaks down.
- Symptom 2, moving in many directions at once. Individually, many agents doing different things and a fried brain trying to track who is doing what. Organizationally, engineers sprinting in uncoordinated directions and occasionally into each other, with speed but no focus.
- Symptom 3, declaring agent bankruptcy. Twelve terminals open, a productive-feeling day, then you return next morning to what he describes as a room of strangers, and because they are agents you just kill them and start over. The cost is doing the same work twice and paying tokens twice.
- Symptom 4, and the one he says is most important: critical decisions made by agents. Delegating a critical decision transfers ownership of the code to the agent, and at scale transfers ownership of the product.
- His illustrating anecdote is the sharpest thing in the talk. A newsletter writer built an impressive, genuinely non-slop agent pipeline for research, synthesis, and voice-preserving editorial, then said "I'm basically writing a book every week." Asked whether the audience reads a book every week, the answer was no. Enormous output, unread pages.
- The old shape of engineering: plan up front, then heads-down iterative implementation in isolation, then polish and ship. Our tools, above all the IDE, were built precisely for the implementation and polish parts done by an individual.
- The new shape: plan, then the agent implements (he argues this should not even be on the diagram since it is no longer human work), then polish where you hold the result and ask whether it is what you wanted. The two remaining human phases are the creative and collaborative ones.
- The skill he names is gear selection: recognising which gear you are in and whether your current tool serves that gear.
- Why chat is the wrong primitive for the decision layer: chats are a relic of building for implementation, so they default to isolated, ephemeral, and brain-off. Decisions get made inside a chat, are never shared with the team, and vanish, leaving only code where the reasoning is invisible.
- The specific brain-off failure: the agent proposes options with a recommended one, you click the recommendation without thinking, and you have just delegated a decision.
- His analogy for why docs: if a pre-AI team had an alignment problem, no lead would have prescribed "everyone work in Slack DMs." You would surface the key decisions and spend real time getting them right.
- What he is explicitly not proposing. Plan mode is good but is essentially a richer chat message, still isolated and ephemeral. Full spec-driven development operates at product-behaviour level and is too far from engineering reality. He wants the middle: durable, shared, long-lived, and technical.
- The plan as a portal to the software system, his Tony Stark framing. AI is very good at finding what relates to what, so you ask it to surface the parts of a complex system relevant to your idea and lay them out so you can arrange them to express how you want the system to grow.
- The core architectural flip: separate the agent as action from the doc as state. Extract the implicit context that normally accumulates inside a long-lived session into a doc, so you can spawn many agents that all start from the same context, each effectively stateless. He calls this doing your context engineering in the doc.
- The early adoption signal he treats as healthy: people plan and then do not implement the plan. That means they explored an idea, got it concrete, and then chose not to build it, which is prioritisation working.
- His reframing of the fix: shift from code velocity to idea velocity. Instead of prototype gravity, where you build a thing and become attached to shipping it, you traverse the idea maze more broadly and find the better answer around the corner.
- How the four symptoms resolve. Too many PRs: move the review point earlier, because the hardest part of any code review is deciding what actually matters, and if you aligned on that up front the review is simple. Too many directions: share plans and align before anyone has spent a day building a prototype. Agent bankruptcy: impossible once agents are stateless, since rebuilding your own human context is just reading the doc. And humans own the decisions, which was the point.
- Secondary benefits he claims: easier parallel agents, and a durable decision log captured up front rather than reconstructed later by an LLM summarising a session and possibly selecting the wrong things.
- His prediction: engineering becomes multiplayer by default sooner than people expect, because the work that remains human is the creative work, and creative work wants collaboration.
- Three things to do immediately: think in terms of planning and polish gears and notice when you drift between them inside one session, treat the plan as a portal into the system and ask it to show you what matters, and share a plan with a teammate rather than only with an agent.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The agent-as-action, doc-as-state separation is the one genuinely load-bearing technical idea, and it is worth restating in systems terms because the framing generalises. A long-running chat session is a stateful process whose state lives only in the process. That gives you three well-known problems: you cannot fork it, you cannot inspect it, and when it dies the state is gone. Externalising the state into a durable artifact makes the agent a stateless function over that artifact, which immediately buys forking (many agents from one context), inspectability (a human or another agent can read the state), and crash recovery (his agent bankruptcy just stops existing). This is the same reasoning that pushes any distributed system toward externalised state, and it is the correct response to the problem rather than better session management.

There is a real cost implication too. If state lives in an ever-growing conversation, every subsequent turn re-attends over the entire history, so per-turn cost grows with session length even though the useful state does not. Externalising the state to a compact doc and reinitialising a fresh agent from it means per-turn context stays roughly constant. Combined with prompt caching on a stable doc prefix, this is a meaningful token cost reduction, not just an organisational nicety. His "spend tokens twice" complaint about agent bankruptcy is the visible version of a cost curve that was already bad before the restart.

His move-the-review-point-earlier argument is the one to keep, because it is a claim about where verification is cheapest. Reviewing a decision costs a paragraph of reading. Reviewing the code that decision produced costs reading a diff and reconstructing the decision from it. The cost ratio is large and it grows with implementation size, which is exactly the direction AI pushes implementation size. Every talk at this conference converged on some version of this: verify at the innermost or earliest point where the defect can be identified, because downstream verification cost compounds.

Where he is weakest is that his solution assumes the plan is where the risk lives. For the class of failure that dominates in practice, an agent that produced plausible-looking work while silently skipping a load-bearing step, no amount of upfront decision alignment helps. You aligned on the right decision and the agent did not implement it. His polish phase is supposed to catch that, but he says almost nothing about how, and "hold it in your hands and ask if this is what I wanted" is exactly the human review that the Wharton cognitive-surrender research says is unreliable when the AI is confident. The decision layer and an independent automated verification layer are complements, not substitutes, and this talk only sells one of them.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The strongest support for his diagnosis is DORA's 2026 data, and it quantifies velocity sickness precisely. Throughput gains are now real and organisational rather than merely individual: epics completed per developer are up 66.2%, and Google's DORA researchers report that AI coding tool use no longer correlates with delivery throughput slowdowns, a reversal of earlier years. But the downstream symptoms are exactly his list. Median time in PR review is up 441% versus 2025 findings, pull request size is up 51.3%, and 31% more PRs are merging with no review at all. The mechanism DORA names is a verification tax: the cognitive overhead of reviewing, validating, and auditing AI-generated code. The 2025 report found AI adoption improving throughput by roughly 2% to 18% while raising instability, higher change failure rates, and technical debt where teams lacked code-level observability.

That triad is worth sitting with because it is his four symptoms as measured numbers. Bigger PRs (symptom 1) taking dramatically longer to review, with a growing fraction skipping review entirely (which is symptom 4, decisions passing unexamined, in its most literal form). DORA's 2026 conclusion that the ROI of AI in software development runs through code review, and InfoQ's summary that strong engineering foundations are what drive AI return on investment, both support his structural claim that the constraint moved from generation to the human-judgement steps around it.

Two related findings from elsewhere in this conference series reinforce the same picture from different angles. The Carnegie Mellon difference-in-differences study of 807 Cursor-adopting GitHub repos against 1,380 controls found a 281% first-month increase in lines added that decayed to baseline by month three, while static analysis warnings rose 30% and complexity rose 41% permanently, with a doubling of complexity associated with a 64.5% reduction in future velocity. That is output-without-impact with a measured half-life. And Chainguard's 2026 Engineering Reality Report puts actual code writing at 16% of the engineering week, which is the quantitative case for his claim that the tool built for implementation is optimising a shrinking slice of the job.

On Ref itself: searches surface no substantive public information about the company or the product beyond what Dailey says here, so the specifics of the tool are unverified. The talk should be read as a well-argued position piece from a founder whose product embodies the position, and the ideas evaluated on their merits rather than on evidence of the product working. The framings he borrows are not his own and are worth tracing: "idea maze" comes from the startup-strategy literature (Balaji Srinivasan and Chris Dixon), and it fits his argument well.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Externalise the state in your own optimization work, because the pattern applies directly. Instead of a long-running session where an agent accumulates knowledge about which layers you pruned, what the accuracy delta was, and what you already ruled out, maintain a durable experiment doc holding exactly that state, and spawn fresh agents against it. You get parallel sweeps from one shared context, a readable record of what has been tried, and constant per-turn context cost instead of a growing one. This is a token-cost optimization and a reproducibility fix in the same change.
2. Move the review point earlier for compression decisions specifically. The expensive review is looking at a quantized checkpoint and asking why it regressed. The cheap review is agreeing up front on which layers are sensitive, what the accuracy floor is, and which calibration distribution counts. Write that down and get one colleague to read it before the sweep runs, not after.
3. Build the decision log as an artifact rather than reconstructing it. His point that an LLM summarising a session later may select the wrong things is exactly right for research work. Six months from now the question "why did we choose per-channel over per-tensor quantization for this layer" needs an answer written at decision time, not inferred from a config diff.
4. Use the DORA numbers when arguing for verification investment, since they are the most credible available. Median review time up 441% with 31% more PRs merging unreviewed is a stability argument that survives scrutiny, and it pairs with the CMU complexity elasticity to make a compounding-cost case. Both are stronger than a quality argument.
5. Do not accept the decision layer as sufficient on its own. Pair it with independent automated verification, because aligning on the right decision does not stop an agent from silently failing to implement it. His polish phase is human review, and the research says confident AI output degrades human review below unaided baseline. Upfront alignment plus a deterministic gate is the combination.
6. Take the unread pages anecdote seriously as a personal check. The equivalent in research is running many more ablations because they are now cheap, and reading none of them carefully. If your sweep output volume has grown faster than your analysis throughput, you have velocity sickness, and the fix is fewer, better-motivated experiments rather than faster ones.
