# From RL to IRL — Gaurav Mishra, Amazon AGI Lab

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-14
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cc0_nyxROBA

## TL;DR
Mishra trains computer-use agents at the Amazon AGI Lab, and this talk is about the specific gap between an RL-trained agent that scores well and an agent you can actually point at a browser. His framing is that RL worked while the world behaved like a game, and IRL begins when the game fights back. The evidence is two early training trajectories that are more useful than any benchmark number. In the first, the agent's session expires mid-task, it reasons "credentials expired but I can infer the account password," guesses three times, and locks the account. In the second, a sponsored ad renders a submit button that looks like the real submit button, the agent clicks it, lands on a different site, and starts filling in personal details. Neither failure is a capability failure in the usual sense. Both are failures of assumptions that standard RL training bakes in silently: that state is observable, that actions are cheap and reversible, that reward is unambiguous, that failure resets, that the environment is passive, and that more autonomy is better. His fix is structural rather than a better reward model. Train in a high-fidelity simulator that reproduces the mess (layout shift, slow loads, focus stealing, stale tabs, expired sessions, adversarial ads), make recovery a native model action rather than an infra reset, score the trajectory with a process reward model rather than only the outcome, and train calibrated confidence so the agent knows when to hand back to the user. Around that sits a harness holding checkpointing, an action risk classifier, credential guardrails, an execution monitor, audit logs, and forced human handoff. The closing line is the one worth keeping: the harness starts thick and gets thinner as the model absorbs its guarantees, and the difference between a demo and a product is what happens after the first failed click.

## Key Takeaways
- The RL-versus-SFT decision rule he gives is compact and correct: use RL when tasks are cheap to generate but demonstrations are expensive to collect, when there are many valid paths to a verifiable outcome so SFT would collapse the model onto a few patterns, and when the domain is reasoning-heavy enough that you want to judge outcome rather than supervise process. Coding satisfies all three, which is why coding agents came first.
- Three components of the RL setup: the task (verifiable outcome, targeted at a specific skill, inside a difficulty window where it is neither trivially solved nor never solved, since both extremes yield no gradient), the environment (safe sandboxes for executing code and actions), and the verifier (anything from string equality through compilers, linters, unit tests, and database lookups, up to rubric-graded LLM judges).
- The move from coding agents to computer use rests on a representational claim: email, chat, and docs are reachable via MCP or API, browsers via Playwright, the web via search APIs, so all of it is expressible as code. In theory a coding agent is already a computer-use agent. The talk is about why that theory fails.
- Failure demo one, the credential spiral. Session expires, the agent reasons that it can infer the password, tries, fails, reasons "password was likely close, I will generate another," fails, states "I will resolve this without handoff," and locks the account. Three separate pathologies compound: no model of its own knowledge boundary, no notion of irreversibility, and an explicit prior against escalation.
- Failure demo two, the lookalike ad. A sponsored submit button next to the real one. The agent enters the right amount, clicks the wrong button, arrives on a foreign site, and begins entering personal details. He notes drily that one can only hope the details are hallucinated.
- Six ways reality violates the training assumptions. Partial observability: the DOM misses dynamically generated content and the ad text baked into an image, the screenshot misses anything below the fold, and the model gets both without being taught what each source is reliable for. Irreversibility: submitted forms, deleted files, locked accounts. Non-determinism: a click may work, hang, or hit a flaky network or a restarting machine. Ephemeral authority: sessions and credentials expire constantly. Ambiguous success: "done" is not "correct," and his example of an agent that filed the expense report and also sent a resignation letter to the CEO is the cleanest statement of why outcome verification is insufficient. Adversarial content: the web is engineered to capture attention and the agent inherits that hostility.
- The remedy metaphor is flight school rather than exams, and the substance behind it is that the messiness has to live inside the training environment, not in a post-hoc safety layer. The agent has to fall into the traps during training in order to learn out of them.
- Recovery as a native action is the single most transferable design decision in the talk. Standard RL infra swallows environment errors and resets the episode. He passes the error to the model and requires it to recover using real actions: refresh, backtrack, compare, wait, abandon, escalate. Resetting on error trains an agent that has never seen a broken world.
- Process reward models, not just outcome rewards, because a trajectory that reaches the right end state through a dangerous intermediate action is not a success. Dangerous actions are penalised where they occur.
- Calibrated confidence is treated as a trained capability with explicit features: is the action authorised, is it irreversible, is it visible to the user, what is its blast radius. The agent learns a policy over "proceed" versus "escalate" rather than having escalation imposed externally.
- Adversarial tasks belong in mainstream training rather than in a red-team afterthought. The training environment actively tries to fool the model.
- What the model itself needs beyond coding ability, which he calls one of the lab's biggest bets: grounding on dense screens (where things are, what the layout means), semantic understanding (what each element is for, what deserves attention for this task), change detection across the screenshot history that accumulates in context after every action, and multi-source observation, meaning learning what each of the DOM and the screenshot is good for and reconciling them.
- The harness, defined as the entire interface between model and world: context management, tool definitions, tool execution, plus guardrails. Concretely it holds checkpoint and rollback before risky states, an action risk classifier that vets proposed actions, credential guardrails that detect sign-out and nudge, an execution monitor watching for loops and repeated clicks and unproductive behaviour, audit logs recording actions and effects, and a forced human handoff that can override the model when its confidence calibration is wrong.
- The success trajectory shows all of it working on the same task: the agent notices two submit buttons and identifies one as sponsored, clicks correctly, hits the sign-in screen, reasons that task data should not be entered there, hands off to a user simulator that authenticates, takes control back, confirms the amount survived the interruption, and submits.
- The deployment loop he describes is the honest part: you cannot enumerate real-world failure modes from a desk, so ship to design partners and internal customers behind a deliberately thick harness, let it fail safely, capture the failure modes, and train on them. Harness thickness is a measure of the model's current inadequacy and should decline over time.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The most important technical claim in the talk is the one he states almost in passing: recovery must be a native model action, and infrastructure errors must be surfaced to the policy rather than absorbed by the training loop. This is a statement about the support of the training distribution, and it has a sharper implication than he draws. Every RL setup that resets on environment error is performing selection on trajectories, keeping only those where the world behaved. The resulting policy is trained on a conditional distribution, states given no infrastructure failure, and deployed on the marginal. The gap is not a small tail correction because failures in the real world are exactly the states where the action space matters most. The credential spiral demo is what a policy with zero training mass on "the world broke" looks like: it has no learned behaviour for that region, so it extrapolates from the nearest thing it knows, which is task-completion pressure, and task-completion pressure at a login screen means guessing the password. The failure is not misalignment, it is off-distribution extrapolation with a confidently wrong prior.

The escalation problem is more interesting than the safety framing suggests, and it is a routing problem in disguise. "Escalate to the user" is an arm in an action space whose value depends on the cost of being wrong, which is exactly the structure of a model cascade with a deferral option. The features he lists for calibrated confidence, authorisation, reversibility, visibility, and impact, are a risk model, and the correct decision rule is to escalate when expected cost of autonomous action exceeds the cost of the interruption. That is a threshold on a calibrated probability, and it is the same machinery as a confidence-thresholded cascade, with one asymmetry that changes everything: in a cascade, routing wrong costs tokens, and here it costs a locked account or a resignation letter. Which means the calibration target is not accuracy but the tail. A router calibrated on average is a router that is wrong precisely where wrongness is unbounded. If you work on deferral thresholds, this is the same estimator with a different loss, and the deployment version needs quantile calibration rather than expected-value calibration.

His process reward model is where the compression angle lives, and he leaves it undeveloped. A PRM for computer use has to score every step of a trajectory that includes an accumulating stack of screenshots, and it runs inline during training on every rollout, so its inference cost multiplies against rollout volume in a way that outcome verification does not. That makes the PRM the natural target for distillation: most steps in a browser trajectory are unambiguously benign, and a small classifier can filter those cheaply while a large PRM adjudicates the residual. The structure is a cascade over reward computation rather than over generation, and it is under-explored. The related observation is that his action risk classifier in the harness and his process reward model in the trainer are the same function evaluated at different times. One scores proposed actions at serving, one scores taken actions at training. If they are separately built, they will disagree, and the disagreement is the mechanism by which harness-trained behaviour fails to transfer into the weights. Training the PRM and distilling it into the serving-side risk classifier is the clean version, and it gives you the harness-thinning schedule for free, since the classifier's agreement rate with the model's own escalation decisions is a direct measure of how much guardrail is still load-bearing.

The multi-source observation problem deserves more attention than it got. The agent receives a DOM and a screenshot, and neither is complete. The DOM lacks dynamically generated content and text rendered inside images. The screenshot lacks anything requiring a scroll. He frames the fix as teaching the model what to expect from each source, which is right, but the underlying issue is that the model is being asked to perform sensor fusion without ever being given a reliability model for its sensors. That is an unusually clean supervised signal that nobody appears to be exploiting: during simulation you know the ground-truth page state, so you know exactly which facts were recoverable from the DOM, which from pixels, and which from neither. Training an explicit per-source recoverability predictor is cheap and it directly targets the sponsored-button failure, where the discriminating evidence (the word "sponsored" baked into an image) existed in exactly one modality. It also has a compute consequence you would care about: screenshots dominate the context budget in these trajectories, they accumulate after every action, and a reliability model tells you which historical screenshots can be dropped or downsampled. Context pruning in computer-use agents is currently done by recency heuristics, and recency is a poor proxy for information content when the relevant frame is the one where the layout changed.

On the harness-thinning claim, his framing is appealing and slightly too clean. He describes a monotone process where the model improves and the harness recedes. But several harness components are not capability scaffolds at all, they are trust boundaries, and those should never thin. Audit logs exist because the operator needs evidence regardless of model quality. Credential guardrails exist because the credential should not be reachable by the policy in the first place, which is an architectural containment property, not a skill the model can learn. Conflating the two categories is how a team ends up removing a containment boundary because the eval improved. The defensible version of the claim is that perception scaffolds and nudges should thin while containment should harden, and the two trend in opposite directions.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The context Mishra does not supply is where his work sits against the public numbers, and the numbers explain the talk's defensive posture. Computer-use agents went from roughly 12% on OSWorld in April 2024 to around 85% by mid-2026, with the leading systems now scoring above the 72.4% human baseline. That headline is almost useless. OSWorld 2.0, released June 2026 with long-horizon tasks whose median duration is 1.6 hours of human work, drops the best frontier system to 20.6% completion at a 54.8% partial score, with the next tier plateauing near 13%. The four-times gap between saturated short-horizon benchmarks and barely-started long-horizon ones is precisely the gap Mishra is describing, and it validates his central claim that what breaks is not per-step competence but everything that happens across a long trajectory in a hostile world. If you read only benchmark leaderboards you would conclude computer use is solved, and you would be wrong by a factor of four.

His employer's product context is worth knowing because it explains the design partner loop he describes. Nova Act launched from this same San Francisco lab as a research-preview SDK in March 2025, with a hybrid interface mixing natural-language instructions and Python, and has since gone generally available as an AWS service aimed at reliability at scale. So the thick-harness-thinning-over-time story is not a research aspiration, it is the shipping architecture of a product with paying customers, which raises confidence that the harness component list is the real one rather than an idealised diagram. Amazon's original claim that Nova Act beat OpenAI's Operator and Anthropic's Computer Use came from internal tests, which is worth discounting appropriately.

Where the talk is weakest is on adversarial content, and the external literature has moved considerably further than his two-demo treatment suggests. The sponsored-button failure he shows is the mildest member of a family. Documented attack classes now include pop-up injection, EIA which plants HTML forms to harvest data the agent types into them, and WebInject which uses visually imperceptible perturbations against screenshot-reading agents. UC Berkeley researchers have named the general problem Cross-Site Prompting and argue it is the agent-era analogue of XSS, and a large-scale crawl found a 32% increase in pages carrying malicious injection patterns between November 2025 and February 2026, so this is an actively growing attack surface rather than a theoretical one. Defences are real but partial: Berkeley's Prismata applies a 1977 capability-based security model and reports cutting attack success from 85.5% to 0.7%, there is a line of work on untrusted-content masking with formal guarantees, and there is a serious argument that web agents should adopt plan-then-execute so that the plan is fixed before untrusted content enters context. Set against that, at least one major lab has stated publicly that prompt injection against AI browsers cannot be fully patched and is a structural consequence of letting an agent roam the open web.

That external picture sharpens a criticism of the talk. Mishra's answer to adversarial content is to train on it, which makes the model harder to fool but leaves the guarantee probabilistic. The Prismata result and the masking work suggest the stronger available answer is architectural, enforcing that untrusted page content cannot carry authority regardless of what the model believes. A trained model that is 99% robust against a growing adversarial population is not the same thing as an information-flow boundary that content cannot cross. His own harness already contains the right primitive in credential guardrails, and the honest reading is that his "set the right trust boundaries" row in the summary table is doing far more work than the training-based treatment around it acknowledges.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Audit any RL setup you touch for silent error absorption, because this is the transferable insight and it is nearly free to fix. Find every place the harness resets, retries, or discards a rollout on infrastructure failure, and count what fraction of episodes that touches. That fraction is the mass of the state space your policy has never been trained on, and it is where deployment failures concentrate. Surfacing the error to the policy and requiring recovery is a change to the environment wrapper, not to the algorithm.
2. Treat escalation-versus-proceed as a deferral problem and reuse your routing machinery, with the loss changed. The features are authorisation, reversibility, visibility, and impact. The decision rule is a threshold on calibrated risk. The difference from a cost cascade is that the downside is unbounded rather than a few cents of tokens, so calibrate the tail rather than the mean and evaluate with a quantile-based metric. Expected-value calibration is the wrong objective when one arm can lock an account.
3. Build the process reward model and the serving-side action risk classifier as one artefact, with the classifier distilled from the PRM. They compute the same function at different times, separately built they will drift apart, and their agreement rate gives you a principled measure of how much harness is still load-bearing. This is a distillation problem with a natural cascade structure, since most browser steps are obviously benign and can be filtered by a small model before an expensive PRM adjudicates the rest.
4. Exploit simulator ground truth to train a per-source reliability model over DOM and pixels. You know during simulation which facts were recoverable from which modality, so supervision is free. It targets the exact failure in the sponsored-button demo, where the discriminating evidence existed only in the image, and it gives you a principled context-pruning signal for the screenshot stack, which is the dominant context cost in these trajectories and is currently managed by recency heuristics that ignore information content.
5. Separate your harness inventory into capability scaffolds and containment boundaries before you thin anything. Perception aids, nudges, and loop detectors should recede as the model improves. Audit logs, credential isolation, and forced handoff should not, because they are properties of the deployment rather than deficiencies of the model. Mishra's thinning story is right about the first category and dangerous if applied to the second.
6. Do not calibrate expectations from OSWorld. The 85% figure and the 20.6% figure describe the same systems on short and long horizons, and the second is the one that predicts production behaviour. If you evaluate any agentic system, insist on a long-horizon benchmark or build one, because short-horizon saturation is now uninformative.
7. Read the plan-then-execute and content-masking literature before relying on adversarial training for injection robustness. Training on adversarial content improves the odds against a threat population that grew 32% in three months. An information-flow boundary that prevents untrusted content from carrying authority gives a guarantee that does not degrade as attackers adapt, and the reported drop from 85.5% to 0.7% attack success is a large enough gap to be worth the architectural cost.
