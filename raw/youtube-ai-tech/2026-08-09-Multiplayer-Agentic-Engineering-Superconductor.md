# Multiplayer agentic engineering

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-09
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OL7kfezynJM

## TL;DR
Arjun Singh (co-founder and CEO, Superconductor, previously co-founder of Gradescope) presents six lessons from a year of aggressively pushing agents into a team that has worked together for over a decade, with the explicit framing that most of the industry is designing around the agent and forgetting the people. His most transferable contribution is lesson six: benchmark agents on your own codebase, because public benchmarks may have nothing to do with your work (SWE-bench is Python, they are Ruby on Rails), and plot quality against cost and quality against time as two separate frontiers. Their measured result contradicted the industry default. Anthropic models kept getting better but not faster and were clearly the most expensive for them, while Codex was fast, good, and cheaper, so they switched their default. He is explicit that this data confirmed a vibe check they already had, and that being model-and-harness agnostic is what made the switch costless. The obvious next step, which he says they are building, is automatic per-codebase routing, on the argument that no third party can know what routes well for your repo.

## Key Takeaways

**Lesson 0: be model and harness agnostic**
- The best model and harness can change weekly, either because something new ships or because the best one gets taken away. He experienced both, switching their default to Fable when it launched and switching back to Codex days later when it became unavailable.
- Open weight models are now genuinely good and much cheaper. They have been happy with GLM 5.2 and are increasing investment in it.
- The blunt version of the incentive argument: the people selling you tokens are not aligned with you. You may be happy to pay for as many tokens as a task takes, but not for more than that.
- The payoff is that a new release is a two hour evaluation, not a workflow migration.

**Lesson 1: turn every human interface into an agent and human interface**
- Progression of the problem: an agent on your laptop is trapped on your laptop. A Slack bot moves it from trapped on a laptop to trapped in Slack. Much work happens in Slack, but not all of it.
- What they built instead is one session addressable from every relevant surface. Start and collaborate in Slack, continue in the desktop or mobile app, finish in GitHub, all the same agent session with the same context.

**Lesson 2: make agent work visible and collaborative across the team**
- The session view shows every person who interacted with it, who has been notified, and who has seen it. This matters most when non-technical people trigger the work: you can immediately tell whether a support person's ticket has been vetted by an engineer.
- Because it is one session, a reviewer can ask the agent "why did you do it this way" and get an answer from the thread rather than waiting for the original author to respond on GitHub, and without reading the whole thread.
- Artifacts (screenshots, video) are surfaced everywhere regardless of where the work started, so nobody has to go find which system the image lives in.

**Lesson 3: turn every external signal into code the team can quickly evaluate**
- External signals include Slack conversations, customer and sales and onboarding calls, internal meetings, Sentry and bug tracker output, customer bug reports, emails, and feature requests.
- His critique of the current state is precise: MCP connects the agent to all these systems, but the agent still does not know what to work on, so a human is still shuttling context and saying "solve email 48." That coordination cost is the bottleneck, not access.
- Their meeting bot listens to Meet, Zoom, or Teams and creates work autonomously. They ran it for a four hour stretch at their expo booth. It links to existing work rather than duplicating it.
- The demonstrated example is nicely self-referential: an attendee said out loud that they want agents to have clear criteria for evaluating their own work before declaring done. The bot picked that up with no human involvement, created a ticket, and implemented it by adding acceptance-criteria fields to their ticket form.
- His honest calibration: he would not ship that PR as-is, but it is a concrete artifact he can play with and preview rather than an idea in a backlog. Their claimed outcome per customer call or team meeting is dozens of prototypes and at least a few shippable PRs with minimal intervention.

**Lesson 4: run everything in isolated cloud environments**
- The first benefit is what he calls eliminating lid anxiety, and he is candid that this was his personal impetus. He had a six month old at the time and did not want to be tethered to a laptop or think about whether he could walk away from it.
- The more important benefit is least privilege. Developer laptops contain credentials and data you do not want an agent reaching. Today you are in one of two camps: approving everything constantly, or hoping your auto-approve and sandbox config are correct.
- His failure scenario is specific and credible: you tell the agent to wipe a database, it finds a token on your laptop, it believes it is working against staging, and it was production. He explicitly says this is not constant but it still happens.
- Beyond credentials, the concern is exfiltration of code, projects, secrets, and content. Their answer is a configurable network sandbox with an allowlist, prompting for access when the agent tries to reach something new (a vendor's documentation, for example), grantable per ticket or per project.
- The unlock he cares most about: non-technical teammates can trigger real work without a dev environment. Support and growth people report a bug in Slack, it gets fixed, screenshots are produced, an engineer reviews and merges. No Linear ticket, no PM triage cycle.
- Why this is only now practical: getting a project running in such a sandbox used to be very painful, and agents have gotten good enough to do that setup themselves. He recommends having Claude Code or Codex do it whether or not you use his product.

**Lesson 5: benchmark agents on your own codebase**
- Method: select pull requests that represent great engineering work (agent-authored, human-authored, or hybrid, it does not matter), pick the agents you want to compare, and produce quality against cost and quality against time on your repo.
- The justification is that public benchmark composition may not resemble your work at all. Trends correlate, absolute results can differ substantially.
- Their findings, which he is careful to scope to their Ruby on Rails codebase: Anthropic agents consistently improving in quality but not in speed, and clearly the most expensive for them. Codex agents and Cursor fast and quite good. Open models improving steadily but slow.
- They switched their default to Codex on this data. They still use everything, because different models suit different use cases and they value the variety.
- The secondary benefit is eliminating evaluation FOMO. Instead of the cycle of hearing MiniMax or GLM or Kimi K2 is better, feeling anxious for weeks, spending two hours trying it, and concluding it does not fit, the benchmark answers it continuously.
- Reported scale: roughly 99.9% of their PRs are heavily agent-generated, 1.5 billion tokens in the past month for a relatively small team, 3,300 Claude Code runs at $10,000 per day in token-equivalent cost (covered by plans, not paid at that rate), and Codex at four times as many sessions for less money overall.
- Everything is still human-reviewed, with agents assisting the review, because quality, reliability, and security matter.
- The roadmap item: automatic routing derived from these per-codebase benchmarks, on the argument that a third-party router cannot know what routes well for your repo.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The per-codebase benchmarking argument is the most important thing here and it generalises well beyond coding agents. The failure mode he identifies is distribution mismatch between the benchmark and the deployment. SWE-bench is Python, his codebase is Ruby on Rails, and the ranking he measured locally differed from the public ranking. That is exactly the problem that makes third-party routing tables unreliable: a router calibrated on a public difficulty distribution is calibrated on someone else's traffic. The fix he proposes, mine your own accepted pull requests for a gold set and score candidate models against it, is the coding-agent version of building an eval set from production traffic, and it is the only defensible basis for a routing policy.

Separating quality-versus-cost from quality-versus-time into two plots is a small choice with real consequences. Collapsing them into one score forces a fixed exchange rate between dollars and seconds, and that rate is different per task. An interactive fix a developer is waiting on should buy speed with money. An overnight refactor should not. Keeping the frontiers separate lets the routing decision consume whichever axis the request actually constrains, which is the same multi-objective point that showed up in the Sonar talk about quality being a vector rather than a scalar.

His Anthropic observation deserves being stated plainly because it is a measurement, not an opinion: over successive releases quality rose while latency did not improve, and cost stayed high. If that pattern holds across codebases it says the frontier is currently being pushed on capability rather than on efficiency at the top tier, which is precisely the gap that compression, distillation, and serving optimization exist to fill. The corollary is that a routing layer capturing the cheap-and-fast cases is worth more now than it was a year ago, because the spread between the frontier model and the adequate model has widened on cost and latency while narrowing on quality.

The network allowlist with interactive grant is the right shape for a security control on an autonomous agent, and it is the same architecture GitHub described for Agentic Workflows: default-deny egress, per-project or per-task scope, and a human prompt on the exception path. Notably his justification is not only credential theft but exfiltration, which is the threat model that survives even if you solve secrets management perfectly.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
Background checks out. Singh and Sergey Karayev met in the Berkeley PhD program (Singh in robotics, Karayev in computer vision) and co-founded Gradescope in 2014 with Pieter Abbeel. Gradescope was acquired by Turnitin in 2018, and most of the Superconductor team came from that company, which supports his framing of this as a controlled experiment on a team with a decade-long collaboration baseline. Note the naming: the company is Superconductor, though he refers to "Superagent" and superagent.com verbally in the talk. Its public positioning matches the talk exactly: control AI coding agents from browser, phone, GitHub, or Slack, running many agents in the cloud with live app previews of each agent's changes.

GLM 5.2 justifies his enthusiasm and is the most interesting detail to follow up. Released 16 June 2026 by Z.ai, it is a Mixture-of-Experts reasoning model with 753B total parameters and 40B active per token, a 1M token context window, open weights under MIT licence, available both hosted and self-hostable. It scores 62.1 on SWE-bench Pro against GPT-5.5's 58.6, 81.0% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, and ties Claude Opus 4.8 on tool use. Pricing on OpenRouter runs about $1.20 per million input tokens and $4.10 per million output, roughly a sixth of GPT-5.5 for comparable long-horizon coding. Semgrep independently found it beating Claude on their cyber benchmarks. So an open-weight MoE with an 18.8x sparsity ratio is now at or above the closed frontier on coding while costing a sixth as much, with a real multi-provider market rather than a single endpoint.

That materially strengthens his model-agnosticism argument in a way he understated. The reason to stay portable is no longer just hedging against a release or a capacity withdrawal. It is that the cheapest option on the frontier is now an open-weight model you can host yourself, which means the cost axis of his quality-versus-cost plot is under your control in a way it was not when every option was someone else's API.

His Fable anecdote is the concrete instance of the risk he names, and worth remembering as a planning assumption: they adopted it as their default within days of release and reverted when it became unavailable. Any architecture that makes a specific model load-bearing is exposed to that, and it is a supply-side event you cannot influence.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Build the per-codebase benchmark for your own domain, because this is the highest-leverage idea in the talk and it maps directly onto routing work. Mine accepted pull requests or accepted model-optimization changes as a gold set, then score candidate models on it. Report quality against cost and quality against latency as separate frontiers, never as one blended score. This gold set is the artifact a defensible router is trained against, and it is the thing no vendor can supply.
2. Treat his final observation as a stated open problem you are positioned to solve. "How does some third party know what to route for your codebase" is precisely the case for domain-specific routing over general-purpose routing, and the answer is a calibration set drawn from the customer's own traffic. If you are building routing infrastructure, the productisable piece is the calibration pipeline, not the router.
3. Evaluate GLM 5.2 seriously as a self-hosted routing target this month. A 753B-total, 40B-active MoE under MIT licence at frontier coding quality is the single most consequential fact in this write-up for inference work. Self-hosting it means the sparsity structure, the quantization scheme, the expert placement, and the batching are yours to optimize, which converts routing cost savings into compounding infrastructure savings. Benchmark it on your own tasks before believing either the vendor numbers or the enthusiasm.
4. Note the frontier-model efficiency stall as a market signal. If capability at the top tier is improving while latency and cost are not, the value of the cheap-fast path in a cascade is rising. Size your routing effort against the widening spread, not against last year's spread.
5. Move agent execution off laptops if any of your agents touch cluster credentials, cloud keys, or Hugging Face tokens. His production-versus-staging failure scenario is directly applicable to a GPU cluster, where a confused agent with a real credential can destroy a training run or a dataset. Default-deny network egress with per-task grants is the control that also covers exfiltration of model weights and proprietary datasets, which is the harder problem.
6. Steal the acceptance-criteria idea the meeting bot implemented. Requiring an agent to state, up front, the concrete criteria by which its own work should be judged before it declares completion is cheap to add and directly addresses the self-validation failure mode that showed up repeatedly across this conference.
