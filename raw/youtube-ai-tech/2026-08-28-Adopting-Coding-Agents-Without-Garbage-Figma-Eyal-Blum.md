# How to Get Your Org to Adopt Coding Agents (Without Shipping Garbage) | Eyal Blum, Figma

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Bn0xro2ol8

## TL;DR
Blum describes a three-act adoption arc that both individuals and organisations move through: easy wins that feel 10x, then catastrophic failure when the same practices are applied to bigger problems, which destroys the trust built in act one, then the actual skill of guardrails, prompting, and context. The organisational problem is that adoption is uneven, so act-one teams, act-two burned teams, and act-three transformed teams all have to ship the same product together. His three named friction points are unusually candid: reduced developer agency costing job satisfaction, the best engineers becoming bottlenecks and therefore the slowest adopters, and communication inflating to three or four times its former length while conveying the same content. His answers are invest in verification and push work down the testing pyramid, replace prompting with a week of real planning, put the skeptics in charge of the roadmap, and adopt attention-aware communication that labels what a human wrote versus what an agent generated.

## Key Takeaways
- **The best engineers are the slowest adopters, and for good reasons.** They hold the undocumented institutional context, they know where every pitfall is, and they are personally holding together with "mental duct tape" all the places agents fail. So they absorb the burden, become bottlenecks, and see the problems first-hand. Blum's response is the smartest organisational move in the talk: their complaints are literally the roadmap for making agents safe in your codebase, so put them in charge of that roadmap rather than trying to convince them to use the tools. They come along once the fixes make their own lives better.
- **Verification is the highest-value investment in the codebase.** Every time you left-shift something from a human to an agent, you win. His concrete example is Playwright plus MCP letting the agent explore the running application instead of a human navigating it.
- **When an agent finds something useful, encode it as a deterministic flow.** This is the sharper version of the point: deterministic flows are repeatable, cheaper in tokens and time, and reserve the LLM for actual reasoning rather than rediscovery. Anything already known should become a test.
- **Test-driven development materially improves agent output, and the mechanism is stated correctly.** Ask the agent to work red-green-red-green and it fits the code to the verification criteria. Ask it to write code first and tests after and it fits the test to the code. Same tokens, opposite direction of causation.
- **The agent-adapted testing pyramid.** Push as much as possible down to deterministic analysis (linting, compiler, unit tests). Above that, agent review against encoded criteria such as architectural standards. Only at the apex, human review, restricted to functionality and to whether this is the right thing to build at all.
- **Planning replaces prompting, and it restores the craft.** Blum reports it is not uncommon to spend a week writing a detailed plan, making every decision, iterating, and circulating it for teammate review, then handing it to the agent as a mechanical implementation. He credits this with both the speedup and with returning the joy of building, because the developer keeps the design agency and gives away only the typing.
- **What makes a good plan, four rules.** Start with the why in a bold executive summary, which prevents agent drift, and instruct the agent not to modify it. Break it into small parts each independently verifiable. Size each part by whether you would want to review the corresponding PR in one sitting, his heuristic being "if I need to get a cup of coffee first, it is too big." Put a validation gate or exit criteria on every phase, so stage two is not built on stage one's unvalidated assumptions.
- **His concrete result: roughly 20 PRs from a plan, nothing bigger than about 100 lines,** representing about six weeks of pre-AI coding work delivered in one week including the review cycle, so a claimed 5x. Notably he insists on counting the review cycle, which most such claims omit.
- **Do not centralise on one workflow.** He argues there are diminishing returns to standardising everyone's loop, as long as each person's flow works and others can iterate with them.
- **Attention-aware communication is the most original contribution.** Human attention is the scarce resource, so mark what was AI-generated versus human-written so readers know how much scrutiny to apply and how much slop to expect. His team's convention: every PR description opens with a short hand-written summary of what the change does, followed by the AI-generated description, signalling explicitly which part to trust.
- **He tells a story against himself to make the point land.** He ran an AI analysis of a skeptical senior engineer's PR comments and sent it without distinguishing his words from the model's. The engineer was genuinely offended, saying he had not expected someone he respected that much to send something that sloppy. Blum's read: he should have labelled it and stated his intent, which was to ask for the context he lacked.
- **Meet people where they are.** The single most effective adoption lever he found was tagging an agent into an existing Slack thread and letting it close the loop there, framed non-passive-aggressively as "let's see if the agent can get it this time." A good experience there leads people to try it themselves.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The TDD finding is the most rigorous claim in the talk and it generalises well beyond code. Writing tests first fixes the objective function before the search begins, so the agent is optimising against a specification. Writing tests afterward lets the agent choose an objective that its existing output already satisfies, which is reward hacking in miniature. For Amit's work the direct analogue is experiment design: define the acceptance metric and the ablation before generating any implementation, or the implementation will quietly define what counts as success. This is the same failure mode as tuning a compression method against a benchmark you selected after seeing preliminary results.

The independently-verifiable-phases rule is a specific and important fix for a real failure mode. An unvalidated stage one means stages two through five inherit its assumptions, and by the time anything fails the error is buried five layers deep and expensive to attribute. Phase gates convert a single high-variance long run into a sequence of short verified runs, which caps the cost of any individual failure. Combined with Ramp's activity-level durability point from the same conference, the pattern is clear: checkpoint at verifiable boundaries, and the boundary should be defined by what you can check, not by what feels like a natural unit of work.

The "encode what the agent discovered as a deterministic flow" habit is the highest-return practice here and it is measurable. Every repeated exploration the agent performs is tokens spent rediscovering something already known. Converting it into a test or script pays once and saves on every subsequent run. Concretely: audit agent traces for repeated tool-call sequences, and every recurring sequence is a candidate for codification. That is the same optimization Cloudflare made by pre-aggregating data and Notion made by precomputing context bundles, arrived at independently for the third time in this conference.

The attention-aware communication point deserves elevating from etiquette to architecture. If human review is the binding constraint, as Clare Liguori's talk at the same event concluded, then anything that lets a reviewer allocate attention correctly is a direct throughput gain. Labelling provenance is a cheap metadata channel that lets the reviewer skip cheaply-generated text and concentrate on load-bearing human judgment. Unlabelled AI text imposes a uniform-scrutiny tax on the scarcest resource in the system. Blum's story about the offended engineer is not really about manners, it is about a reviewer being unable to budget attention and defaulting to distrust of the whole message.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The context that most sharpens this talk is the empirical record on whether AI-assisted codebases actually degrade, because "without shipping garbage" turns out to be the right worry.

GitClear's research is the strongest available longitudinal data and it is unflattering. Analysing over 200 million lines of code changes, GitClear found duplicated code blocks rising sharply: measured per million changed lines, block duplication climbed from 40.3 in 2023 to 73.0 year-to-date in 2026, an 81% increase over 2023 and the highest level on record. Meanwhile refactored or moved code fell from roughly 25% of changed lines in 2021 to under 10% in 2024, and 2024 was the first year in their dataset where copy-paste exceeded moved code. Their framing of the consequence is precise and worth quoting in substance: a duplicated block imposes a propagation tax, because a developer changing one copy of a five-line block inherits the obligation to find every sibling across files and domains they may not know, and decide whether the change must propagate.

That data validates Blum's premise and complicates his solution. His verification pyramid catches functional regressions well. It does not catch duplication, because duplicated code passes every test by construction. The maintainability decay GitClear measures is invisible to linting, compilers, and unit tests, and it is exactly the kind of thing his apex human reviewer is least likely to catch when reviewing 20 PRs of 10 to 100 lines each in isolation. His own reported artefact, 20 small PRs from one plan, is the shape of change most likely to introduce duplication and least likely to reveal it in review. The missing layer in his pyramid is a codebase-level structural metric (duplication rate, coupling, churn) measured across PRs rather than within them.

On Figma itself, the AI-first pivot he references is real and substantial. At Config 2026 Figma unveiled AI agents, code-native design tools, code layers on the canvas, and user-buildable custom AI plugins and skills. The independent assessment of output quality is mixed in a way that is relevant to his talk's theme: Figma's generated code is useful for inspiration and rapid iteration but is generally not production-ready, and outputs typically need human review for accessibility, semantics, and cleanliness. That is the same accept-the-probabilistic-core-and-wrap-it-in-verification conclusion, seen from the product side.

Two caveats on the talk's numbers. The 5x figure is one engineer's account of two plans, self-measured against his own estimate of what the pre-AI work would have taken, which is the weakest form of productivity evidence even though his inclusion of the review cycle is more honest than most. And the three friction points, while credible and specific, are reported observations rather than measured effects. The communication-inflation observation in particular ("emails three or four times as long saying the same thing, two or three times as many of them") is the kind of claim that would be straightforward to measure and has not been.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Write the acceptance criteria before generating anything, every time.** The TDD result is the cleanest causal claim in this talk: specify first and the agent optimises toward the spec, specify after and it rationalises what it produced. For Amit this applies verbatim to experiment design, define the metric and the ablation set before the implementation exists.
- **Add a codebase-level structural metric to the verification pyramid.** Blum's pyramid misses the failure mode GitClear measures, because duplicated code passes all tests. Track duplication rate, coupling, and churn across PRs, not within them, and gate on the trend. This is the single most valuable addition to his framework.
- **Mine agent traces for repeated tool-call sequences and codify each one.** Every recurring sequence is tokens spent rediscovering a known fact. Convert to a script or test, pay once, save forever. This is measurable as tool calls and tokens eliminated per task.
- **Adopt phase gates sized by reviewability.** The coffee-cup heuristic is a genuinely good proxy for the right unit of work, and independent per-phase validation prevents error compounding across stages. Amit should apply this to multi-stage experimental pipelines, where an unvalidated stage one silently invalidates everything downstream.
- **Label provenance in all written output.** A short hand-written summary followed by generated detail, in PRs, in design docs, in Slack. If reviewer attention is the bottleneck, provenance metadata is a direct throughput gain, and Blum's offended-engineer story is the cost of skipping it.
- **Put the skeptics in charge of the enablement roadmap.** This inverts the usual approach and it is correct. The people who see the failures are describing the work queue. For Amit, whoever on his team most distrusts the agent tooling is the person whose complaints should define the next quarter of tooling investment.
- **Do not force a single workflow.** Blum's diminishing-returns argument on standardisation is well taken, with one exception implied by the GitClear data: the verification layer and the structural metrics must be shared and enforced centrally, even when everyone's authoring loop is personal.
