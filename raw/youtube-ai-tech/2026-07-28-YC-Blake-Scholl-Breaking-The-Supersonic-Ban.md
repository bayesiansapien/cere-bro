# Blake Scholl: Breaking the Supersonic Ban

**Channel:** Y Combinator
**Published:** 2026-07-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs
**Secondary domain:** global-news (tech policy, US-China manufacturing)

## TL;DR
Boom Supersonic's founder gives the origin-to-present talk plus an unusually long Q&A. The technical core is a claim about iteration cost: something that historically took billions of dollars and thousands of people was done by 50 people, because Boom moved aircraft engineering out of spreadsheets and into real software. Their tool Makeboom defines an entire airplane in a configuration file and runs a whole-aircraft simulation in minutes, which let them search the space of conceivable supersonic jets to pick which one to build rather than building one and hoping. Bladerunner does the same for turbine blades, collapsing a dozens-of-engineers, many-months loop to real time. They vertically integrated a machine shop to go from digital design to prototype part in 24 hours, and their own engine test stand so they never queue for someone else's test cell. The regulatory story is the headline: XB-1 broke the sound barrier six times with no audible boom on the ground by exploiting Mach cutoff, which converted "how quiet is quiet enough" from a fight into a non-question, and 115 days later an executive order made civil supersonic flight legal again. The financing insight is the sleeper: because they built their own engine, they can sell it as a stationary natural gas power turbine, generating test data, reliability hours and capital without shipping an airplane. Q&A highlights: regulatory capture is usually incumbents weaponising regulators rather than safety concern, "why now" is a bad question that funnels founders into a small pool of recently-possible ideas, and his need for software engineers went *up* as the cost of writing software went down.

## Key Takeaways
- **The central mechanism is reducing cost of iteration in both bits and atoms, and it is the only reason a 50-person team could do this.** Not a better idea, not more capital. Loop latency.
- **Aircraft are the anti-modular system and that is precisely why the tooling mattered.** Add a row of seats, the fuselage gets heavier, engines need more thrust, the wing gets redesigned. Everything couples to everything. Classical practice handled this with specialists passing spreadsheets between disciplines, which makes each iteration enormously expensive and eventually forces a "good enough, start building" decision on an unoptimised design.
- **"Spreadsheet engineering is baby software" is the sharpest line in the talk.** It is real code being treated as a second-class citizen: no automated integration, no tests, no CI. The fix was to promote it to actual software with actual engineering practice around it.
- **Makeboom turned design selection into a search problem rather than a guess.** You cannot find product-market fit on a supersonic airliner by building one and seeing if anyone likes it. Being able to evaluate the universe of buildable aircraft digitally is what let them narrow to the correct one. This is the single most transferable idea in the talk.
- **The Mach cutoff result is a genuine engineering-changes-the-politics story.** Break the sound barrier high enough and at the right speed for current atmospheric conditions and the boom refracts upward, U-turns, and never reaches the ground. His framing is the strategic insight: with no boom at all, there is nothing left to argue about.
- **Regulatory strategy is contextual and he gives a real decision rule.** Uber ignored regulators because there was an entrenched enemy, the taxi industry, actively using regulation as a weapon, so customers had to be the ally. Boom had no entrenched enemy since Boeing thought they were cute and had self-inflicted problems, and the product was safety-critical, so early engagement won. Result: an approval process that normally takes months completed in 90 minutes, and the first ever civil supersonic flight permit issued before the airplane had even flown.
- **"Boom plus FAA versus the problem, not Boom versus FAA" is the operative framing.** They invited the FAA to see anything, anytime, and showed plans before finalising them.
- **His claim about the nature of regulation is blunt and worth sitting with.** Most regulation does not come from a well-meaning person protecting the public. It comes from an entrenched, lazy interest that runs to Washington to shut down competition. He calls it deeply unethical and says it happens constantly.
- **Superpower is the best structural idea in the talk and it solves the hardest problem in deep tech.** Development that needs billions in R&D, with unknowable total cost and timeline, is nearly unfinanceable. Selling the supersonic engine separately as a ground-based natural gas power turbine produces revenue, reliability data and learning years before an airplane flies. First unit ships to a customer within 12 months.
- **Vertical integration was forced by supplier iteration latency, not chosen for margin.** He has "no kind words" for aerospace suppliers. Their Denver R&D shop, where engineers work hand-in-hand with manufacturing technicians, is explicitly a room that does not exist at GE, Rolls-Royce or Boeing.
- **His US-versus-China manufacturing answer is the most strategically interesting thing in the Q&A.** You do not beat China at its own game by repatriating the manufacturing that left. China hoovered up the tool and die industry, so Boom's answer is to eliminate tooling entirely: an all-digital process going from digital design to the most advanced turbine blade in the world in 24 hours with zero tooling. Invent the next generation of manufacturing rather than recompeting on the last one.
- **He rejects the AI-kills-software-jobs thesis with a concrete counterexample from his own company.** Boom needs more software engineers post-AI, not fewer, because when anyone including a hardware engineer can produce code, someone has to make sure the architecture is coherent. Cheaper software production raises the value of architectural judgement.
- **The "why now" critique is a genuinely useful contrarian point.** The belief that every technology arrives at the earliest possible moment funnels founders into a small pool of recently-unlocked ideas, which is why there is an explosion of AI companies now and was one of photo-sharing apps before. The technology for supersonic existed long before Boom. Nobody had started. "The answer to why now can be because I started now."
- **He names his own biggest process mistake, and it is the one worth copying.** XB-1 was too ambitious a first prototype. He should have planned something simpler that gave multiple shots on goal, because iterating at the product level is how you discover you got the *requirements* wrong, not just the engineering. Ten years to a proof point, and by his own estimate it should have been two or three.
- **The confusion list is a concrete self-teaching method.** Learning aerospace from textbooks and remedial Khan Academy, he kept a written list of things he could answer correctly on a test but did not actually understand from first principles, and worked to remove one per week. The list grew without bound. The value was not clearing it, it was building the internal signal that distinguishes real understanding from plausible hand-waving.
- **The hiring rule for inexperienced engineers is well-designed.** It is fine for a young engineer to do something they have never done, just as it is fine to do something nobody has ever done. But if somebody in the world has done it before, you must find them, call them, and hear their advice. You do not have to take it.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The structural insight is that Boom's problem is a coupled, non-separable multi-objective design search under a fixed evaluation budget, and their entire engineering strategy is an attack on evaluation cost. This is exactly the shape of neural architecture and compression search, and the parallel is close enough to be operationally useful rather than merely cute.

Consider what the classical aerospace process is, described in optimisation terms. Each specialist owns one objective in one spreadsheet. Evaluating a single candidate design requires a sequential human handoff chain across disciplines, so one function evaluation costs weeks to months of a large team. With an evaluation budget that low, you cannot search. You can only take a handful of expert-guessed points and then declare the best one good enough. That is not optimisation, it is a manually seeded local descent that terminates on schedule pressure rather than convergence. And crucially, the coupling Scholl describes means the objective is not separable: adding seats propagates to mass, thrust, wing geometry and systems packaging. Per-discipline optimisation does not compose into a good aircraft, which is the aerospace equivalent of layer-wise pruning sensitivity failing to predict joint-compression behaviour.

Makeboom changes the exponent on the whole problem. Airplane as a configuration file, one script for whole-aircraft simulation, results in minutes: range, fuel burn, passenger capacity, trade-offs. Function evaluation drops from months to minutes, which is roughly four orders of magnitude, and that changes what class of algorithm is even admissible. At months per evaluation you get expert guessing. At minutes per evaluation you get genuine design-space exploration, sensitivity analysis, and the ability to ask which point in the space maximises market opportunity subject to physical feasibility. Note what he uses it for: not merely evaluating ideas that engineers proposed, but *studies of which airplane to build out of the infinitely many conceivable supersonic jets*. That is the difference between a verifier and a search procedure, and it is the same difference as between evaluating a hand-designed quantisation scheme and searching mixed-precision assignments over a whole network.

Bladerunner is the inner-loop version, and the compression ratio is even more extreme: a process that took dozens of engineers many months now runs in real time with a couple of engineers, giving simultaneous structural and aerodynamic response to a blade geometry change. Real-time means the loop is now interactive, which is a qualitative change and not just a quantitative one. It moves the human from batch-submitting candidates to steering continuously, which is the same shift as going from overnight profiling runs to a live kernel autotuner.

The atoms half of the strategy is the part most engineering organisations get wrong, and it maps directly onto the gap between simulated and measured performance. A fast simulator that disagrees with reality is a fast way to be confidently wrong. Boom's answer is a 24-hour path from digital design to physical prototype part in their own shop, plus their own engine test stand thirty minutes from the engineers so they never wait for someone else's test cell. In compression and inference terms this is the difference between reasoning about FLOP counts and parameter reductions, and actually measuring latency and throughput on target hardware whenever you want. The queueing detail matters more than it sounds: an organisation that must reserve slots on shared test infrastructure has a real iteration latency set by the queue, not by the test. Anyone who has waited on a shared A100 or H100 pool to validate a kernel change knows that the wall-clock cost of an experiment is dominated by scheduling, not compute, and that this silently caps how many experiments get run.

Two further mechanics worth extracting. First, tooling elimination as a strategic axis. Their turbine blade process targets zero tooling, going digital design to finished blade in 24 hours. The generalisation is that fixed setup cost per design variant is what makes iteration expensive, and removing the fixed cost matters more than speeding up the variable part. The analogue is any compression pipeline requiring per-configuration calibration, recompilation or kernel specialisation: that fixed cost, not the search algorithm, is usually what caps how many configurations get evaluated. Second, Superpower is a way to extract learning from a subsystem before the full system exists. The engine ships and earns and accumulates reliability hours as a ground turbine while the airframe is still years out. The parallel is deploying a component of a research stack, a router, a quantised serving path, a distilled draft model, into production on its own to harvest real-world reliability data long before the full system is ready.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The load-bearing facts check out, and the timeline is slightly compressed in the telling. XB-1 broke the sound barrier on 28 January 2025, reaching Mach 1.122 at 35,290 feet, and it is correctly described as the first independently developed supersonic jet and the first civil supersonic aircraft built in America to fly supersonically. Scholl says "last year" and "just about a year ago," which at a July 2026 talk understates the gap by roughly six months. The boomless result is confirmed: six supersonic passes with no audible boom reaching the ground via Mach cutoff, where the boom refracts in the atmosphere and dissipates before reaching the surface. Boom has productised this as Boomless Cruise, targeting Mach 1.3 over land for Overture and claiming up to 90 minutes saved on US coast-to-coast routes.

The regulatory chain is real and has advanced further than the talk states, with one correction. A Trump executive order in June 2025 directed the FAA to lift the 52-year overland supersonic ban for aircraft producing no audible boom, which is about 129 days after the 28 January flight rather than the 115 he cites. Bicameral legislation, the Supersonic Aviation Modernization Act, was introduced on 14 May 2025 to repeal 14 CFR 91.817, and the House passed it in March 2026. One nuance he glosses: it passed by voice vote, which means unopposed rather than a recorded unanimous tally. The bill requires the FAA to revise its rules within one year. His claim that it cleared Senate Commerce Committee unanimously the week before this talk is consistent with that trajectory, and I could not independently confirm that specific vote.

Where the talk is most optimistic is the part it says least about. Symphony engine testing was anticipated to begin during 2026, with the test site at Colorado Air and Space Port expanding to full-scale testing through the year. Scholl maintained a 2029 entry-into-service target at the AIAA Aviation Forum on 11 June 2026. That target depends on a clean-sheet engine, developed in-house by a startup after Rolls-Royce withdrew, completing development and certification, and industry analysis has flagged specifically that Overture's 2029 passenger service promise has substantial unsolved items. No full-scale Symphony has yet demonstrated the performance the airliner requires, which is the actual critical path and gets one passing mention in the talk. There is also a factory ambiguity worth flagging: the Overture Superfactory at Piedmont Triad International Airport in Greensboro already had its ribbon-cutting and has been reported as sitting unfilled pending Overture production start. The building Scholl describes, leased in January 2026, machines arriving six months later, first production engine parts within weeks, is a different and engine-focused facility. Treat "superfactory" in this talk as the engine plant, not the Greensboro airframe plant.

The honest summary: the demonstrator flew, the boomless physics was demonstrated, the ban is being dismantled, and the tooling and iteration claims are credible and specific. The airliner does not exist, the engine has not run at full scale, and 2029 is a target rather than a schedule.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **Audit the wall-clock cost of one function evaluation in your current pruning or quantisation work, and treat that number as the thing to attack.** Not accuracy, not the search algorithm. If evaluating one compression configuration end to end takes a day, you are running the aerospace spreadsheet process and you will ship the first design that clears the bar rather than a good one. Makeboom's four-orders-of-magnitude reduction is what made design search possible at all.
2. **Build the config-file-plus-one-script harness for the compression stack, explicitly modelled on Makeboom.** One declarative file specifying sparsity pattern, bit widths per layer, expert count, KV cache policy and batch and sequence shape. One command producing predicted accuracy, latency, memory footprint and throughput. This exists in fragments in most research repos as scripts and notebooks passed between people, which is exactly the spreadsheet-engineering failure he describes. Promote it to real software with CI and regression tests on the simulator's own predictions.
3. **Separate the search from the verifier and be explicit about which one you have.** Most compression tooling evaluates human-proposed candidates. Once evaluation is cheap, ask his actual question instead: out of all buildable configurations, which point maximises the objective under the deployment constraint? That reframing is what turned Boom's design process from guessing to selection.
4. **Attack fixed per-variant setup cost before optimising the variable cost.** The zero-tooling turbine blade is the model. If every quantisation variant needs recalibration, recompilation or a hand-written kernel, that fixed cost is what actually caps the number of experiments. Removing it beats making each run faster.
5. **Measure the queue, not just the benchmark.** If validating on target hardware means waiting for a shared GPU pool, that queue is your true iteration latency, and it is invisible in every experiment log. Boom built its own test stand thirty minutes from the engineers specifically to delete this. Quantify the queue for one week, then argue for dedicated capacity with that number.
6. **Find the Superpower in your own stack: a subsystem that can ship and earn learning before the full system is ready.** A router that reduces cost on production traffic today, a quantised serving path deployed for a single non-critical workload, a distilled draft model used for speculative decoding. It generates reliability data, real failure modes and internal credibility while the ambitious version is still in development. This is the strongest funding and de-risking argument available for a long-horizon research programme.
7. **Iterate at the product level to catch wrong requirements, not just wrong engineering.** His stated biggest mistake. In research terms: a smaller end-to-end pipeline that reaches a deployment decision, run several times, beats one heroic model that takes a year and only tells you whether your engineering was right. Multiple shots on goal is how you learn the objective was misspecified.
8. **Adopt the confusion list, literally.** A written list of things you can answer correctly but do not understand from first principles. For inference optimisation the candidates are obvious: memory-bandwidth versus compute bounds per kernel, why a specific attention variant actually wins on real hardware, what tensor core utilisation is really measuring, where MoE all-to-all cost genuinely goes. The value is developing the internal signal that separates understanding from plausible hand-waving, which is the failure mode of reading papers rather than deriving results.
9. **Apply the phone-call rule on the team.** If nobody has done a thing before, go ahead. If somebody has, find them and hear them out before starting, and you are not obliged to follow their advice. Cheap insurance against re-deriving a known dead end, without the deference that makes people defer to the past.
10. **Take the "why now" critique seriously as a research-agenda filter.** The AI-company explosion he cites is the same crowding dynamic that has everyone chasing the same three optimisation ideas the moment a paper lands. The corollary is that unfashionable and technically available problems in compression and routing are underexplored not because they are hard but because nobody started.
