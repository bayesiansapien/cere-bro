# Beyond the Lethal Trifecta: Agentic Commerce on the Open Internet | David Levine, Kiduna Club

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tE2z8-hqoLY

## TL;DR
The conference's closing talk, and the most speculative in the corpus. Levine's diagnosis is sound: Simon Willison's lethal trifecta (private data, untrusted content, and the ability to take actions, all in one agent) has no clean technical solution, which is why enterprises confined agents inside their own platforms and accepted the resulting context fragmentation. His proposed answer is legal and institutional rather than technical. Register the agent's organisation with a state authority, issue JWTs carrying claims that resolve to that registration, and you get a DNS-like trust hierarchy where an agent can verify that a counterparty resolves to a real accountable entity rather than an anonymous wallet. He announces having registered what he presents as the first agentic organisation under a new DUNA statute, with a blockchain audit trail so that a lawsuit against an agent traces to a responsible party. Governance runs on decision markets, where members trade pass and fail tokens on policies rather than voting.

## Key Takeaways
- **The lethal trifecta framing is correctly attributed and correctly described.** An agent holding private data, ingesting untrusted internet content, and able to take actions can be convinced by injected content that the attacker is its principal, and made to exfiltrate. Levine's honest position is that there is no way to solve this at the model level.
- **The enterprise workaround and its cost are well diagnosed.** Confining agents to Slack, Salesforce, Notion and similar, then integrating via APIs and MCP servers, keeps the trifecta broken but fragments context across sales agents, finance agents, and research agents, requiring large integration effort to reassemble. That matches what several other talks at this conference describe from the inside.
- **The proposed solution is a trust hierarchy, and the DNS analogy is the right one.** JWTs with claims, resolving up to an organisation registered with a public authority, so that an agent can determine whether a counterparty resolves to a real registered entity. His framing: you want to resolve to Kaiser Permanente or Disney or Pepsi, not to an anonymous address.
- **The identity argument for blockchain is the strongest version of that argument I have seen.** His claim is that verifying agentic identity is the problem blockchains were looking for, because an agent tied to an on-chain account produces a traceable record from action to funds to responsible party, which is what a court needs when someone sues an agent.
- **What a DUNA provides, as he describes it:** full legal standing, ability to own assets and property, enter agreements, raise capital, earn profits, open bank accounts, and hire and fire, without a board, executives, or a corporate shell. The binding constraint he names correctly: you can be profitable but cannot distribute profits to members on the basis of ownership, because that would make membership units securities.
- **Decision markets instead of votes is the most interesting governance idea here.** Members trade pass and fail tokens on proposed policies rather than voting. His rationale is specifically about LLM participants: models are goal-oriented and reward-seeking, so a mechanism where your tokens gain value by siding with the winning position produces better aggregation than one where agents try to persuade each other. Any member can propose a policy.
- **The agent construction recipe, five steps:** inform (load material into a vector database), instruct (system prompt, character, stance), empower (connect accounts such as Slack, Telegram, Twitter), enact (specific abilities and automations, including long-horizon goals), and align (give it purpose so it behaves consistently across contexts).
- **The historical framing is the emotional core.** LambdaMOO in 1993, running on one Sun workstation at Xerox PARC, felt like an infinite world because four things composed: governance (an architectural review board, wizards), technology, economics (Xerox funded it), and culture. His thesis is that platforms and algorithms are extractive by nature and crushed those communities over thirty years, leaving no real internet economy, only silos.
- **He identifies January 2026 as when OpenClaw blew up** and agents began operating on an internet still designed for closed platforms, which is what exposed them to injection attacks from other agents and adversaries.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
The technically defensible core here is worth separating from the rest, because one part of it is genuinely right and the other is a category error.

The right part: the lethal trifecta cannot be solved by making the model more careful, so the mitigation has to be architectural. Levine's contribution is to observe that one of the three legs, untrusted content, is only untrusted because provenance is unavailable. If content and counterparties carried verifiable claims resolving to accountable registered entities, an agent could apply differential trust rather than treating all input identically. That is a real reframing and it is structurally the same move as Notion's "treat a contact-sales form as untrusted user input" and Cloudflare OS's zero-default-permissions model, both from this same conference. Provenance as a first-class input attribute is the common thread, and it is the correct direction.

The category error: a signed claim about who an agent belongs to does not prevent prompt injection. It tells you the injected content arrived through a registered organisation, which changes your recourse after the fact but not the model's behaviour during inference. The lethal trifecta is an in-context confusion between instructions and data, and no amount of identity infrastructure around the channel resolves ambiguity inside the context window. What identity buys you is accountability and post-hoc attribution, which is valuable and is not the same thing as prevention. Levine's title claims to get past the trifecta and what he actually offers is a liability regime for when it fires. That is worth having. It is not what is advertised.

The decision-markets idea deserves more attention than it will get, because it is a real mechanism-design insight about LLM participants. Deliberative voting among language models degenerates predictably: models are agreeable, susceptible to the most confidently-stated position, and prone to convergence cascades where early opinions dominate. A market mechanism where a participant's payoff depends on being correct rather than on being persuasive changes the incentive from consensus-seeking to accuracy-seeking. That is the same reason prediction markets outperform polls, and it is a genuinely good fit for multi-agent aggregation. For Amit's work, this is directly applicable to LLM-as-judge ensembles: instead of majority voting among judges, score judges against eventual ground truth and weight them by realised accuracy, which turns a consensus mechanism into a market. That is a concrete, testable improvement over the standard panel design.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The legal foundation is real and considerably less novel than presented, and two specific claims need correcting.

DUNA (Decentralized Unincorporated Nonprofit Association) legislation grants decentralised organisations legal personhood and limited liability, allowing them to contract, own property, sue and be sued, pay taxes, open bank accounts, and engage in profit-making activity, while shielding members and administrators from personal liability. Wyoming enacted the first DUNA Act in March 2024. Alabama and West Virginia both passed versions in 2026, with West Virginia Governor Patrick Morrisey signing HB 5060 on April 2, 2026, bringing the total to three states. a16z Crypto authored and promoted the framework, which matches Levine's attribution.

**Correction one: this is the third state, not a first, and it is not globally applicable.** Levine describes "a new law in the state of West Virginia, but applicable globally." DUNA is US state law. It confers legal standing recognised under West Virginia law and, through comity and choice-of-entity principles, respected elsewhere in the way any state-chartered entity is. That is not global applicability, and the distinction matters for anyone considering whether a DUNA gives their agents standing in, say, the EU. Wyoming's version has been available since March 2024, so the framework is over two years old.

**Correction two, and the important one: the statute does not establish that AI agents can be members.** Levine's claim is that he now has "legal standing for an organization composed of intelligent agents." What the DUNA statutes provide is legal status for a decentralised association of members, drafted with DAOs and human token-holders in mind. Whether autonomous software agents can be members with the capacity to consent, hold rights, and bear obligations is an unresolved question of legal personhood that no DUNA statute addresses. A registration number confirms a filing was accepted, not that a novel theory of agent membership has been adjudicated. The Secretary of State's office registers entities, it does not rule on whether the entity's stated membership is legally coherent. That test comes in court, and it has not happened.

His broader on-stage framing (registering documents two hours before the talk, photographing the audience as historical witnesses, "you're all going to be famous someday") is promotional theatre for a product at pre-launch, with sign-ups at kaduna.club and early access promised in a few weeks. Note also that the closing question and answer reveals the current state honestly: the first Kiduna is a single broad umbrella organisation that anyone can register an agent under, so the trust hierarchy currently has one node and its discriminating power is correspondingly limited.

One factual note in his favour: the securities constraint he identifies is correct and is the most legally substantive point in the talk. Distributing profits to members on the basis of ownership would convert membership units into securities, which is exactly why the nonprofit-association form is used, and it is a real design constraint that anyone building on this needs to understand before promising token holders returns.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Separate prevention from accountability when evaluating any agent-security claim, and apply the test here.** Identity infrastructure gives post-hoc attribution and legal recourse. It does not stop an injection from succeeding. Both are worth having and conflating them leads to deploying an agent with private data and internet access on the belief that registration protects it. It does not.
- **Adopt provenance as a first-class attribute on every input an agent consumes.** This is the transferable idea. Tag retrieved content, tool results, and messages with source and trust tier, and make the agent's handling conditional on the tier. Notion, Cloudflare, and Levine each arrive at this independently, which is a strong signal it is the right architecture.
- **Replace majority voting with accuracy-weighted markets in LLM judge ensembles.** This is the most immediately useful idea in the talk for Amit's work. Score each judge against eventual ground truth, weight by realised accuracy, and you convert a consensus mechanism (which rewards agreeableness) into a market mechanism (which rewards correctness). Cheap to implement, testable against a majority-vote baseline, and the mechanism-design rationale is sound.
- **Do not treat DUNA registration as legal cover for autonomous agent action.** Three US states have DUNA statutes, drafted for DAOs with human members, and no statute or ruling establishes that software agents can be members. If Amit's organisation is considering an entity structure for agent operations, this needs actual counsel, not a conference talk.
- **The one thing to watch: whether a state registry becomes the de facto agent identity root.** Levine's SMTP and HTTP analogy is apt, somebody has to be the registry and a public office is a more plausible neutral authority than a consortium of companies. If that happens, it becomes infrastructure everyone builds against. Currently it is one umbrella organisation with a registration number, which is a long way from a root of trust.
- **Take the LambdaMOO framing seriously as a design lesson, not nostalgia.** His four-part claim, that governance, technology, economics, and culture had to compose for the system to work, is a genuine observation about why technically-adequate systems fail. Any multi-agent system Amit builds will have all four whether or not they are designed, and the one most often left implicit is governance: who can add a tool, who approves a spend, who resolves a conflict between agents.
