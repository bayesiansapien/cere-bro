# How do you diffuse AI into the real world? | Varun Shenoy, Long Lake

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B0fjR3yaZFU

## TL;DR
Shenoy's framing is that capability is no longer the bottleneck, diffusion is. Walk into a 200-person property management firm and nothing has changed, which is exactly what history predicts: electricity was demoed at Pearl Street in the 1880s and Ford's electrified moving assembly line arrived in 1924, because adopting a general-purpose technology requires ripping out equipment, installing new equipment, and retraining everyone. Long Lake's answer is structural rather than technical. They do not sell software, they buy the businesses. 35 acquisitions across HOA and property management, architecture, and HR services, culminating in a $6.3 billion take-private of American Express Global Business Travel. Because they own the outcome, a failing AI deployment is their problem and not a customer's complaint. Three lessons follow: earn your way up the autonomy ladder rather than jumping to co-worker, mine real-world traces for evals and post-training data that no frontier lab has, and treat continual learning and enablement as one loop rather than two teams.

## Key Takeaways
- **The autonomy ladder, five rungs.** Co-pilot (RAG chatbot, instant answers). Synchronous agent (Claude Code, Codex, runs 1 to 5 minutes with tools and skills, human still initiates each turn). Asynchronous agent (goes away and returns, and critically the trigger need not be the user, an external event or job queue can wake it and it can proactively offer advice). Long-running agent (hours to weeks, which he identifies as the current frontier problem labs and Long Lake are both working on). AI co-worker (proactive partner). His central operational claim: everyone tries to sell you the top rung, and you have to earn the right to do more, both because models are not there for some tasks and because the organisation has to be brought along.
- **The jagged frontier applied to autonomy, which is the sharpest idea in the talk.** For code, the synchronous agent is solved (agent on a desktop with file system access) and the asynchronous agent is also largely solved (same agent, wrapped in a sandbox, builds and tests and returns a PR). His observation about why: engineers are already culturally comfortable with parallelism, launching ten jobs and accepting that job seven finishes before job three. For services, the synchronous co-working agent exists, and the asynchronous services agent is genuinely unsolved, because services work is done serially by people who clean their inbox one email at a time.
- **His proposed shortcut is the most interesting technical bet here.** Rather than waiting for models to get good at services knowledge work, represent knowledge work as code, so the existing code competence transfers. Models are trained on code, want to write code, and are very good at it.
- **The valuable tasks are not on the internet.** How to close the books when receipts are missing, how to scope a building from a blueprint collaboratively, how to coordinate vendors to fix a roof. That knowledge lives in people's heads and in 20-year-old software.
- **The trace flywheel, and its three distinct products.** Agents collaborate with employees on real work, producing traces of tool calls, hiccups, and papercuts. Those yield (1) automatically built and scored real-world evals with actual ground truth (did the roof get repaired, did the books close), plus explicit feedback (thumbs) and implicit feedback (the diff between what the AI generated and what was ultimately submitted, which he correctly identifies as rich data almost nobody has); (2) internal post-training on data that is out of distribution for frontier labs; (3) the agents themselves, customised per company, per user, and per client.
- **Every week's hill-climbing benchmark becomes next week's regression test.** That ratchet is the mechanism by which the system cannot silently regress, and it is the strongest process idea in the talk.
- **"The exceptions are the job."** His illustration contrasts the smooth-slope mental model of LLM tasks against the real terrain of hills, ravines, and death by a thousand papercuts. The demo is the slope. The job is the exceptions.
- **Continual learning and enablement are one loop, not two org charts.** Research or platform owns continual learning, growth or deployment owns enablement, and they are typically siloed. But the agent only improves if people use it, and people only use it if it is worth adopting. So the snowball is usage driving learning driving a better agent driving usage.
- **The elephant: how do you get the initial usage.** Everyone assumes usage shows up. Giving Claude Code to an entire enterprise does not change a person who has closed the books the same way for twenty years.
- **His answer is "extreme software-service co-design,"** modelled explicitly on Jensen Huang's hardware-software co-design. Meet people metaphorically (embed the product natively in Excel, the ERP, the 3D design tool, Outlook) to keep enablement energy low, and physically (fly out, lunch-and-learns, run a cotton candy stand at their conference, go mountain biking and ask what actually hurts, sit one-on-one and watch them work). His claim: you cannot co-design software with a services business over Zoom or a support ticket.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
Three things here are directly useful to Amit, and one is a genuine research direction.

First, the async-parallelism asymmetry is a scheduling insight, not a cultural one. Code work parallelises because it has cheap forking (a sandbox or worktree), independent verification (build and test), and commutative merge (a PR). Services work resists parallelism because state is shared and mutable (one inbox, one ledger, one vendor relationship), verification requires external parties, and operations do not commute. That is a precise diagnosis and it tells you what to build: to parallelise services work you need to construct the missing primitives, a forkable snapshot of business state, a local verifier that does not require a phone call, and a merge discipline for conflicting agent actions. Framed that way it is a distributed-systems problem, which is exactly how Notion's team described their GTM work in this same conference. Independent convergence.

Second, the implicit feedback signal is the highest-value asset described in the talk and it is undervalued in the ML literature. The diff between AI-generated output and what a human actually submitted is a preference pair harvested for free from ordinary work, at scale, with no annotation budget. It is exactly the data shape needed for preference optimisation, and unlike thumbs-up data it carries the corrected target rather than just a scalar. For Amit's own work this generalises directly: any place where a human edits a model's output before using it is a free source of paired training data, and instrumenting for the diff rather than for a rating is a design decision worth making up front.

Third, the "represent knowledge work as code" bet deserves scrutiny because it is doing more work than it appears. The claim is that transferring a task into a code representation lets you inherit the models' code competence. That is partly true and the mechanism is important: code is verifiable. A code representation gives you a deterministic oracle (it runs or it does not, tests pass or they do not), and the entire reason models look strong on code is that code training had dense automatic verification available. So the real transfer is not the syntax, it is the verifier. This is the same conclusion the Lean 4 talk and the Cloudflare verification-chain talk reach from different directions, and it is the most consistent theme across this whole conference track: agentic capability is a function of oracle quality and latency. Where the bet gets weaker is that many services tasks have no faithful code representation, closing books with missing receipts is a judgment call under incomplete information, and forcing it into code either loses the judgment or hides it in a heuristic.

The autonomy ladder also has an under-discussed cost structure. Each rung up increases the token cost of a failure, because a long-running agent that goes wrong burns hours of compute before anyone notices. That means the economically correct rung is a function of verifier quality, not of model capability. If you cannot verify cheaply mid-run, you should not be on the long-running rung regardless of how good the model is. This is a useful decision rule and Shenoy's "earn the right" framing is the organisational version of it.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The company details check out and one figure in the talk is misstated.

Long Lake Management was co-founded in 2023 by Alex Taubman, formerly of Oaktree Capital Management, and Zach Frankl, a co-founder of Ramp. Backers are General Catalyst, Alpha Wave Global, Elad Gil, D1 Capital, and Thrive Capital, so Shenoy's list is accurate but incomplete. At roughly three years old the company has acquired more than 30 businesses across HOA management, construction, and now corporate travel, consistent with his figure of 35. Its prior portfolio included 18 HOA management firms, and it runs a proprietary AI platform called Nexus tuned to per-industry workflows that frontier labs are not initially targeting, which is the concrete product behind his customisation claims.

The Amex GBT deal is real and larger in significance than the talk conveys. The $6.3 billion take-private was agreed in May 2026 at a 65% premium, and Elad Gil has publicly characterised it as the world's first AI-driven take-private of a public company. The target is an order of magnitude beyond anything Long Lake had attempted: roughly 22,000 employees, $2.7 billion in revenue, and an 18% adjusted EBITDA margin, at a 100-year-old company. Long Lake is also part of a broader pattern, VC-backed AI-infused roll-ups of mature businesses, with Khosla Ventures and others experimenting in the same direction and the strategy now getting Wall Street attention as a new buyout playbook.

**One correction.** Shenoy says they have "raised over $3 billion from Elad Gil, General Catalyst, and AlphaWave since our founding." That figure almost certainly conflates equity raised with total capital deployed including acquisition debt. A $6.3 billion take-private of a business with $2.7 billion of revenue and 18% EBITDA margins is financed predominantly with debt against the target's cash flows, not with venture equity. Treat "$3 billion raised" as capital-under-management or total transaction capacity rather than as venture funding, because the distinction matters enormously for how much runway the technology bet actually has.

**The structural caveat the talk does not raise.** The owner-operator model genuinely aligns incentives, and Shenoy's "when the AI doesn't work it's our problem" is a real advantage over vendor deployments. But it also means the thesis is untested in the way a software business would be. Long Lake's returns can come from ordinary private-equity levers, multiple arbitrage, cost consolidation across 30-plus acquired firms, and debt paydown, entirely independent of whether the AI works. There is no published evidence in the talk or in the public record that the AI deployment is driving the economics. That is not a criticism of the engineering, which sounds serious, but a reader should not treat commercial success here as validation of the AI diffusion thesis. The most honest read is that Long Lake has bought itself the deployment access and the proprietary data that the thesis requires, and the results are still ahead.

The electricity analogy is also worth holding at the right resolution. The Pearl Street to Ford's 1924 electrified assembly line gap is roughly 40 years, and the deeper economic history point (Paul David's work on the dynamo) is that productivity gains arrived only after factories were physically reorganised around distributed unit drive rather than replacing a central steam engine with a central electric motor. That is precisely Shenoy's argument, and it is also the argument Clare Liguori makes about workflow restructuring in a talk at the same conference. The analogy supports his patience thesis, and it also implies his 20-year timeframe is optimistic rather than pessimistic.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Instrument for the diff, not the rating.** This is the single most transferable idea. Anywhere a human edits model output before using it, capture the before and after pair. That is free preference data with the corrected target attached, and it is strictly more informative than thumbs-up feedback. For Amit's own tooling (generated configs, generated eval scripts, generated analysis), logging the human's edit is a near-zero-cost decision now that becomes an irreplaceable dataset later.
- **Make every hill-climbing benchmark a permanent regression test.** Shenoy's ratchet is process discipline that costs nothing and prevents silent regression. For compression and quantization work specifically, this is the guard against the classic failure where a new technique wins on the current benchmark while quietly degrading something fixed three months ago.
- **Choose the autonomy rung from verifier quality, not model capability.** Concrete rule: if you cannot cheaply verify mid-run, do not run long. Amit should audit his agentic pipelines by asking what the mid-run oracle is and how much a wrong run costs in GPU-hours before it is caught.
- **Diagnose parallelism blockers as missing primitives.** For any serial workflow he wants to parallelise, ask three questions: can state be cheaply forked, is there a local verifier, and do the operations commute. Whichever answer is no is the thing to build. This turns "this work is inherently serial" into a concrete engineering backlog.
- **Take the out-of-distribution data point seriously as a moat argument.** Frontier models have not seen the internal operating data of ordinary businesses, which means domain-specific post-training on proprietary traces is genuinely defensible where general capability is not. For Amit this is the strongest available case for small specialised models over frontier API calls: not cost, but that the small model can be trained on data the frontier model has never seen.
- **Read the electricity history properly.** The productivity gain came from reorganising the factory, not from installing the motor. Every talk in this corpus that reports large gains attributes them to workflow restructuring, and every one that reports disappointment describes sprinkling tools on an unchanged process. That is the most consistent empirical finding across the entire conference.
