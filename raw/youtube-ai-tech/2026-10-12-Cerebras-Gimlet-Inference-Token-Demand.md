# How Inference Keeps Up with Our Insatiable Need for Tokens (Cerebras & Gimlet Labs)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-10-12
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_cRgl3maiT8

## TL;DR
Cerebras (Daniel) and Gimlet Labs (Michelle) walk the inference optimization stack from runtime tricks (continuous batching, PagedAttention, prefix caching, chunked prefill) to system-level disaggregation, then argue the next frontier shift comes from heterogeneous hardware: run each sub-task (prefill, decode, draft model, attention, FFN) on the silicon whose compute/bandwidth ratio matches it. Gimlet claims 3 to 10x faster inference than homogeneous clusters at equal power, plus an agentic kernel generator (KForge) that sped up a gpt-oss model 33% end to end on AMD on top of already-tuned kernels. The runtime half is a solid primer you already know. The useful signal is the AWS Trainium-prefill / Cerebras-decode split, speculative decoding with the draft on SRAM silicon, and attention-FFN disaggregation as a first-class routing target.

## Key Takeaways
- **Demand context:** Google reported 480T tokens/month at I/O 2025 and 3.2 quadrillion/month at I/O 2026 (~7x YoY). Llama 3.3 70B is now ~25% cheaper and 3.6x faster than at launch.
- **Decode is bandwidth bound:** up to ~90% of decode time the ALUs wait on weight streaming. Tensor parallelism cuts per-token latency but pays two all-reduces per layer, so energy per token rises superlinearly with GPU count. The speaker's "4x GPUs gives only 2x" is a pessimistic illustration; on NVLink, 4-way TP usually lands closer to 3x.
- **The speed vs throughput Pareto frontier** is the frame for every optimization: the goal is to shift the curve right, not trade along it. Claude fast mode is the cited example of sitting at the speed end (Opus 4.6 fast mode: ~2.5x faster at 6x price).
- **KV cache arithmetic (verified):** Llama-70B class with GQA (80 layers, 8 KV heads, d=128, FP16) is ~0.33 MB/token, so ~1 GB per 3K tokens and ~42 GB at 128K context. Long-context agents blow up batch size before they blow up compute.
- **Runtime layer:** continuous batching fills idle slots per weight read; PagedAttention gives ~3x more concurrent requests by eliminating reservation waste; prefix caching skips prefill for shared system prompts; chunked prefill stops a 500K-token agent prompt from stalling everyone else's decode.
- **System layer, three disaggregation axes:**
  1. *By phase* (prefill/decode): scale pools independently by workload shape (summarization is prefill heavy, generation is decode heavy).
  2. *By model* (speculative decoding): draft on fast small-model silicon, verify on the big model. Gimlet's chart claims ~10x interactivity at equal throughput vs PD-disagg on homogeneous GPUs when the draft runs on Cerebras or d-Matrix.
  3. *By layer type* (attention/FFN): attention is KV-read heavy (bandwidth), FFN is dense GEMM (compute). Ping-pong activations between machine types per layer. Relevant for 1T+ parameter MoE.
- **"No best disagg":** the right split depends on model, ISL/OSL mix, and latency/throughput SLOs. This is effectively a routing problem over hardware, not just over models.
- **Agents for the stack:** KForge generates kernels from hardware and workload profiles; agent-built compilers (they cite Anthropic's agent-team C compiler) suggest per-workload JIT inference stacks.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **Why Cerebras wins decode:** WSE-3 has 44 GB on-wafer SRAM with 21 PB/s aggregate bandwidth versus H100's 3.35 TB/s HBM3 (~6,300x, the talk rounds to 7,000x). Decode tok/s scales roughly with bandwidth / bytes-per-token, so SRAM-resident weights give the claimed ~3,000 tok/s vs ~600 on H100 (model unspecified, so treat as directional). The catch: 44 GB per wafer. A 70B FP16 model needs ~4 CS-3s split at layer boundaries, and KV cache capacity is tight, which is exactly why handing prefill (and the bulk KV build) to Trainium makes sense.
- **Trainium + CS-3 pipeline:** Trainium runs compute-bound prefill, KV cache moves over EFA to CS-3, CS-3 decodes. Claimed "5x high-speed token capacity in the same footprint." The hidden cost is KV transfer: at ~0.33 MB/token, a 100K-token agent prompt ships ~33 GB per request (less with FP8 KV or MLA-style compression). KV compression is therefore a direct multiplier on this architecture's viability.
- **Speculative decoding on heterogeneous silicon:** speedup = (accepted tokens per verify) x (verify cost) / (draft cost + verify cost). Putting the draft on SRAM hardware drives draft cost toward zero, so acceptance rate becomes the only lever. Distilled drafts (EAGLE-style heads, or a distilled small model) are where compression work pays twice.
- **Attention-FFN disaggregation (AFD):** formalized by ByteDance's MegaScale-Infer (up to 1.9x per-GPU throughput for MoE) via ping-pong micro-batch pipelining and M2N dispatch. Motivation: MoE sparsity makes expert FFNs memory-ish at small per-expert batch, so replicate attention with DP to aggregate tokens per expert. Later design-space work (2026) shows AFD gains depend heavily on ISL/OSL, prefix length, and per-user tok/s targets.
- **KForge:** multi-agent loop over hardware profile + workload shape, generating and verifying kernels across CUDA, ROCm, Metal. 33% end-to-end on gpt-oss over a tuned baseline in under a week of agent time. The talk said "GPT-OSS 12B," which is likely 120B or 20B misheard; no 12B variant exists.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The AWS and Cerebras deal is real: announced March 13, 2026, Trainium handles prefill and CS-3 handles decode over EFA, delivered via Amazon Bedrock with open models and Nova planned on Cerebras later in 2026. AWS pitched it as an order of magnitude faster than current offerings, which is a vendor number; financial terms were undisclosed. Analysts frame it as Cerebras's way into prefill-heavy coding-agent traffic. The 3.2 quadrillion tokens/month figure is confirmed from Sundar Pichai's I/O 2026 keynote (up from 480T and 9.7T the two years prior), though "tokens across surfaces" is undefined and includes AI Overviews. On fast mode, the 6x markup applied to Opus 4.6 and 4.7; Opus 4.8 fast mode dropped to ~2x standard price for the same ~2.5x speed, which itself shows the frontier moving right.

Gimlet's 3 to 10x claim is self-reported with no independent benchmark; the company has raised roughly $390M (including a $300M Series B) and supports NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Arm, Cerebras, and d-Matrix without building its own chip. KForge has a June 2026 arXiv paper (2606.02963) on cross-platform kernel generation, so that claim has a primary source. Be skeptical of the Cerebras bandwidth framing: the 21 PB/s is on-wafer aggregate, off-wafer I/O is far smaller, and capacity rather than bandwidth is the binding constraint for trillion-parameter MoE and long-context KV. Sources also disagree on whether production Cerebras inference keeps all weights resident in SRAM versus streaming from MemoryX.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Extend your router to route over hardware, not just models.** Add a cost model with features (ISL, expected OSL, prefix-hit probability, latency SLO) and target pools (prefill-optimized, decode-optimized/SRAM, spec-decode). The same query-difficulty features you use for model routing predict prefill/decode ratio.
- **Treat KV bytes/token as a first-class compression target.** Benchmark FP8/INT4 KV quantization and MLA-style latent KV for transfer cost in a PD-disagg setup (vLLM or SGLang disaggregated prefill with NIXL/Mooncake). Measure TTFT including KV transfer, not just decode tok/s.
- **Build a distilled draft model** for your main served model and measure acceptance rate per domain. If acceptance is above ~0.7 at k=4 to 5, a draft on cheap fast hardware is a bigger win than further pruning the target.
- **For MoE work:** prototype AFD with SGLang/MegaScale-style ping-pong on a Mixtral or DeepSeek-class model and sweep ISL/OSL to find where it beats plain EP. Expert pruning changes the FFN side's compute/memory ratio, so co-design pruning with the disaggregation split.
- **Try an agentic kernel loop** (KForge paper as reference) on one hot kernel in your quantized path, e.g. a GPTQ dequant-GEMM on non-NVIDIA hardware, gated by numerical-equivalence tests.
