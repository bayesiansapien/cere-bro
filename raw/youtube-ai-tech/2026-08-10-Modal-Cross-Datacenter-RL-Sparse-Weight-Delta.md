# Taking Reinforcement Learning Cross Datacenter

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-08-10
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=maRzp4kImJ4

## TL;DR
Nan Jiang (Modal) makes the sharpest infrastructure argument at this conference: the RL post-training loop is forced into a compute shape it does not actually need. Backpropagation genuinely wants one RDMA-connected cluster, but the rollout fleet does not, because there is no global all-reduce across rollout workers. The only thing binding rollout to the trainer cluster is weight sync, and the insight that breaks that binding is that a full checkpoint is the wrong unit of synchronization. Because Adam's per-parameter step is on the order of the learning rate while the BF16 rounding boundary is roughly |w|/256, roughly 99% of rollout-visible weights are bit-identical between consecutive optimizer steps. The updates are dense in FP32 master weights and simply too small to survive the cast. Ship a lossless bit-level patch instead of a checkpoint and the sync link drops from about 500 GB to about 500 MB, at which point rollout workers can live in any region on any provider. Modal's implementation is Stitch: an immutable versioned weight bulletin board plus a version-aware sidecar in front of each inference engine. The strategic consequence is the line to remember. Inference capacity becomes RL capacity.

## Key Takeaways
- The framing that carries the talk is cathedral versus bazaar. The cathedral is one region, one fast interconnect, many GPUs wired together, and it is the right shape for the trainer. The bazaar is where most usable compute actually lives: different providers, regions, prices, and availability windows. The default RL loop demands the cathedral, and the cathedral is exactly the scarce thing.
- RL wants four properties simultaneously: enough GPUs, same region, fast fabric, available now. Any one is manageable. All four together is the hard constraint. And unlike inference capacity, training capacity is not elastic, so you cannot grow the trainer cluster mid-run just because rollout wants more nodes.
- The asymmetry that licenses everything else: training is one tightly coupled job with collectives and model-parallel communication on every step, so it needs the fabric. Rollout is a fleet of independent serving jobs generating trajectories, calling environments and tools, and there is no global all-reduce across rollout jobs. Do not move backpropagation. Move the rollout fleet.
- The movable unit is precisely defined as the rollout serving island: a coherent endpoint or local group of endpoints serving one policy version. Local parallelism, prefill/decode disaggregation, and local serving constraints all stay inside the island. Across islands the only dependency is a policy version in, trajectories and metadata out.
- Once you define the unit that way, the architecture reduces to one remaining problem: the weight update link. If that link is a full checkpoint from disk or over the network, the whole thing collapses immediately. Kimi-scale NVFP4 checkpoints run around 500 GB and take minutes to hours to move, while async training wants weight update latency in seconds.
- The central bet, stated as a testable claim: less than 1% of rollout-visible weights change between versions. Rollout-visible means the weights the serving engine actually uses, in BF16 or FP8 or NVFP4, not the FP32 master weights and not the Adam moments.
- Mechanism, ingredient one, the floor. BF16 has 7 explicit mantissa bits, so ULP near magnitude theta is about theta/128, and the nearest rounding boundary is half that, about theta/256. For a weight near 1.0, ULP is 0.0078 and the visibility floor is 0.0039. An update smaller than that rounds back to the same bit pattern.
- Mechanism, ingredient two, the push. Adam's per-parameter update is the learning rate times a normalised direction, because the raw gradient is divided by running gradient statistics. The cited paper proves a bound of B times the learning rate. The exact constant does not matter. What matters is that Adam makes the step small and tightly controlled regardless of gradient magnitude.
- Put them together at post-training learning rates: a typical Adam step is around 3e-6 against a boundary of 3.9e-3, so the update is more than a thousand times smaller than the boundary. The served value does not change. This is update absorption. Critically, this does not mean the master weights are frozen. It means the value the rollout engine would serve is unchanged.
- The visualisation is the thing to internalise. Plot weight magnitude on x and update magnitude on y. The BF16 visibility boundary is a diagonal (theta/256), the Adam push is a roughly flat line at the learning rate. Small weights on the left sit above the boundary and can move. Large weights on the right sit below it and get absorbed. Sparsity is a consequence of that geometry, not of anything about gradients.
- The misconception he explicitly kills: this does not work because RL gradients are sparse. The paper reports gradients are dense, with about 99% of parameters receiving non-zero gradients, and the FP32 master update is dense too. It is just small. Sparsity exists only in the rollout view.
- The patch is bitwise lossless, not an approximation. Cast to the serving dtype, diff version t-1 against t, ship changed positions plus replacement bits plus metadata. Encoding can be selective override or XOR. Because it is bit replacement rather than floating point addition, there is no additive delta drift, and the engine reconstructs exactly the version it would have gotten from a full sync.
- Reported measurements: roughly 99% bit-identical per step across model families, and the change set stays small even under staleness when rollout lags the trainer. One internal Modal run serving GLM 4.7 Air in FP8 showed 0.15% of weights changing on the first step where the learning rate is high, settling to about 0.05% per step once Adam stabilises.
- Lower serving precision makes this strictly better, which is the counterintuitive and important part. The visibility floor scales as theta/2^(mantissa+1), so FP8 has a coarser floor than BF16 and MXFP4/NVFP4 coarser still. Fewer weights clear it, so the patch shrinks. Group-scaled formats like INT4 and hierarchically scaled NVFP4 need separate reasoning because a shared group scale couples elements, but the same rationale applies.
- LoRA is small for a completely different reason and should not be conflated. Full-parameter deltas are small by absorption. LoRA updates are small by construction because the base is frozen. No push-versus-floor argument is needed.
- System shape: trainer publishes immutable rollout weight versions to a shared bulletin board after each optimizer step, in HuggingFace safetensors checkpoint layout so any compatible engine (SGLang, vLLM) can load and shard it however it likes without changing the served version. Rollout engines live outside the training cluster, no RDMA to the trainer required.
- The request lane carries versions explicitly. A request states which version it targets and which versions it will accept. The response returns the version alongside tokens, log probs, and router replay information, so the trainer gets exactly the same data it would have gotten in-cluster. That is what makes off-policy correction sound rather than best-effort.
- The sidecar is the elegant piece. It makes an unmodified rollout engine version-aware with three cases: if the engine is at an acceptable version, proxy the request. If it is behind but can catch up, apply the missing transitions. If it cannot, return not ready. That is what turns any idle GPU anywhere into usable rollout capacity.
- Stitch is Modal's concrete implementation, deliberately agnostic to trainer framework, inference engine, and transport, and described as async-first and agentic-first.
- Open questions he flags honestly. Does the sparsity hold for Muon, since the entire push argument is Adam-specific and Moonshot and DeepSeek are moving post-training to Muon. How far does fully async RL actually scale once compute is globally available. And does the paradigm generalise back to pre-training, mid-training, and SFT.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
This talk is the cleanest example I have seen of a numerics fact becoming a distributed systems primitive, and the reason it is worth studying closely is that the reasoning pattern transfers directly to compression work. The move is to ask which representation actually reaches the consumer, then measure change only in that representation. The optimizer operates in FP32 and its updates are dense there. The consumer is a BF16 or FP8 forward pass with a quantisation grid roughly a thousand times coarser than the per-step update. Everything between those two facts is where the 100x bandwidth reduction lives. State it as compute-visible sparsity: communicate an update only when it can change the next forward pass.

The precision inversion deserves emphasis because it runs opposite to normal intuition and it is directly actionable for anyone serving quantized models. Aggressive serving quantisation is usually framed as a pure accuracy-for-throughput trade. Here it also buys weight-sync bandwidth, and superlinearly. Going BF16 (7 mantissa bits) to FP8 E4M3 (3 mantissa bits) coarsens the visibility floor by 16x, and MXFP4 (E2M1, 1 mantissa bit) coarsens it by another 4x. Each factor removes weights from the change set. So an NVFP4 serving path in a cross-region RL loop is not merely cheaper to run, it is cheaper to keep in sync, and the two effects compound. That is a real argument for low-precision rollout that nobody makes.

The flip side is the failure mode worth thinking about, and the talk does not address it. Absorption means the served policy is quantisation-frozen for the large-magnitude weights while the master weights drift underneath. Over many steps those sub-threshold updates accumulate in FP32 and eventually cross the boundary in a burst, so the served policy moves in discrete jumps rather than smoothly. For RL specifically, the served policy is the behaviour policy generating trajectories. If the behaviour policy updates in staircase fashion while the target policy moves continuously, the importance ratio between them has structure that a standard GRPO or PPO ratio clip is not modelling. The reported per-step change rates (0.15% falling to 0.05%) are stable enough that this is probably second-order, but it is the thing I would measure before trusting the setup at scale, because it is exactly the class of bug that shows up as slightly worse final reward and gets blamed on hyperparameters.

Group-scaled formats are the genuinely unresolved technical risk and he moves past them quickly. In INT4 or NVFP4 with a shared group scale, per-element independence is gone. One element crossing a threshold can shift the group's scale factor, which re-encodes every element in that group. So the patch granularity is the group, not the element, and the sparsity story degrades toward the fraction of groups touched rather than the fraction of elements. With a group size of 16 or 32, a 1% element change rate that is spatially uncorrelated could touch a much larger fraction of groups. Whether that is fine depends entirely on whether the changed elements cluster, which is an empirical question about gradient structure that nobody in this literature seems to have answered yet.

The Muon question he raises is the one that decides whether this whole architecture survives the next year, and the answer is not obvious. The push bound comes from Adam dividing by running second-moment statistics, which is what makes the per-parameter step scale with the learning rate rather than the gradient. Muon orthogonalises the update via Newton-Schulz on the gradient matrix, and Moonshot's scalable version explicitly enforces a consistent update RMS across parameter groups. That RMS consistency is arguably a stronger and cleaner guarantee than Adam's, which suggests absorption should hold and possibly hold more uniformly. But Muon's updates are spectrally spread by construction, meaning the update is dense across a matrix's directions with controlled norm, so the distribution of per-element step sizes at fixed RMS could have heavier tails than Adam's. The prediction worth testing is that Muon preserves the aggregate sparsity rate but changes which weights move, concentrating visible changes in low-magnitude weights where the diagonal floor is lowest.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The core claim is published and holds up, with the caveat worth knowing. The formalisation is the PULSE paper (Mihai and Belilovsky, 2026), arXiv 2602.03839, "Understanding and Exploiting Weight Update Sparsity for Communication-Efficient Distributed RL". Its measured result is roughly 99% of BF16 weights bit-identical between consecutive RL optimizer steps, and never less than 98% in the worst case. That worst-case floor is the number Jiang omits and the one to plan capacity against, since 2% of a 500 GB checkpoint is 10 GB rather than 500 MB. The paper's phrasing of the mechanism is better than the talk's: the optimizer is whispering and BF16 cannot hear it. It gives the same threshold, |w|/256, and the same conclusion that the sparsity is free with zero approximation.

The surrounding systems literature has moved fast and independently confirms both the phenomenon and the architecture. SparseRL-Sync (arXiv 2605.07330) reports lossless weight synchronisation with roughly 100x less communication using sparse BF16 patches. TensorHub (arXiv 2604.09107) attacks the same cross-datacenter problem with reference-oriented storage and reports up to 6.7x reduction in GPU stall time, 4.8x faster elastic rollouts, and a 19x improvement specifically in cross-datacenter scenarios, which is direct quantitative support for Jiang's thesis from a different design. HuggingFace has shipped delta weight sync in TRL with a hub bucket as the transport for trillion-parameter models, so this is in mainstream tooling rather than being a Modal-only capability. Relax (redai-infra) implements the elastic side with grow and shrink of inference engines mid-training over a REST API and explicit same-cluster and cross-cluster federation modes. The convergence of four independent implementations on the same primitive within six months is the strongest signal that this is the correct decomposition.

On Muon, the state of play supports his concern being live rather than settled. Muon originated with Keller Jordan et al. in 2024 and was scaled by Moonshot with weight decay plus consistent update RMS across matrix and non-matrix parameters, reportedly about 2x the compute efficiency of AdamW, and Moonshot's own guidance is that the benefit is largest when used for both pre-training and post-training with smaller gains from post-training only. Muon already has bf16 compression in its communication path and NVIDIA has it in NeMo-RL, so it is production-adjacent. No published work yet measures rollout-visible update sparsity under Muon, so this is a genuine open question and a cheap, high-value experiment for anyone with a Muon post-training run already going.

Modal's own positioning is worth reading with appropriate scepticism, since Stitch is a commercial product and the talk is a product argument. Their public material frames elastic interruption-aware infrastructure as the direct lever on RL economics, which is true and also exactly what they sell. The protocol design he describes is genuinely general though, and the parts that matter (immutable versioned weight artifacts in safetensors layout, an explicit version contract on the request path, a version-aware sidecar in front of an unmodified engine) are all reimplementable without Modal.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. Run the absorption measurement on your own training runs this week. It is roughly twenty lines: cast weights to your serving dtype at consecutive steps, XOR the byte buffers, count non-zero. You get your own sparsity curve, and if you are fine-tuning anything you now know your true weight-sync cost. Do it per layer, because the per-layer breakdown is the interesting artifact and nobody has published it in a form that maps onto pruning sensitivity.
2. Correlate that per-layer absorption rate with your existing pruning and quantisation sensitivity rankings. This is the highest-value idea in the talk for your work. Absorption rate is a measurement of how much of a layer's gradient signal survives the serving precision cast, which is a functional sensitivity signal derived from training dynamics rather than from weight magnitude or Hessian approximation. A layer whose updates are almost entirely absorbed is a layer whose training signal your serving precision is already discarding, which is a strong prior that it tolerates more aggressive quantisation. Cheap to compute, and it is a genuinely novel sensitivity metric.
3. Add the sync-bandwidth term to your quantisation cost model. When you next argue for FP8 or NVFP4 serving, the case is not only memory and throughput. If the model is ever updated in place, the coarser grid superlinearly reduces the number of weights whose changes are visible, which cuts weight-transfer bytes. Quantify it with the theta/2^(mantissa+1) floor and your measured weight magnitude distribution.
4. Reuse the versioned-artifact plus sidecar pattern for your own model serving, independent of RL. Immutable versions on a bulletin board, requests that declare acceptable versions, and a proxy that either serves, catches up, or returns not-ready. That is the correct shape for any staged rollout of a newly compressed checkpoint, and it gives you honest A/B attribution because every response carries the version that produced it.
5. If you touch an RL post-training loop, instrument the staircase. Log the per-step fraction of served weights that change and the resulting behaviour-policy-to-target-policy divergence separately. Absorption means those two quantities decouple, and any importance-ratio correction that assumes the served policy equals the current policy is silently wrong. This is the failure mode most likely to cost you reward and least likely to be diagnosed correctly.
6. Take the general lesson into routing work, because it is the same trick. Measure change and error where the consumer observes it, not where the computation happens. For LLM routing, the consumer-visible quantity is the final decision, so a router update that does not change any routing decision on your traffic distribution costs nothing to deploy and needs no propagation. Define your router's version boundary by decision-flip rate rather than parameter delta, and most updates become free.
7. Test Muon absorption if you have a Muon run available. Nobody has published it, the experiment is a day of work, and the answer determines whether the entire cross-datacenter RL architecture survives the industry's optimizer migration. Concrete hypothesis to test: aggregate sparsity holds under Muon's RMS consistency, but visible changes concentrate in low-magnitude weights relative to Adam.
