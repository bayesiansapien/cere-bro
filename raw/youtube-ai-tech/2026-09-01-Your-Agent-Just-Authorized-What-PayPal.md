# Your Agent Just Authorized What?! (Jay Mok and Ben Coumes, PayPal)

**Channel:** AI Engineer
**Published:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vGn6N4-bxBY

## TL;DR
The most useful conceptual framework in the agentic-payments track, and it is three questions plus a two-by-two. The questions: did the human authorize this, is this allowed right now in this scope, and can we prove it later. The matrix axes are stakes and counterparty (open or closed ecosystem), and the point is that the correct answer to all three questions changes with position on that matrix rather than being uniform. Low stakes and closed, such as Claude Code with OAuth connectors and tool permissions, needs no cryptographic proof because system logs and revertibility suffice. Medium stakes and closed, a vaulted commercial card shared with a travel buyer agent under OAuth scopes, borrows trust from a shared intermediary and relies on transaction logs. High stakes and open, where neither party knows the other, is the case nobody has in production, and their proposal is FIDO verifiable intents plus AP2 mandates: a multi-layer selective-disclosure JWT where each party verifies only the layer that concerns them and no one needs a prior relationship. Their closing generalisation is the best line: this model applies to any hard-to-reverse agent action, not just payments.

## Key Takeaways
- **The three questions are the reusable artifact.** Human authorization (a passkey or equivalent), current scope validity (a time-bounded token with an amount and often a merchant or product intent), and later provability (dispute evidence). Every authorization design answers all three; the interesting variable is how strongly each must be answered.
- **Stakes and counterparty are the right axes, and the reasoning is sound.** Closed ecosystems can borrow trust from an intermediary that both parties already rely on, which is why they need weaker evidence. Open ecosystems have no such intermediary, so evidence must be cryptographic and self-verifying.
- **The badge analogy is genuinely clarifying.** Badge into the building once and you do not re-badge for every room, and when you meet a colleague inside you extend some trust because you both badged in. High stakes and open is meeting a stranger on the street, where a badge they show you is not sufficient, so you need something independently verifiable.
- **Low stakes worked example.** Claude Code plus GitHub, Jira, or Linear connectors: OAuth supplies the human authorization, Claude's allow/deny/ask tool permissions supply the scope, and coding is low stakes because you can inspect system logs and revert changes. No cryptographic proof needed. Reasonable, and worth noting that "revertible" is doing the load-bearing work in that assessment.
- **Medium stakes worked example.** A travel content provider wants to monetise occupancy data and reviews to buyer agents. PayPal and Braintree supply two primitives: a vault holding payment credentials on behalf of buyer agents, and OAuth-scoped access to those credentials granted to merchants. The vault alone does nothing; combining it with OAuth creates a closed ecosystem of buyer and seller agents with a shared trusted intermediary. Human authorizes a commercial card, scopes attach as a mandate, and disputes resolve against existing transaction logs.
- **High stakes and open is unsolved in practice, and they say so.** "We haven't actually really seen [it] in production yet." The user gives an agent autonomous instructions, and you do not know who it will transact with, so counterparties need verifiable proof the agent has permission.
- **The three-layer mandate structure.** Layer one issued by a trustworthy credential provider. Layer two encapsulates the user's instructions to the agent, signed with the user's private key. Layer three, present only for autonomous payments, signed by the agent. Selective disclosure means merchants verify the checkout layer, processors verify the payment mandate layer, and nobody needs a relationship with anybody.
- **PayPal's approval token is the concrete near-term shipment.** Historically PayPal orders were synchronous: find item, open app, approve, done. The new flow redirects the user to PayPal to confirm the instructions given to the agent, and returns a JSON payload with amount, expiry, and intended merchant. Currently an opaque string only PayPal can approve, shipping to production, and used by anyone selecting PayPal as a payment method in agent flows.
- **The generalisation beyond payments is the most valuable part.** Medical orders, e-signatures, securities trading, and any hard-to-reverse agent action need the same structure. That is a much broader claim than the talk's title suggests and it is the right one.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
- **Selective disclosure is the load-bearing design choice.** In a closed ecosystem, trust composes transitively through a shared intermediary, so a bare token suffices. In an open ecosystem there is no transitive path, so the credential itself must be verifiable by parties with no shared relationship, and it must not force each party to see everything. A layered JWT where a merchant validates the cart layer without seeing payment details, and a processor validates the payment layer without seeing cart contents, is exactly the right shape. This is minimum-necessary-disclosure applied to authorization rather than to data.
- **Time and amount bounding is the same capability-scoping pattern as everything else in this batch.** A mandate bounded by expiry, amount, and merchant is functionally identical to Stripe's shared payment token, Circle's wallet policy, and Cloudflare's zero-access-by-default grants. Four vendors independently converged on "bound the capability, enforce it outside the agent," which is strong evidence it is the correct primitive.
- **Revertibility is the real determinant of required evidence, and it deserves to be named explicitly.** Their low-stakes justification is not that coding is unimportant, it is that a bad commit can be reverted. That suggests the honest axis is not "stakes" but "cost of reversal," which is why their generalisation to medical orders and securities trades works: those are hard to reverse, so evidence must be strong regardless of amount. For anyone designing agent permissions, "how expensive is undo" is a better question than "how much money is involved."
- **Proving-it-later is an observability requirement disguised as a compliance one.** Closed systems can rely on logs because a single party holds them and both sides accept that party's records. Open systems cannot, so the proof must travel with the transaction. That is a meaningful architectural constraint: the audit trail becomes part of the payload rather than a side effect.
- **The matrix's practical value is that it prevents overbuilding.** Most internal agent work sits in the low-stakes closed quadrant, where OAuth plus tool permissions plus logs is genuinely sufficient. Reaching for cryptographic mandates there is wasted effort. The framework tells you when to escalate, which is more useful than a maximalist recommendation.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The standards they point at are real, further along than the talk conveys, and already under documented attack.

AP2 was announced by Google on September 16, 2025 with over 60 launch partners including Mastercard, PayPal, Coinbase, American Express, and Salesforce. Its structure matches Coumes's description: three signed mandates (Intent, Cart, Payment), each a W3C Verifiable Credential signed by the user's wallet or the agent's key and passed between parties as a verifiable JSON object, structurally resembling the JWTs used in OAuth 2.0 flows. Google has since donated AP2 to the FIDO Alliance to keep it platform-agnostic and community-led, and Verifiable Intent is an AP2-compatible standard co-developed with Mastercard and also donated to FIDO, creating a tamper-proof log of user-authorized agent actions. So "the industry should converge on FIDO verifiable intents and AP2 mandates" is not a proposal, it is a description of a transfer that has already happened, and PayPal is a launch partner rather than a neutral observer.

**The part the talk omits, and it matters.** There is already published security work attacking exactly this design. "Whispers of Wealth: Red-Teaming Google's Agent Payments Protocol via Prompt Injection" demonstrates that a cryptographically sound mandate does not help if the agent can be induced to request a mandate for the wrong thing: the user signs an intent, an injected instruction shapes what the agent then asks to be authorized, and every signature validates correctly on a transaction the user did not want. Separately, "Zero-Trust Runtime Verification for Agentic Payment Protocols" addresses replay and context-binding failures in AP2, meaning mandates can be reused or detached from the context that justified them. Both are the same class of problem as the [lethal trifecta gap](./2026-09-01-Beyond-The-Lethal-Trifecta-DUNA-David-Levine-Kiduna.md): the authorization layer proves *who* approved *what amount to whom*, and proves nothing about whether the user actually wanted it. Mok and Coumes's three questions are the right three questions and they are incomplete, because there is a fourth: did the instruction that produced this mandate originate with the human or with the content the agent read.

To be fair to them, that fourth question is unsolved industry-wide, and their framework is honest about the high-stakes open case being unproven in production. The bounded-amount mandate does cap the damage from a successful injection, which is real mitigation even though it is not prevention. That is the same conclusion [Anna Spysz's persona demo](./2026-09-01-Teaching-Agents-To-Pay-Anna-Spysz-Stripe.md) reaches from the consumer-protection side: the payment rails are being hardened well, and the layer that decides what to buy has no enforcement at all.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
- **Adopt the three questions verbatim as a design checklist for any agent that takes consequential action.** Did the human authorize this, is it in scope right now, can we prove it later. For Amit's work the mapping is direct: an agent that can deploy a model, change routing weights, or modify a production config should have an explicit human authorization artifact, a time-and-scope-bounded grant, and a durable record sufficient to reconstruct the decision afterwards.
- **Replace "stakes" with "cost of reversal" when classifying agent permissions.** This is the sharper version of their own framework. A config change that can be rolled back in thirty seconds needs logs. A quantized model pushed to production serving that silently degrades a query class for a week needs the full mandate treatment, even though no money moved. Sort your agent's tools by undo cost, not by apparent importance.
- **Do not overbuild for the low-stakes closed quadrant.** Most internal agent work genuinely only needs OAuth, scoped tool permissions, and logs. The framework's value is telling you where that stops being enough.
- **Bound every consequential grant by time, amount, and target.** Expiry plus ceiling plus intended counterparty, enforced outside the agent. Fifth independent arrival at this pattern in this batch, which is as close to consensus as the field currently offers.
- **Read the red-team papers before trusting a mandate-based design.** The prompt-injection and replay work on AP2 is short, specific, and identifies the exact gap the vendor talks leave open. A validated signature on a transaction the user never wanted is the failure mode to design against, and the mitigation is a tight amount ceiling plus binding the mandate to the context that produced it.
- **Apply the generalisation.** Their point that this covers medical orders, e-signatures, and securities trading is correct and underexplored. Any team building agents that touch irreversible actions in any domain should be reading the payments literature, because payments is where the authorization thinking is furthest along.
