# State of Data — Sean Cai, Independent / State of Data

**Channel:** AI Engineer  
**Published:** 2026-07-26  
**Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZyIoTOAbRfs  

## TL;DR
Cai gives an unusually unguarded tour of the training data market from a position with no vendor to defend. His central framework is a three-axis decomposition of verifiability, extending Jason Wei's verifier's law: asymmetry (can the task be decomposed into checkable steps), veracity (is there consensus on what correct means), and proliferation (does the world hand you fresh verified examples). Coding matured first because GitHub scored high on all three at once, giving unit tests, community consensus on working code, and effectively infinite commit messages as free reasoning traces. Everything the money is now chasing (biology, security, finance, healthcare, law, taste) scores low on veracity and has its verified examples locked inside enterprise workflows that no Web 2.0 system ever captured, which is why vendors are showing up to buy your Slack and Jira logs. He separates data into state-based (the ERP row, the saved file) versus process-based (the trajectory, the reasoning trace, the sequence of decisions), and orthogonally into type one (real captured workflows) versus type two (experts manufacturing examples in an arbitrary setting). His accusation is that everybody sells type two and bills it as type one, and that the dominant recipe (hire experts, generate plausible tasks, cherry-pick where models diverge, package as a hard benchmark, then sell the data to hill-climb that same benchmark) is Goodhart's law with a profit motive. He closes with the strategic claim that successful data companies are all quietly pivoting to enterprise and becoming neo-labs, because the durable value accrues to the application layer, and that a builder's moat is never the data itself but the pipeline into real-world work plus the infrastructure to keep retraining as models improve underneath.

## Key Takeaways
- **The three verifiability axes are the most portable idea in the talk and they are predictive rather than descriptive.** Score any profession's tasks on decomposability, consensus on correctness, and frequency of naturally-occurring verified examples, and you get the maturation order. Cai's own sequence, code then search then finance then healthcare and law then cyber, biology, and scientific discovery, with taste last, falls out of the framework rather than being asserted.
- **GitHub is the reason coding was first, and there is no GitHub for anything else.** Unit tests give objective decomposable correctness, the community agrees what working code means, and public commits supply near-infinite free reasoning traces. Every subsequent domain has to manufacture what GitHub provided incidentally, and that manufacturing cost is the actual bottleneck in the data market.
- **The state-versus-process distinction explains why the available supply is worse than the demand.** State-based data is the final artefact, the row in the ERP, and it is mostly personal data wrapped in privacy law. Process-based data is how a professional got from blank page to finished output. Labs want the second and enterprises have only ever been instrumented to store the first.
- **The type one versus type two framing plus the claim that everyone mislabels is the highest-value accusation in the talk.** Type two (experts manufacturing examples in a contrived setting) is the right way to start models that are reading at first-grade level. Type one (real captured workflows, minimally shaped) is what takes you from 20 to 80 percent, because realism is inherited from the work rather than imagined. His structural point is that type one's only durable source is a live business you partner with, not a dead startup's codebase, which is what a lot of vendors are actually buying.
- **His benchmark critique is sharper than the usual contamination complaint and it targets the incentive rather than the artefact.** The perverse loop is selling the benchmark and then selling the data to climb it. When the measure becomes a target and the target was set by people who are not true domain experts, it stops measuring anything. He calls the resulting market a fog of war in which labs, vendors, and enterprises are all guessing which data actually improves a model, and nobody can see clearly.
- **"A single benchmark number under a single scaffold is one sample from a distribution nobody measured" is the line to keep.** He attributes most cross-benchmark performance divergence to cross-harness and cross-infrastructure differences rather than model capability, and points at high false positive and negative rates that are invisible in the headline number. His phrase for the resulting behaviour is benchmark psychosis.
- **The finance rubric results are the concrete payoff and they are genuinely useful.** Running an ARR waterfall reconciliation, an LBO valuation memo, and a long-short pair trade with deterministic verifiers paired with correctly applied LLM judges, he finds Opus 4.8 worse than 4.7 on several rubrics due to over-engineered self-reflection, and finds GPT 5.5 and Opus 4.8 scoring within three points while failing in exactly opposite directions: GPT nails the arithmetic and misses methodology, Opus nails methodology and loses arithmetic. Two models with the same score are not interchangeable, and the aggregate hides the entire decision.
- **Data spend is a two-to-three month leading indicator of lab product launches.** Anthropic buying cybersecurity data in January and biological data in March and April preceded its cyber and life sciences products. If you want to know what the labs will ship, watch what data they are buying, not what they are saying.
- **The counterexample he offers to keep himself honest is robotics, and the lesson generalises.** The modality is unsettled (ego versus teleoperation versus UMI), so a vendor choice is entangled with an unsolved research question. His warning, do not die on a modality hill, plus his observation that environment companies that succeed are really research accelerators and therefore a boutique industry, is the most commercially honest thing in the talk.
- **The strategic thesis is that model-layer lock-in is weaker than it looks, and the evidence he cites is real.** No pioneer of an infrastructure technology has held more than 10% of the market long run, railroads were nationalised once the automobile arrived, AWS and Google consolidated infrastructure and never captured the application layer, and open weights beating closed frontier models on real-world rubrics is proof that application-layer companies can decouple. His caveat is the right one: models differ on efficiency and modality, so they are not fungible like electricity, which means neither durable lock-in nor commodity substitution is the correct model.

## Architecture & Optimization Mechanics
There are three mechanically useful things here for compression and routing work, and one of them is a job description for the exact role you occupy.

Start with that one, because Cai says it out loud. His list of the abstraction layers that do not yet exist, needed once enterprises stop renting intelligence from labs and start owning their own, opens with "serve and route small models targeted by cost, latency, and performance profiles." That is the routing problem stated as an enterprise infrastructure gap rather than as a lab optimisation. The second item on his list is the more interesting one and is rarely discussed: managing RL datasets across base model migration, so that when you swap to a new open-source base you rerun post-training automatically instead of starting over. That is a real and underbuilt piece of infrastructure. Every organisation that fine-tunes an open base model faces a migration cliff every time a better base ships, and the asset that should survive the migration is the dataset plus the training recipe, not the weights. Treating post-training as a reproducible pipeline keyed to a swappable base is the difference between benefiting from open-weight progress and being repeatedly reset by it. If you are building internal capability around compressed or fine-tuned models, this is the architecture decision that determines whether your work compounds.

The second mechanic is the opposite-failure-directions finding, which is the strongest empirical argument for routing I have seen stated compactly. Two models within three points on the same finance task, one nailing arithmetic and failing methodology, the other the reverse, is not a tie. It is a specialisation signal that an aggregate score deletes. The routing implication is immediate: if failure modes are anti-correlated across models at similar aggregate capability, then per-task-type routing captures real quality gains at no cost increase, purely by matching task requirements to each model's failure profile. Even better, anti-correlated failures are the ideal condition for cheap ensembling, because a second opinion from a model that fails differently carries far more information than one from a model that fails the same way. The practical prerequisite is that you must have rubric-level rather than pass-rate-level evaluation, because pass rates are exactly what hides this. Cai's whole methodological point is that the decomposed rubric is where the signal lives, and that is a claim about your eval infrastructure, not about the models.

The third mechanic is his cross-harness attribution, which should change how you interpret every number you did not generate yourself. If most divergence between benchmarks is explained by harness and infrastructure differences rather than model capability, then the model is not the unit of measurement, the model-plus-scaffold is. For compression work this cuts both ways and it matters. A quantised model evaluated under a harness with retries, self-consistency, or a strong system prompt will show almost no degradation, because the scaffold is absorbing it. The same model in a bare harness will show the real damage. Neither number is wrong, but only one answers the question you are asking, and which one you want depends on whether the production system has that scaffold. The discipline that follows is to hold the harness fixed and version it alongside the model when reporting any compression result, and to state explicitly whether the number is scaffold-assisted. This is also the reason the DeepSWE team's choice to use an agent-agnostic harness for base-model comparability was methodologically correct and operationally incomplete, as noted in the [DeepSWE page](2026-07-26-DeepSWE-Contamination-Resistant-Coding-Benchmark-Datacurve.md).

The fourth thing worth extracting is the verifiability framework applied inward, to your own work rather than to markets. Compression and routing are unusually blessed on all three axes and it is worth appreciating why, because it tells you where to spend effort. Asymmetry is excellent: checking whether a quantised model preserved task performance is far cheaper than figuring out which quantisation scheme to use. Veracity is good but not perfect, because "acceptable degradation" is genuinely contested and workload-specific, which is precisely the judgment that cannot be outsourced or automated and is therefore where your durable value sits. Proliferation is the weak axis: production traffic hands you fresh examples continuously, but only if you instrument to capture the trajectory rather than just the outcome. That is Cai's state-versus-process distinction applied to your own logs, and it is the cheapest structural improvement available. Logging the request, the routing decision, the alternatives available, and the observed quality signal turns your own production into a proliferating source of type one data about your own system. Logging only the final response gives you state-based data, which is nearly worthless for improving the routing policy.

Finally, his "stop outsourcing your definition of realism to the same vendors you buy your evals from" is the same structural insight as clinician-owned evals in the [SonderMind talk](2026-07-25-Evals-Driven-Guardrails-Mental-Health-SonderMind.md), arrived at from the opposite direction. Letting the test writer grade the test is the failure. The fix in both cases is that the definition of correct must be owned by someone with no stake in the score.

## Grounded Context (Web Enrichment)
The framework's foundation is real and correctly attributed. Jason Wei's [asymmetry of verification and verifier's law](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) states that the ease of training AI to solve a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is, with the stronger corollary that any task possible to solve and easy to verify will be solved by AI. Wei's own examples are sudoku and crosswords, hard to solve and trivial to check, and he also notes the reverse case that Cai does not mention: some tasks take longer to verify than to produce, such as fact-checking an essay. That reverse asymmetry is the deep problem in the domains Cai says the money is chasing, and it is a stronger form of his veracity objection.

The product-launch prediction checks out, with the names slightly garbled in transcription. Anthropic's cybersecurity model is [Claude Mythos, initially released April 2026, built to find software vulnerabilities and used by a consortium of companies to secure software systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Mythos), with Anthropic publishing its own [assessment of Mythos Preview's cybersecurity capabilities](https://red.anthropic.com/2026/mythos-preview/). On the life sciences side, [Claude for Life Sciences launched in October 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-life-sciences) and [Claude Science, an AI workbench for scientists, launched 30 June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-science-ai-workbench), described by MIT Technology Review as [Anthropic's newest flagship product](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/06/30/1139987/claude-science-is-anthropics-newest-flagship-product/) and covering reproducible genomics, proteomics, and cheminformatics pipelines. So January cyber data spend to an April cyber model, and March to April biological data spend to a late-June science product, both land within his two-to-three month window. That is a genuinely useful leading indicator and the strongest verified claim in the talk.

The open-weights argument is understated rather than overstated. GLM-5.2, which Cai references, is a [753-billion parameter open-weights model released under an unrestricted MIT licence](https://venturebeat.com/technology/z-ais-open-weights-glm-5-2-beats-gpt-5-5-on-multiple-long-horizon-coding-benchmarks-for-1-6th-the-cost), scoring 62.1 on SWE-bench Pro against GPT-5.5's 58.6 at roughly one-sixth the cost, 74.4% on FrontierSWE against GPT-5.5's 72.6% and near-parity with Claude Opus 4.8 at 75.1%, and beating GPT-5.5 substantially on the longest-horizon tests including 34.3% versus 25.0% on PostTrainBench. Former Meta and Google DeepMind VP Matt Velloso called it the first open model reliable enough to use as a daily coding driver. For a routing practitioner this is the single most consequential fact in the talk: a frontier-competitive open-weights model at one-sixth the cost changes the economics of every routing tier below the frontier, and MIT licensing means it can be quantised, pruned, and served however you like.

His market-structure claim is where I would push back, because the data points the other way. Cai argues that fragmentation is permanent rather than transitional and that specialists outcompete the vertically integrated giants at sourcing, environment building, reward design, and evals. But [over 75% of AI training-data and RL-environment revenue flows to just four vendors: Scale, Surge, Mercor, and Handshake](https://blog.pebblous.ai/blog/ai-data-supply-oligopoly/en/). That is an oligopoly, not a fragmented market. Both things can be true if labs are diversifying across 20 to 30 vendors for the marginal specialist work while the overwhelming majority of spend consolidates into four, but that is a very different picture from the one he paints, and it means the specialists are competing for a minority slice. Anyone taking his fragmentation thesis as an investment or career signal should weigh the concentration number against it.

His enterprise-pivot claim, by contrast, is well supported. Mercor booked [$614 million of revenue in H1 2026 with 90% from AI model firms](https://finance.biggo.com/news/865c23cb-3e0d-4c21-aa0d-6bf2a7fc8d1a), having gone from roughly $500 million annualised in September 2025 past $1 billion in February 2026 and past $2 billion by June, and it is explicitly diversifying away from big-client dependency by listing financial institutions including Ramp, Blue Owl, and Citigroup as clients. It also [acquired software simulation company Deeptune in July 2026 to build realistic sandbox environments, targeting tens of millions in annualised RL-environment revenue by September](https://finance.biggo.com/news/865c23cb-3e0d-4c21-aa0d-6bf2a7fc8d1a). That is precisely the neo-lab trajectory Cai describes: data company acquires simulation capability, sells environments, courts enterprises directly. The 90%-from-labs figure does qualify his claim that an incredibly large share of revenue is already enterprise, at least for Mercor. The pivot is underway rather than complete.

Two caveats on the source itself. Cai announces at the end that he is building a company to help enterprises monetise data assets and to offer RL-as-a-service, which means the talk's central conclusion, that the moat is the pipeline into real-world work rather than the data, is also his pitch. It happens to be well argued and consistent with the evidence, but it is not disinterested. Second, several of the specific claims are unverifiable from outside: the 20-to-30-vendor mandates, the private finance rubric results, and the characterisation of what unnamed vendors sell. The finance findings in particular are the most decision-relevant part of the talk and rest entirely on his own unpublished harness.

## Real-World Application / Actionable Step
1. **Score your own work on the three verifiability axes and act on the weak one, which is proliferation.** Asymmetry and veracity are already strong in compression and routing. Proliferation is weak by default because production systems log outcomes rather than trajectories. The concrete change is to log the process: request, routing decision, alternatives available with prices, latency, and a downstream quality signal. That converts your production traffic into a continuously proliferating source of type one data about your own system, which is the only data asset in your domain that no vendor can sell you.
2. **Build rubric-level evaluation for at least one workload, because pass rates are hiding your best routing opportunity.** The opposite-failure-directions result is only visible if you decompose scoring into components (arithmetic, methodology, format, completeness). Pick one high-value workload, define four or five rubric dimensions, and score two similarly-capable models on it. If the failures are anti-correlated, you have found free quality: route by task type, or ensemble cheaply. If they are correlated, you have learned that the models are substitutes and you should route purely on cost.
3. **Version and report your harness alongside every compression result, and state whether the number is scaffold-assisted.** Cai's attribution of benchmark divergence to harness differences means an unstated scaffold makes a quantisation result uninterpretable. Adopt the convention that every reported degradation number carries a harness version and a flag for whether retries or self-consistency were enabled. This costs nothing and prevents the most common way compression results mislead their own authors.
4. **Treat base model migration as a first-class pipeline problem now, before the next open-weights release forces it.** Cai names this as a missing abstraction layer and he is right. The asset that should survive a base swap is the dataset plus the recipe, not the tuned weights. Concretely: can you rerun your post-training on a new base with one command? If not, that is the highest-leverage infrastructure work available, and GLM-5.2 at MIT licensing and one-sixth the cost is exactly the kind of release that makes the migration worth doing repeatedly.
5. **Use lab data-buying as a two-to-three month leading indicator for where model capability will land.** The cyber and life-sciences examples both validated within the window. If you can observe which domains vendors are recruiting experts for, you know which capabilities are about to improve, which is directly actionable for deciding what to build against and what not to bother optimising for a model generation that is about to obsolete it.
6. **Never buy evals and training data from the same vendor, and apply the same rule internally.** Letting the test writer grade the test is the structural error behind the fake-benchmark economy Cai describes. Internally the equivalent is one person or team owning both the optimisation and its acceptance criteria. Separate those owners explicitly, and pre-commit the eval set before the optimisation work starts.
7. **Take the robotics warning seriously as a general rule about betting into unsettled research.** When the modality or representation is unresolved, any tooling or vendor commitment is a bet on the research outcome rather than on execution. Before committing to a new representation or format in your own work, ask whether the underlying question is settled. If it is not, build the layer that is agnostic to the answer.
