## Introduction
**Ling-3.0-flash-Sante-fp8** is the FP8-quantized release of [Ling-3.0-flash-Sante](https://huggingface.co/inclusionAI/Ling-3.0-flash-Sante), our healthcare-specialized model built on [Ling-3.0-flash](https://huggingface.co/inclusionAI/Ling-3.0-flash). Its name comes from the French word _santé_, meaning “health.”

The FP8 checkpoint preserves the same **124B total parameters** and **5.1B active parameters per token** as the BF16 model while reducing model-weight memory and improving deployment efficiency on supported hardware. It is designed for **medical reasoning, healthcare research, deep research, and tool-assisted workflows** while retaining the general capabilities of Ling-3.0-flash.

> **Important:** Ling-3.0-flash-Sante-fp8 is a research model, not a medical device. Quantization does not change its intended-use boundaries. Its outputs may be incomplete or incorrect and must not be used as a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. High-stakes use requires qualified human review, independent verification, and compliance with applicable privacy, security, and healthcare regulations.

## FP8 Quantization
Ling-3.0-flash-Sante-fp8 is the deployment-oriented quantized counterpart of Ling-3.0-flash-Sante. The checkpoint uses blockwise **FP8 E4M3 quantization** for expert weights, with a **128 × 128 block size**, following the FP8 recipe used for Ling-3.0-flash-fp8. Compatible serving frameworks detect the quantization configuration directly from the checkpoint, so an additional runtime quantization flag is not required.

The FP8 model is intended to provide a practical balance between model quality, memory footprint, and serving throughput. Because numerical behavior and performance can vary by runtime, kernel, hardware, and parallelism configuration, users should validate the checkpoint on their own workloads before production use.

 Lend and borrow resources across teams with AWS Batch’s quota management for SageMaker Training jobs

 With the launch of AWS Batch support for Amazon SageMaker Training jobs, customers gained a new fully managed way to queue, schedule, and retry machine learning (ML) training workloads without building their own infrastructure. Instead, they use the queuing and scheduling features of AWS Batch. As organizations scale their artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) operations and onboard more teams, coordinating access to shared graphics processing unit (GPU) capacity can introduce additional communication, coordination, and configuration overhead for administrators. This overhead includes setting up separate queues, adjusting capacity limits, and carefully orchestrating the right workloads to get the right resources at the right time. A core value proposition of AWS Batch is helping customers increase utilization of their compute, and as training environments grow, so does the opportunity to do more with existing capacity.

 Working backward from customers managing multi-team training environments, we heard a consistent request: give us a way to define allocations per team or project, share idle capacity automatically, and run our highest-priority workloads first. This request led us to build quota management , a new scheduling capability for AWS Batch. Administrators can use it to divide compute capacity into quota shares, organized by team, project, or workload. Each quota share uses configurable resource-sharing strategies that maximize GPU utilization while honoring each team’s allocation when it matters most.

 This post shows how quota management works, including how to configure quota shares with lending and borrowing strategies, set up in-share preemption for priority workloads, and get started using the SageMaker Python SDK.

 Quota shares and preemption

 Administrators can allocate and share constrained GPU resources by defining a quota share for separate entities, such as individual teams or projects. Each quota share includes compute quotas (capacity limits) and resource-sharing strategies for idle compute. These strategies determine whether idle compute is reserved until jobs arrive ( RESERVE ), lent to other quota shares that have work available ( LEND ), or both lent when idle and borrowed when the share has work that might benefit ( LEND_AND_BORROW ).

 A quota share operates as a virtual queue nested hierarchically within an associated job queue. When scheduling jobs for a job queue, AWS Batch prioritizes quota shares that are under their capacity limits as it iterates through the associated quota shares. If jobs arrive in a quota share that has lent out its capacity, AWS Batch performs cross-share preemption. This reclaims resources for the lending quota share, so administrators don’t need to track down the jobs borrowing capacity and terminate them on their own. If jobs arrive in a quota share that is at or above its capacity limit, AWS Batch checks whether there’s available capacity to borrow from another quota share. If so, workloads can run using otherwise-idle capacity.

 You can turn on priority-based in-share preemption when you configure a quota share. Where cross-share preemption reclaims lent capacity as jobs arrive in a lending quota share, in-share preemption preempts lower priority jobs that are in the SCHEDULED, STARTING, or RUNNING state. This allows higher priority jobs at the front of the queue to start running sooner.

 This post was co-written with Nikhil Kumar and Shakir Khan from MoEngage. 

 When engineering teams start building AI agents, the capabilities those agents need usually already exist as internal APIs. Campaign creation, segment queries, delivery stats. The challenge isn’t building new functionality. The challenge is making hundreds of private APIs safely reachable by agents without exposing them to the public internet, without giving models control over identity, and without flooding context windows with hundreds of tool definitions.

 MoEngage, the agentic customer engagement platform that more than 1,350 consumer brands use to deliver personalized experiences across push, email, in-app, SMS, WhatsApp, and RCS, faced exactly this problem. Behind the product sit roughly 400 internal REST APIs, built by 25 engineering teams, running in private virtual private clouds (VPCs) across seven AWS Regions. MoEngage isolates these APIs regionally to meet data residency commitments and doesn’t expose them to the public internet. The capability the agents needed was already running in production. Making it reachable, safely, was the hard part.

 In this post, we walk through how MoEngage used AgentCore Gateway, a capability of Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, to turn those private APIs into permission-scoped agent tools. The approach saved MoEngage an estimated six months of platform engineering effort, and onboarding a new API now takes hours instead of days. No product team wrote Model Context Protocol (MCP) server code, no networking was provisioned per integration, and no public endpoint exists anywhere in the path.

 Why 20 MCP servers was the wrong answer

 The Model Context Protocol (MCP) gives agents a standard way to discover and call tools. The straightforward path to adopting it is for each team to expose its own MCP server. MoEngage costed that out and rejected it.

 Over 20 MCP servers means over 20 deployments to monitor, patch, and keep protocol-compatible as MCP evolves. Cross-domain agents, the useful ones that read a campaign, check its segment, and inspect delivery stats, would need to connect to several servers and reconcile them. And every team would need MCP expertise unrelated to its actual product work.

 So MoEngage established the principle that shaped everything that followed: Individual engineering teams do not implement MCP. Teams build REST APIs and declare which operations should be tools. The AI Platform team owns the MCP layer end to end. A team’s responsibility ends at a well-described OpenAPI spec.

 That principle immediately created three requirements: a central tool surface that agents connect to once rather than per-service, per-agent authorization enforced server-side rather than by convention, and private connectivity that does not need to be rebuilt for every new API.

 Building all of this from scratch was an option. A custom central MCP server would solve the 20-servers problem but leave MoEngage building a tool-generation pipeline, a policy file format, a tool-alias registry for renames, and circuit breakers. All custom code, all owned by one team, all of it undifferentiated work that does not make MoEngage’s agents smarter or its customers’ campaigns better.

 Four constraints that shaped the architecture

 Before evaluating any solution, MoEngage defined four constraints. All four had to be met simultaneously. A solution that addressed two but broke the third and fourth was not viable.

 Nothing gets a public endpoint. MoEngage’s internal APIs are VPC-private by policy. Any solution that required exposing them, even behind additional authentication, was a non-starter. The conventional workaround is per-integration networking: a NAT gateway here, a reverse proxy there, a VPN tunnel for the third one. Multiply that by seven Regions and the operational surface grows faster than the set of agents does. Every new API would mean another networking change request, another set of security reviews, another piece of infrastructure to maintain.

 Identity can’t be an agent’s decision. Every internal API call carries tenant, user, role, and database context. If that context is a large language model (LLM)-visible tool parameter, a model can get it wrong or be talked into changing it. MoEngage needed identity to be structurally out of the model’s reach, injected by the calling service and not surfaced as something the model can set or override.

 A 400-tool catalog doesn’t fit in a context window. At roughly 50 tokens of JSON schema per tool, a full tools/list across the estate is over 20,000 tokens transmitted on every connection. That token budget is spent before the agent does anything useful, and it crowds out the context the agent actually needs to reason about the task. Client-side filtering ( tool_filter in ADK or the MCP SDK) doesn’t fix this. The full catalog has already crossed the wire, and filtering by client convention isn’t an authorization boundary. An agent that is not supposed to see write operations shouldn’t receive them in the first place.

 That last constraint is the one that avoided the obvious alternatives. A custom central MCP server solves the 20-servers problem but still leaves MoEngage building a tool-generation pipeline, a policy file format, a tool-alias registry for renames, and circuit breakers. All custom code, all owned by one team, none of it making MoEngage’s product better.

 How AgentCore Gateway maps to those constraints

 Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Gateway is a fully managed MCP server. You register OpenAPI specs as targets and the gateway generates MCP tools from them, then routes calls to the backing services. Four properties mapped directly onto MoEngage’s constraints.

 OpenAPI in, MCP tools out. There’s no MCP server to build or operate, and no separate artifact to keep in sync with the API. The spec teams already maintain is the tool definition. Each operation becomes a tool named Target___operationId , for example CampaignsAPI___searchCampaigns . When the spec changes, the tool changes. When a team adds an endpoint, the tool appears. When a team removes one, the tool disappears. No deployment, no protocol expertise, no coordination with the AI Platform team.

 Cedar policies, evaluated before the catalog is serialized. The AgentCore policy engine uses partial evaluation to filter tools/list per principal. Each agent authenticates as its own IAM role, and its Cedar policy determines which tools it can see and call. This is what makes 400 tools tractable. It isn’t client-side filtering after the fact, but server-side scoping before the response is built.

This is the property that broke the multiply by seven Regions problem. The networking exists once and every API uses it.

 Semantic search as a gateway capability. With the x_amz_bedrock_agentcore_search capability, an agent can find tools by describing the task instead of naming the tool. It removed the need for a custom discovery layer.  Travel platforms serve millions of daily users by answering questions about destinations, offering hotel recommendations, and helping users plan their trips. In recent years, many platforms have turned to large language models (LLMs) to improve these services.
 The LLMs give accurate, contextually relevant travel advice thanks to their size: they typically have tens of billions of parameters. Unfortunately, such large models are also computationally expensive, and that expense scales with the number of customer queries. What’s more, such large models can be too slow for web and mobile applications, where a delay of even 100 milliseconds can cost a company customers and revenue.
 To address these scaling and latency challenges, we explored two complementary optimization techniques. One is a compression technique known as knowledge distillation (KD), in which a slow but powerful model produces training targets for a faster, lightweight model. The other, called Medusa , is a framework that transforms sequential next-token predictions — a common bottleneck in LLMs — into parallel computations.
 Working with leading online travel agency Booking.com, we applied KD and Medusa techniques to the LLMs that power various aspects of its user experience, looking to improve either latency or cost effectiveness. We focused on three features in particular:

 The AI Trip Planner: A conversational assistant that must extract travel preferences from dialogue in less than a second (latency);
 Smart Filters: A feature that converts natural-language search queries into structured information filters in real time (latency);
 Accommodation Description Generation: A feature that produces property descriptions on the scale of millions of listings (cost effectiveness).
 We demonstrated that combining both KD and Medusa maintains the quality of the best open-source LLMs at less than one-third the cost while running up to 20 times as fast. Our framework ensures that travel platforms or other applications that rely on LLMs can deliver fast, cost-effective, accurate responses in real-world scenarios.  LLM-powered Smart Filters convert natural-language search queries into structured information filters in real time. 

 Our approach: Medusa plus knowledge distillation

 Medusa is a framework designed to reduce LLM latency in real-time applications such as chat and code generation. It improves computational efficiency at run time — inference time — rather than during training. Ordinarily, an LLM generates text one token — word or word part — at a time. Medusa accelerates this process by predicting multiple tokens in parallel.
 Medusa does this by adding prediction modules, called Medusa heads, to the base LLM, one head for each token decoded in parallel. GPU hardware that LLMs run on is optimized for parallelism, so it can handle parallel token decoding with little additional overhead. Medusa offers significant speedups, typically 2-4-fold without compromising generation quality.
 Knowledge distillation addresses the deployment challenges of large, parameter-heavy models by transferring knowledge from larger “teacher” models to smaller “student” models. Typically, a teacher model is first trained or fine-tuned on high-quality labeled data, then used to generate predictions on additional unlabeled data. The manually labeled and machine-labeled data are then combined to train a smaller student model, which learns to mimic the teacher’s behavior. The student model retains much of the teacher’s performance but is significantly more efficient, making it better suited for real-world scenarios such as high-volume consumer-facing websites.
 In our work, we integrate KD and Medusa in a unified pipeline starting with pretrained base models. As in standard KD, we fine-tune the teacher on the labeled dataset and use it to generate predictions on unlabeled, domain-relevant text. During the student-training stage, however, we attach Medusa heads to the student model and train it on the combined corpus of teacher predictions and labeled data. This produces a single compact model that inherits the teacher's knowledge while also gaining multitoken-prediction capabilities.

 The experiment

 We chose TinyLlama — a compact model with 1.1 billion parameters — as our student and Llama-3.1 with 70 billion parameters as the teacher. We ran four progressive experiments, each building on the last.
 We first fine-tuned TinyLlama on task-specific data for each of our three use cases — AI trip planning, smart filtering, and accommodation description generation. Even without further optimization, the fine-tuned models were significantly more accurate than few-shot GPT-4o, confirming that domain-specific fine tuning compensates for their compact size. However, their performance still fell short of the much larger teacher model’s.
 Applying knowledge distillation — training the student on a blend of human-annotated and teacher-annotated data — closed that gap by more than 90%, bringing the small model to near parity with the teacher. The smaller model size also made inference roughly 6 times as fast, but that’s still not fast enough for real-time use.
 Adding Medusa heads, however, sped up inference an additional 2 to 3.6 times. We tested two Medusa variants: with Medusa-1, we first fine-tuned the base LLM, then froze its weights and fine-tuned the Medusa heads separately; with Medusa-2, we trained everything in a single stage. Both achieved comparable speedups with no meaningful quality loss, but Medusa-2's simpler pipeline made it more practical for production teams managing multiple models.

 Outcomes

 Combining KD with Medusa-2 delivered both gains simultaneously: our final model matched the quality of the 70-billion-parameter teacher while running 10 to 20 times as fast, depending on the use case.
 For each of the three use cases, we used the pipeline described above to fine-tune a dedicated model with domain-specific data.  How We Implemented Catching JiTTests at Meta 

 JiTTests are automatically generated and designed to fail, surfacing bugs in code changes (diffs) before they land in production. When they’re applied to a codebase as large as Meta’s, one challenge is distinguishing strong catches (i.e., true-positive failures that reveal real bugs) from weak catches (i.e., tests that may be false positives). 

 It boils down to a specific question: For every proposed diff, how can we automatically generate tests that find the most severe bugs before they cause severe issues, and do so with a high true-positive rate and without impeding the velocity of our engineers? 

 To address this, we developed two tandem workflows: 

 An “intent-aware” workflow that infers the intention and risks of a code change and targets test generation toward likely failure points. 

 A  “dodgy diff” workflow that treats every code change as if it were a mutant/bug and generates tests to expose its behavioral differences without reasoning about intent. It implicitly asserts that a diff is buggy (hence the term “dodgy”).

 To filter false positives, we employed a traditional rule-based approach combined with an ensemble approach we call “LLM-as-Judge,” which aggregates judgements from several state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs, all tasked with classifying weak catches as likely true or false positives. 

 We also maintained human review, with an eye toward reducing review load. Our goal is to maintain the consistency of human labeling but reduce the likelihood of an engineer being brought in to review a weak catch. 

 How It Works 

 The entire system workflow happens in five stages: 

 Stage 1: Targeting and Triggering  

 The system runs overnight on spare capacity and targets diffs submitted the previous day that are deemed “risky” using a mechanism similar to Diff Risk Score (DRS) and trained on previous code changes. This helps ensure test generation is focused on diffs that are most likely to contain severe regressions. 

 Stage 2: Two Diff-Aware Test-Generation Workflows 

 The dodgy diff workflow treats each diff as if it were a mutant and uses mutation-guided LLM-based test generation to produce tests that distinguish the diff’s behavior from its parent. The goal here is to maximize the pool of weak catches and with it the chances of finding a strong catch among them. 

 At the same time, the intent-aware workflow uses an LLM to infer the diff’s intention using the code changes, diff title, and summary. The LLM then describes the “risks” — the ways an attempt to implement the intent could produce errors. It then uses those risk descriptions to construct mutants — each representing a bug that could be introduced while implementing the inferred intent. 

 Tests are generated to kill each mutant (i.e., distinguish mutant behavior from parent). 

 Those tests are then run on the actual diff. Any that fail are harvested as weak catches. 

 Stage 3: LLM-as-Judge Ensemble (Automated Assessment) 

 Once weak catches are generated, the system must determine whether each is a false positive or actually a strong catch (true positive). 

 The LLM-as-Judge ensemble consists of a collection of LLMs that review: 

 T he weak-catch test-case code. 

 T he test-execution outcome (failure code, message, execution trace). 

 The code changes between parent and child (the diff). 

 The inferred diff intent. 

 Stage 4: Rule-Based Assessor (RubFake) 

 We complement LLM-as-Judge with a more traditional, rule-based approach called Rule-Based False-Positive Killing Environment (RubFake). 

 LLM-as-Judge has rich semantics, but its judgements are non-deterministic and challenging to verify. By contrast, RubFake provides verified, deterministic judgments. Working together, RubFake and LLM-as-Judge produce an overall ranking of weak-catch candidates, with those ranked sufficiently highly being worthy of further consideration. 

 Stage 5: Human-on-the-Loop (Engineer Reach-Out) 

 Based on the ranking from LLM-as-Judge and RubFake, the system then decides whether to reach out to human engineers. 

 Rather than a human-in-the-loop approach, where a human engineer reviews every output, we’ve reduced friction through a human- on -the-loop approach, where human engineers set criteria and review outcomes at an aggregate level rather than supervising every output.

 Atomistic simulations help researchers understand how molecules and materials behave, from chemical reactions and phase transitions to mechanical failure. These simulations come with a cost-accuracy trade-off. Quantum-mechanical methods such as density functional theory (DFT) can provide accurate energies and forces, but their computational cost often limits their use for large systems or long simulations. Classical interatomic potentials are much faster, but their fixed functional forms limit accuracy and generalizability across different atomic environments. Machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) help bridge this gap. They use neural networks to predict potential energies and interatomic forces at a fraction of the cost, enabling researchers to study larger systems and longer timescales while retaining much of the required accuracy.

 Running an MLIP efficiently, however, presents its own challenge. Atomistic simulations are sensitive to small errors that can accumulate over time and are most visible when computing forces, which are obtained by differentiating the predicted energy. During molecular dynamics simulations, these forces guide updates to atomic velocities and positions over many timesteps, making numerical precision important not only for physical accuracy but also for the stability of the resulting trajectory.

 For this reason, many MLIPs continue to run inference in FP32, even though modern NVIDIA GPUs offer substantially higher matrix-multiplication throughput through low-precision Tensor Core operations. Tensor Core methods for FP32 matrix multiplication offer a way to narrow that gap. They decompose each FP32 operand into lower-precision components, evaluate their cross-products on Tensor Cores, and recombine the results in FP32. This approach can increase matrix-multiplication throughput while retaining accuracy close to native FP32, without retraining or changing the model’s weights or architecture.

 In this post, we evaluate how Tensor Core FP32 methods perform in practice on the Point Edge Transformer (PET), a transformer-based MLIP developed by Lab COSMO at EPFL. PET spends much of its runtime in highly optimized general matrix multiplications (GEMMs), leaving limited headroom for further optimization and making it a demanding test case of whether these methods can deliver additional performance gains.

 We evaluate BF16x9 on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs and TF32x3 on NVIDIA Ampere GPUs, comparing kernel and end-to-end molecular dynamics performance and trajectory-level behavior. We further test the TF32x3 method for potential-energy smoothness, energy conservation, and structural relaxation. We show how these methods can accelerate MLIP inference while retaining behavior close to the FP32 baseline and outline how to evaluate them on other precision-sensitive scientific machine learning workloads.

 How Tensor Core FP32 closes the hardware gap 

 Modern NVIDIA GPUs deliver substantially higher Tensor Core throughput for BF16 and TF32 than for FP32 matrix multiplication, creating a gap between the available throughput at lower precision and the precision requirements of many scientific workloads. Tensor Core FP32 methods help narrow this gap by decomposing FP32 operands into lower-precision components, evaluating selected products on Tensor Cores, and recombining them with FP32 accumulation. Depending on the method and workload, this can deliver accuracy at or near native FP32 while taking advantage of higher Tensor Core throughput.

 Figure 1. The BF16x9 algorithm on Blackwell GPUs. Each FP32 operand is split into three scaled BF16 components, producing nine pairwise products that are accumulated in FP32 along five anti-diagonal bands 

 On Blackwell GPUs, NVIDIA cuBLAS provides a BF16x9 Tensor Core path (Figure 1). Each FP32 operand is decomposed into three BF16 components, and nine pairwise products are recombined with the required scaling. cuBLAS can enable this path through the CUBLAS_EMULATE_SINGLE_PRECISION=1 environment variable, so compatible PyTorch GEMMs can use it without changing the model architecture or implementing a custom GEMM.

 Because this approach changes how GEMMs are evaluated, it may provide additional gains when combined with optimized graph construction, fused operations, custom kernels, and NVIDIA CUDA graphs. We did not measure the combined gain in this evaluation; it should be established end to end for each workload.

 Figure 2. The TF32x3 algorithm on Ampere GPUs. Each FP32 operand is split into a leading TF32 component and a smaller residual component. The leading-by-leading and two leading-by-residual products are accumulated in FP32; the residual-by-residual product is omitted 

 The TF32x3 method applies the same principle on Ampere GPUs (Figure 2). TF32x3 represents each FP32 operand using a leading TF32 component and a residual TF32 component. Unlike BF16x9, our TF32x3 implementation does not use scaling factors. It evaluates three cross-products and omits the residual-by-residual term, as shown in the following equation

 a ⋅ b ≈ a hi b hi + a hi b lo + a lo b hi a \cdot b \approx a_{\mathrm{hi}}b_{\mathrm{hi}} + a_{\mathrm{hi}}b_{\mathrm{lo}} + a_{\mathrm{lo}}b_{\mathrm{hi}} 

 NVIDIA CUTLASS provides this approach through the OpMultiplyAddFastF32 operator. Inputs and outputs remain in FP32. Tensor Cores execute the three TF32 products with accumulation in FP32.

 On Ampere GPUs, we implemented TF32x3 by replacing PET’s standard nn.Linear layers with a custom TF32Linear layer that calls a CUTLASS C++ TF32x3 kernel.

 C++

cutlass::arch::OpMultiplyAddFastF32

 Key Takeaways 

 Let people go first. Invite their sketch, draft, or point of view before AI suggests anything.

 Make expertise easy to give and keep. Let people supply context and carry it forward, so they don’t re-explain it each time.

 Build checking into the work. Put verification, review points, and ways to intervene in the workflow, not in people’s memory.

 People bring their own ideas to AI, give it context it can’t infer, and check answers they remain responsible for. How could the experiences we design make those contributions easier?

 At Dreamforce, the Salesforce Research & Insights team asked 2,714 Trailblazers which human strengths they already bring to their work with AI, which they want to bring more of, and how they apply them. The research wall activity we hosted was one wide-angle look at this question. It sits alongside a deeper research partnership with the Salesforce Office of Ethical and Humane Use on how experts encode their judgment to get higher-quality outcomes with AI.

 The responses, from a self-selected group of conference participants, inspired five questions designers might consider when they build AI experiences.

 Here’s what we’ll cover:

 Who gets to offer the first idea: the person or AI? 
 Does the experience build on people’s expertise, or make them explain it all over again? 
 Is it as easy to check and challenge an AI answer as it is to accept it?

 Who gets to offer the first idea: the person or AI?

 Creativity showed the widest gap between the strengths people said they already bring and those they want to bring. It received the most “want to bring” votes. One Trailblazer wrote, “I rely on ideas from AI too heavily. I’d like to strengthen that skill.”

 For us, that gap suggests a concern beneath the excitement about AI. In a world of instant drafts and confident answers, people may want more room to exercise their own creativity.

 Participants got one vote each for the most important strength they are currently bringing and the one they most want to bring. The highest share in each column is starred. [Salesforce Research & Insights] 

 Trailblazers also described ways they protect that room. A recurring approach was simple: human goes first. “I draw a sketch first, then take it to AI,” one wrote. Another explained, “I come up with the creative idea, then ask AI about any challenges with it, and to expand on it to bring it further to life.”

 In these examples, people bring an idea before asking AI to pressure-test or extend it. Others described using prompting itself as a creative medium: questioning the norm, reframing a problem, and iterating. They also revised generated content and rejected suggestions that didn’t sound like them.

 These practices raise a design question about where the thinking begins. Could an experience invite a person’s sketch, draft, or point of view before offering a suggestion? Giving people that option could help them protect the creativity they want to bring, while still using AI to develop it.

 Does the experience build on people’s expertise, or make them explain it all over again?

 Domain expertise received the most “already bringing” votes at our wall. Participants also wanted to apply it more. It was a strength they recognized as central to their work with AI.

 That expertise includes hard-won knowledge of the business, industry, and Salesforce org. It also includes a read on organizational politics and culture: what will work, what might stall, and why. People described giving AI context it couldn’t infer and telling it which sources to trust.

 Their contributions extended across business and technology. They translated requirements into technical solutions, connected knowledge across systems, and used what they knew to spot mistakes or nuances in AI’s output. Expertise also helped them decide where AI fit and when not to use it.

 For designers, the question is how to let that knowledge into the experience and keep it available. Can people supply relevant background and correct an assumption? Can the expertise they contribute support later work, rather than have to be reconstructed in every prompt? Skills, agents, and workflow templates are possible ways to carry expertise forward ; the design consideration is making context easy to give and easy to keep.

 Is it as easy to check and challenge an AI answer as it is to accept it?

 Judgment was the second-most-selected strength people said they already bring. Their comments were less about having good instincts and more about deliberately testing AI’s work.

 “Pressure test outputs based on real-life scenarios,” one Trailblazer wrote. Another asked, “Don’t just tell me. Show me your sources of how you got there.” Participants described checking answers against domain knowledge, comparing responses across models, and questioning the original request before prompting.

 These are ways of deciding whether an answer holds up and whether it addresses the right problem. Some also asked AI to validate its own output. That can be part of the process, but it doesn’t replace checking against reliable sources and real-world knowledge.

 “At the end of the day, I still own it and have to attest to it.”

 The design opportunity is to treat people’s checking habits as something the experience can support. Can they inspect sources, test an answer against a relevant scenario, and challenge an assumption? Can they repeat those checks as the work develops? The question is whether scrutiny has a place in the workflow, rather than depending entirely on a personal routine.

 Is verification built into the work, or left for people to remember?

 Accountability received the fewest votes among the named strengths, both for “already bringing” and “want to bring.” Yet the most-starred note on the wall was about accountability. It observed that AI has “no skin in the game.”

 Our read is that people may notice the expertise and judgment they bring up front more than the checking they do at the end. That final checking can be where they decide whether the work is ready to use. As one Trailblazer put it, “At the end of the day, I still own it and have to attest to it.”

 Participants described reviewing drafts before external use, rerunning numbers, testing repeatedly, and escalating to a human subject matter expert when unsure. Verification gave them a basis for taking responsibility for the result.

 For designers, this raises the question of whether review is a visible part of the work or something people have to remember afterward. Where can someone verify a consequential result before it is shared or acted on? What happens if they find a problem or need expert help?

 For an agent that takes action , responsibility also involves defining boundaries beforehand and reviewing behavior over time. The experience could support those steps by making limits, review points, and opportunities to intervene clear. The aim is to give people a way to exercise the accountability that remains theirs.

 How could the experience help people build trust with others?

 Relationships received fewer votes, but participants described them as important to building organizational trust in the AI systems they implement. They also wanted to use AI to deepen those relationships.

 Trailblazers described starting with small proofs of concept so hesitant coworkers could see value, then protecting that trust through careful data stewardship. They involved multiple stakeholders and gathered feedback before prompting, to confirm they were solving the right problem. They also revised generated content to sound human and resonate with its intended audience.

 “I bring my life experiences that no AI has, like paper cuts!”

 These practices connect trust in a system with the relationships around it. For designers, they raise questions about how an experience could support stakeholder input, thoughtful communication, and opportunities for people to respond to the work. AI can assist, while people continue to listen, understand concerns, and earn trust.

 Some of the strengths people shared didn’t fit neatly into the categories on our wall: empathy, values, and lived experience. One of our favorite notes captured that with a wonderfully specific example: “I bring my life experiences that no AI has, like paper cuts!”

 That comment is a reminder that the contribution a person makes can extend beyond the expertise a task calls for. Their experiences and values also shape what they notice, question, and care about.

 Bring these questions to your next design review

 People at our research wall described ways they already bring their strengths to AI-assisted work. Use these five questions to consider how your design could support those practices: making room for an idea, retaining expertise, enabling repeatable checks, making verification visible, and involving others. Look for one moment where doing so could reduce the extra work a person has to remember.

 We found a hopeful thread in these reflections: people want to bring their whole selves to their work with AI, and they’re finding ways to do it. That leaves a question for us as designers and as people using these tools: What’s the human strength you most want to bring, and how could the experience make room for it to shine?
