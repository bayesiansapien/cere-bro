```text
 Running a frontier-scale model locally means keeping all its weights in GPU memory; the largest models no longer fit on one machine. Two NVIDIA DGX Stations connected over ConnectX-8 RDMA serve one model through a single endpoint, with tensor parallelism splitting the model weights and computation across both GPUs. 

 The NVIDIA GLM-5.3 NVFP4 model, a quantized version of Z.ai ’s mixture-of-experts (MoE) model, has 753 billion parameters, with 40 billion activated per token. Sparse activation reduces computation, but all model weights still need to remain available because each token can be routed to different experts. At about 464 GB, the checkpoint exceeds the 252 GB of HBM3e on a single GB300 GPU. This tutorial uses two DGX Stations to keep the weights in GPU memory, with CPU weight offload disabled.

 Before starting, connect the two Stations with the Connect Two DGX Stations playbook .

 This post shows how to serve GLM-5.3 locally across two NVIDIA DGX Stations with vLLM, send a request, and measure behavior under concurrent load. Tensor parallelism (TP=2) splits the model’s tensors across the two GB300 GPUs. Both Stations compute every request, and Station A returns the answer through one OpenAI-compatible API. By the end, you’ll have a working endpoint and a baseline for how it behaves under load.
```

```text
XYBench: Helping with the problem behind the question
 November 13, 2026
 Ai2 

 Share 

 Paper Data Code 

 Truly useful AI assistants need to be able to help users who ask misguided questions—not just users who ask the right question with the right amount of detail. We’re releasing XYBench , a benchmark of 8K+ questions that tests how AI assistants respond to requests based on misconceptions, such as asking how to thicken frosting that keeps sliding off cupcakes when the real problem is that the cupcakes are still too warm to frost. XYBench won the Best Resource Paper Award at EMNLP 2026.
 A mismatch between the approach someone asks about and the goal they want to achieve is known as the XY problem : a person wants to accomplish X, assumes a particular approach Y will work, and asks for help with Y. In the cupcake example, the person is so committed to making the frosting stick that they fail to consider the warmth of the cupcake as the true problem to be addressed.
 Developed with researchers at Carnegie Mellon, NVIDIA, and Johns Hopkins, XYBench exposes a critical gap in LLMs' ability to troubleshoot problems: recognizing when a user's request is based on an implicit misconception and appropriately redirecting them toward a solution that addresses their actual goal. Drawing on 8,115 real-world questions spanning programming and everyday activities such as cooking and home repair, we introduce a benchmark that measures whether models identify these misconceptions and are able to prioritize effective solutions in their responses.
 The models we evaluated on XYBench – Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen3.5-9B, and DeepSeek-V4 Pro – often suggested a solution to the user’s problem but kept their answers focused on the original, mistaken approach. Unlike human reference answers, all five models were less likely to steer users away from that approach and toward a solution suited to their goal.
 People use AI systems with different levels of knowledge and may not always know how best to approach a problem. We hope XYBench helps researchers measure whether model improvements make assistants better at recognizing mistaken assumptions, explaining why a proposed approach might not work, and suggesting more effective alternatives—even when users don't know the right questions to ask.
 Building XYBench
 We built XYBench from four sources: 1,557 questions from Stack Overflow, 184 from Stack Exchange, 102 manually curated examples, and 6,272 questions generated from WikiHow guides. For each question, we identified the stated request (Y), what the person explicitly asks for, and the intended request (X)—the underlying goal that can be inferred from context.
 For the public forum questions, we used the three highest-scored human answers and the accepted answer, where available, to establish the intended request and possible solutions. We filtered out cases where answering the stated request was judged sufficient. For WikiHow, we used Sonnet 4.5 to generate questions around plausible misconceptions in procedural guides, then refined them to include enough context for an expert to infer the underlying goal without stating it outright. We removed examples where answering the literal request would largely resolve the problem.
 Evaluating how an answer helps
 A model might spend several paragraphs recommending a more powerful vacuum cleaner, then briefly suggest checking for a clogged hose. If a clog is causing the weak suction, clearing it could solve the problem.
 A more helpful answer would recognize that the user wants to restore suction and focus on diagnosing the cause. This draws on pragmatics, the branch of linguistics concerned with how context and communicative intent shape meaning. To test whether models use this understanding in their answers, XYBench measures three aspects of each response:
 Pragmatic solution —records whether a model’s response includes solutions to the stated request (Y) and the intended request (X), checking each separately.
 Pragmatic emphasis —measures whether a model’s answer focuses on the stated request (Y), focuses on the intended request (X), or balances the two.
 Misconception identification —measures whether a model’s response steers the user toward the underlying goal (X) while discouraging or avoiding the mistaken approach (Y).
 In XYBench, Claude Sonnet 4.5 serves as the automated judge. It classifies individual suggestions within each model’s response and assesses the answer’s pragmatic emphasis.
 To validate Sonnet 4.5’s judgments, two human reviewers independently assessed 500 model responses to XYBench questions. Agreement with the judge, averaged across the reviewers, ranged from 97.5–100% on whether a solution appeared, 84.6–93.3% on misconception identification, and 74.2–79.8% on pragmatic emphasis.
 What the experiments show
 We evaluated Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen3.5-9B, and DeepSeek-V4 Pro on XYBench in a single-turn setting, with one response per question. We evaluated human reference answers separately using the same metrics.
 Across all four sources, every model was more likely to include a solution to the stated request (Y) than to the underlying goal (X). On Stack Overflow, stated-request solutions appeared in 75–86% of responses, compared with 59–71% for underlying-goal solutions.
 A passing mention of a solution does not make it the focus of an answer. In the vacuum example, a model could briefly suggest unclogging the hose but focus most of its response on buying a more powerful vacuum. We would count that response as containing a solution to the underlying goal, even though the model emphasized the stated request. We measured overall emphasis separately: on Stack Overflow, models focused primarily on the underlying goal in only 35–41% of their responses, and on the stated request in 47–57%. In the remaining responses, models gave similar emphasis to the stated request and the underlying goal.
```

```text
By Hadrien Zalek , Joe Knox , Alex Chong , Marcel Apfelbaum 

 How Meta's Reality Labs brought Cuttlefish and its QEMU backend to macOS and Apple silicon. 

 Meta’s Reality Labs builds products such as AI glasses . Emulators provide us with a way to scale development and testing without relying on scarce physical hardware. 

 Testing software for non-phone Android devices depends on how closely a virtual device reproduces the software behavior of the physical product. While a lightweight emulator like Goldfish gets you part of the way, differences from the real device can hide bugs until later in development. This lack of fidelity also erodes trust in device emulation, increasing reliance on scarce physical hardware. 

 Cuttlefish is the Android Open Source Project’s (AOSP’s) virtual-device framework for testing Android software against virtual hardware. It boots the same system images as real hardware and runs a realistic software stack against virtual hardware. This enables Cuttlefish to more closely track a physical device’s behavior than a lightweight emulator does. 

 Unfortunately, Cuttlefish is only supported on Linux, and our application and library developers work on Macs with Apple silicon. This forced us to support Goldfish and Cuttlefish side by side. 

 We knew if we could consolidate onto a single Cuttlefish-based platform, we could give all of our developers the benefit of the same high-fidelity virtual device. The challenge was porting the stack to make Cuttlefish run natively on macOS. 

 The Impact of Fragmentation 

 Maintaining dual emulator platforms imposed a heavy operational burden: It drained computing resources, created confusion for developers, and slowed down the emulator team. Every tool required support for two separate platforms, while infrastructure and engineering capacity were divided between them. Furthermore, developers had to choose an emulator target based on how they worked: Goldfish ran everywhere but had less fidelity while Cuttlefish needed Linux. 

 The AOSP mirrors this exact divide: Goldfish targets application developers, whereas Cuttlefish serves Android platform developers. So long as we maintained that division, we would suffer the effects of fragmentation. 

 The Stack and the macOS Gaps 

 Cuttlefish is the orchestration layer, while the component that actually runs the guest OS is a virtual machine monitor (VMM). On Linux, Cuttlefish normally uses crosvm but previously included support for QEMU as an alternative VMM. That integration had become inactive over time . To enable native Apple silicon support on macOS, we revived QEMU support. 

 Many of Cuttlefish’s virtual devices use vhost-user backends, separate processes that provide virtio devices to QEMU over a socket. The architecture is clean, but large parts of it were written with a hard Linux assumption. Three of those assumptions break on macOS. The vhost-user machinery is gated to Linux and won’t build or run. The Apple hypervisor imposes resource limits QEMU didn’t expect. Finally, the native display, build, and signing paths didn’t exist. We tackled all three. 

 The Cuttlefish-on-Mac stack. Cuttlefish orchestrates virtual devices backed by QEMU. The highlighted orange blocks are the Mac-specific work: adding macOS support to vhost-user, increasing memory-region tracking slots in QEMU’s HVF backend, and using the native Cocoa display and code-signing paths. 
 Making vhost-user Portable Beyond Linux 

 The most structurally important changes were in QEMU’s vhost-user path. The frontends, libvhost-user , and the contrib backends such as vhost-user-blk were all gated to Linux. We relaxed those build restrictions so the vhost-user  frontends and libraries can build on macOS as well as Linux. This lets Cuttlefish’s device backends hand off to QEMU on a Mac the same way they do on Linux. Without this, the higher-fidelity device model that makes Cuttlefish worth using isn’t available outside Linux. 

 Living Within Hypervisor.framework 

 On Apple silicon, QEMU accelerates through HVF (Hypervisor.framework) instead of KVM . QEMU’s HVF backend did not track enough memory regions for larger Cuttlefish configurations. We increased its tracking-slot count so those configurations could run correctly. 

 A Usable Native Display 

 Local application development requires an interactive display allowing developers to view and engage directly with the virtual device. To enable direct interaction and bypass reliance on the WebRTC interface, we transitioned to QEMU’s native Cocoa display. The changes include: 

 BGRA Pixel Format Support: Enables accurate display rendering to eliminate visual artifacts on Apple silicon. 

 Mouse-Click-as-Touch: Translates pointer actions into touch input for interacting with touchscreen devices. 

 Custom Geometry Options: Adds the -g flag to graphic_console_init for display configuration. 

 We also enabled the unstable Rutabaga structs needed to match the Rust-side graphics ABI in gfxstream /rutabaga configurations. 

 Building and Signing on Apple Silicon 

 Finally, there was the build and packaging work: initial Darwin build support, building QEMU for arm64 on macOS, and toolchain fixes. The macOS builds also needed the JIT entitlement in addition to support for code signing and notarization. On the Cuttlefish side, we added macOS host support so the framework’s orchestration builds and runs on a Mac instead of assuming a Linux host. 

 The Benefits 

 By moving to a unified platform, we cut maintenance requirements, reduced infrastructure needs, eliminated redundant tooling, enhanced fidelity, and ensured developers run the exact same Cuttlefish virtual devices used across CI. 

 A single platform also made it easier to support capabilities on a shared stack, including: 

 Bluetooth pairing and connectivity. 

 Wi-Fi. 

 Cellular. 

 3D graphics. 

 Over-the-air (OTA) update testing to exercise over-the-air update flows against a virtual device. 

 Fastboot. 

 Snapshotting to save and restore device state in CI and development flows. 

 Beyond supporting Reality Labs engineers, this identical virtual device is shared with external developers building for our ecosystem. This alignment guarantees that third-party teams test against the exact same Cuttlefish-based platform we use to develop and validate the OS itself. 

 With AI agents playing a growing role in Android development, validation execution and runtime behavior is just as essential as code generation. Virtual devices empower both engineers and automated agents to operate concurrently within isolated environments, facilitating application execution and rapid iteration. By enabling instant state resets and repeated test runs, teams eliminate dependencies on shared physical hardware and prevent workflow interruptions. Ultimately, high-fidelity emulation ensures these continuous feedback loops are both rapid and meaningful. 

 Giving Back to the Community 

 Although we built this for development within Reality Labs, the macOS support is useful beyond our products. We’re sharing our work with the community so other developers can build on the QEMU and AOSP foundations that enabled our work. 

 We’re distributing the work in two parts:
```

```text
 Human motion offers roboticists a great starting point for teaching humanoid robots how to move. A human demonstration captures the coordination and timing behind natural movement, from everyday actions to complex tasks. But a robot cannot simply copy the same joint motion from a human. Its proportions, joint structure, and range of motion are different, so the movement needs to be adapted to fit the robot.

 The open source NVIDIA SOMA Retargeter helps you adapt human motion to your robot’s proportions and range of motion. Built around the shared human-motion representation in NVIDIA SOMA-X , it provides tools to configure your robot, refine the resulting movement, and process motion data for downstream training.

 This post explains the retargeting process, the tools for refining robot motion, and the connection to policy learning.

 From human motion to robot joint trajectories 

 Human motion can come from many sources, including a motion capture session, a video, an online dataset, or a generative model. These sources often output motion with different skeletons and file formats. Representing compatible human motion on the SOMA skeleton gives the retargeter a consistent input.

 SOMA Retargeter reads a Biovision Hierarchy (BVH) animation on the SOMA base skeleton. As Figure 1 below shows, it scales motion targets to the robot’s proportions, solves for joint poses, and applies configured corrections. The output is a comma-separated values (CSV) file containing the robot’s root position, orientation, and joint angles for each frame. These trajectories describe the desired movement for a downstream learning or control system.

 Figure 1. The retargeting pipeline converts SOMA BVH motion into robot joint trajectories through scaling, inverse kinematics, and post-processing. Across many clips, the resulting animations can form a motion library for downstream reference preparation and policy training. 

 Sources of human motion 

 Developers can draw on recorded performances, reconstruct motion from video, or generate new movements:

 Motion-capture data. BONES-SEED provides a collection of human motion-capture data.

 Video reconstruction. GEM-X reconstructs whole-body 3D human motion from a single video using the SOMA body model. Its demo workflow connects video reconstruction with G1 retargeting, showing how a recorded action can become a robot motion reference.

 Motion generation. Kimodo and ARDY let developers generate human movement from text and pose constraints. Kimodo supports creating motion sequences from those descriptions, while ARDY adds interactive steering as the motion unfolds. Both offer models that generate motion on the SOMA skeleton.

 Adapting motion to different humanoid robots 

 The SOMA Retargeter v0.2 release includes sample motions supplied by Bones from the BONES-SEED dataset, along with reference retargeting results and configurations for five humanoid robots: Unitree G1 , Unitree H2 , Booster T1, AgiBot X2 Ultra , and AgiBot A3T3 . These examples show how joint angles and foot placement change across humanoid embodiments according to their specific proportions and range of motion. The paired human motions and robot results give you a starting point for exploring the tool and comparing your own output.

 For this example, use the supplied Unitree G1 configuration to retarget a forward walk from the sample motions. After installing SOMA Retargeter , open a terminal in the repository root and start the viewer:

 Under Scene Options → Motion, select Retarget on the BVH Motion row. The retargeter scales the source targets to G1, solves the configured inverse-kinematics objectives for each frame, and applies the configured post-processing. The viewer then displays the robot animation with the human source motion. Positioning gizmos let you separate them for inspection.

 Figure 2 . The BVH to CSV Converter displays the human source motion alongside the retargeted Unitree G1 motion, with controls for loading, retargeting, and saving the result. 

 Use the playback controls to inspect the result. A recognizable forward walk and continuous motion are useful first checks; the robot’s poses will differ from the human’s because its proportions and joints are different.  Configuring your robot and refining its motion 

 The supplied G1 configuration gives you a starting point for retargeting, while the included reference result provides a point of comparison as you adjust the settings to refine the motion. With the Robot Configurator , you can refine an existing robot configuration or create one for a new robot by loading its robot description and defining how human motion maps to it. 

 You can align human and robot reference poses, adjust scale, and map the source skeleton to robot body parts. The resulting configuration can be saved and reused across motion clips.

 Figure 3. The source motion is a one-handed wave, but an unaligned reference pose makes Booster T1 hold both arms outward (left). After aligning the robot’s reference pose with the SOMA human reference, one hand waves beside its head while the other arm rests at its side (right). Both panels use the same input motion and IK settings, isolating the effect of reference-pose alignment. 

 The retargeting solver uses inverse kinematics (IK) from Newton , built with NVIDIA Warp , to find joint poses that follow the scaled human targets. When a robot cannot match every target at once, IK weights determine which body parts take priority.

 The experimental IK weight optimizer tunes these weights using a small set of motions representative of the robot’s intended tasks. It tests weight adjustments by retargeting those motions and checking how closely and smoothly the robot follows the targets. The selected weights are saved in a new retargeting configuration.

 Sliding feet can introduce conflicting targets for a downstream motion-tracking policy: the reference foot moves while the intended action calls for planted contact. Foot-plant correction addresses this artifact by adjusting foot motion during detected contact intervals. You can refine contact timing and correction settings to reduce sliding in the reference motion before it enters a training pipeline.

 Once you have refined a configuration on representative motions, headless batch retargeting lets you reuse it across a larger collection for your target robot, without operating the viewer for each clip.

 Using retargeted motion to train robot policies 

 During training, a motion-tracking policy learns how to turn the retargeted reference into control actions, using both the desired motion and the robot’s current state.

 The SONIC training workflow includes a SOMA CSV converter for G1 . It converts retargeted trajectories into SONIC’s motion-library format for training. ProtoMotions offers another framework for physics-based motion tracking. In either case, the input representation and policy need to match the target robot.

 Simulation provides the next stage of evaluation, where a trained policy can be assessed for balance, ground contact, actuator limits, and tracking accuracy.
```
