 Introducing enhanced infrastructure observability in Amazon SageMaker HyperPod

 Large-scale model training is one of the most expensive workloads you can run on GPUs, and a single unhealthy accelerator can stall an entire distributed job. When training throughput drops, the hard part is rarely noticing that something is wrong. It is figuring out why and where. Teams often spend hours or days sifting through logs and disconnected dashboards to decide whether a slowdown came from a throttled GPU, a saturated network link, or a stalled data pipeline on the host.

 Today, we are announcing enhanced observability in Amazon SageMaker HyperPod, a new capability that helps you diagnose GPU training issues faster. It brings together diagnostic and profiling capabilities for training workloads that run on both Amazon Elastic Kubernetes Service (Amazon EKS) and Slurm orchestrators:

 Diagnostic summary uses Amazon CloudWatch’s DetectGroupOutliers API to compare each GPU in a training job on utilization, memory, temperature, power, and SM clock, and flags GPUs that deviate from their peers. Static diagnostic rules surface common failure patterns such as GPU hardware faults (XID errors, ECC memory errors), EFA throughput drops, thermal throttling, and overheating, so you catch degradation before it affects training.

 Why you need it

 Distributed training is expensive, and small inefficiencies compound into significant wasted GPU hours. Until now, getting visibility into HyperPod infrastructure meant stitching together separate tools, and even then, the metrics told you that performance had degraded without telling you why.

 Enhanced observability closes three gaps. It isolates a single bad accelerator across hundreds or thousands of GPUs whether through XID errors, ECC memory faults, or thermal throttling, so you spend time on the fix instead of the search.

 Together, these capabilities reduce troubleshooting time and help you keep expensive accelerators busy.

 How it works

 Each capability targets a different layer of a distributed training job.

 Default and advanced metrics

 Enhanced observability collects telemetry at two levels:

 Default metrics (enabled automatically) cover cluster health, CPU/disk/OS-level usage, GPU utilization and temperature, and per-job resource utilization. These metrics are collected every 30 seconds and sent to your chosen metric store with approximately 1–2% CPU overhead and 250–300 MB of memory.

 Advanced metrics (opt-in) provide deeper visibility:

 Full node exporter suite: comprehensive host-level metrics.

 All NVIDIA GPU DCGM and AWS Neuron metrics: detailed accelerator telemetry including clock speeds, power, PCIe activity, and error indicators.

 Elastic Fabric Adapter (EFA) metrics: network interface throughput, drops, retransmissions, and errors.

 Advanced metrics are optional because they increase the volume of telemetry sent to your metric store, which has a cost implication. Most customers running small jobs on small clusters may not need them, but they are essential for long-running training jobs on large clusters where patterns emerge over time.

 Metric stores

 You can choose between two metric stores:

 Amazon CloudWatch (new default) provides native monitoring through CloudWatch SageMaker Insights, so you can visualize metrics without leaving the AWS Management Console.

 Amazon Managed Service for Prometheus for teams that standardize on Prometheus and Grafana for observability.

 Previously, only Prometheus was supported. CloudWatch is now the default, offering lower pricing compared to Amazon Managed Service Prometheus and tighter integration with the SageMaker console.

 Diagnostic summary

 The diagnostic summary uses two complementary approaches to surface issues across your HyperPod cluster.

 GPU outlier detection uses CloudWatch’s DetectGroupOutliers API to compare GPUs within a training job across utilization, memory usage, temperature, power draw, and SM clock frequency. When a GPU deviates significantly from its peers, it appears as an outlier card in the diagnostic summary. This works for both EKS and Slurm jobs.

 Static diagnostic rules evaluate known failure patterns and organize detected issues by type:

 Hardware: GPU ECC memory errors, XID faults, accelerator failures.

 Network: EFA throughput degradation, packet drops, retransmissions.

 Throttling: GPU clock throttling because of heat, board, or reliability limits.

 Overheating: GPU temperatures exceeding configured limits.

 Each detected issue identifies the affected job, cluster, node, accelerator, and rank.

 Using NVIDIA Run:ai GPU Fractioning on Amazon EKS

 GPU sharing is not new: Multi-Instance GPU (MIG), time-slicing, and Multi-Process Service (MPS) address different isolation and utilization needs. NVIDIA Run:ai GPU Fractioning builds on MPS with Kubernetes-integrated scheduling, per-Pod memory contracts, enforcement, and metrics.

 NVIDIA Run:ai is AI workload orchestration software for Kubernetes. Its GPU Fractioning Operator can help several teams share one or two GPUs on Amazon Elastic Kubernetes Service (Amazon EKS) . Notebooks, model evaluations, and small inference services can leave teams waiting even when GPU memory remains available. In this walkthrough’s baseline, the NVIDIA device plugin exposes the single GPU as one nvidia.com/gpu resource, with GPU sharing disabled. Two Pods each requesting nvidia.com/gpu: 1 cannot run concurrently on that node, even when their combined GPU-memory demand fits.

 NVIDIA Run:ai GPU Fractioning adds Kubernetes scheduling integration, enforced GPU-memory limits, and per-Pod metrics to software-based sharing. The approach applies to NVIDIA GPUs that meet the GPU Fractioning Operator’s hardware and software requirements, including GPUs without MIG. We use one Amazon Elastic Compute Cloud (Amazon EC2) g5.xlarge instance with an NVIDIA A10G Tensor Core GPU as the validated example.  Designing acquisition paths for intelligent instruments 

 The latest DAQIRI release adds hardware packet reordering, an optimized library backend for improved throughput, and direct polling for low-latency packet transmission and reception.

 Reorder data as it arrives with NIC hardware 

 Most networks rely on protocol processing or receive-side buffering to handle out-of-order packets, which can add latency and memory overhead. DAQIRI can now offload packet reordering tasks to supported NVIDIA ConnectX-7 or newer NICs, preserving CPU and GPU resources for analysis. For compatible sequence formats, the NIC parses sequence fields and places each payload directly into its ordered position in memory. The application receives the assembled, ordered data without requiring a separate software reorder copy step.

 Hardware-based reordering is defined here for a specific receive queue. The configuration dictates how to parse the sequence number from the packet header, which reordering method to use, and how missing packets should be handled.

- name: "rx_reorder_seq_1024"
 reorder_engine: "hw" # NIC places payloads in order
 cyclic_sequence: true # Confirm compatible cyclic numbering
 missing_action: “drop” # Drop partial batches on RX timeout
 reorder_type: "gpu" # Deliver packets to GPU memory
 memory_region: "Reorder_RX_GPU"
 payload_byte_offset: 42 # Skip 42-byte Ethernet/IPv4/UDP header
 packet_size: 8192 # Payload bytes per output slot
 flow_ids: [201] # Apply reorder to RX flow 201
 method:
 seq_packets_per_batch: # Reorder by one sequence field
 sequence_number:
 bit_offset: 336 # Sequence starts at bit 336
 bit_width: 32 # 32-bit sequence field
 packets_per_batch: 1024 # 1024 packets per output buffer

 Imagine a field service technician repairing a dishwasher. Their hands are full, the next appointment is approaching, and a missed step could mean another visit. An AI assistant watching the repair could flag a skipped step while there is still time to act. But an assistant that repeats instructions or interrupts every routine action could make the job harder and risk frontline teams not using it.

 BLIP-Live tackles getting the balance of timely help and restraint just right. BLIP-Live is a general model that can support any workflow where a live agent helps people in the moment, across consumer, enterprise, and industry settings.This research explores how proactive video agents can watch an unfolding task, step in at critical moments, and stay quiet during routine work, moving businesses closer to AI assistance that feels like a helpful teammate rather than a distraction.

 Why Proactive AI Needs Restraint in the Real World

 The potential for proactive assistance extends from workplace training and equipment maintenance to everyday tasks such as setting up an appliance or following a recipe. Guidance becomes more accessible when people can keep their attention on the task instead of stopping to search for instructions. This is a core pillar of Salesforce AI Research’s Ambient Intelligence vision, bringing context-aware help directly into ongoing work—powered by live video agents like BLIP-Live that spot issues proactively, and Interactive Visual Gym , which tests and refines those models in simulation before they reach frontline work.

 Video agents today combine live camera and voice interaction with knowledge grounded in product data to help humans through complex procedures. Understanding the product and the current scene provides the foundation for relevant guidance. BLIP-Live addresses a complementary research question: when should a visual assistant speak? Real proactiveness demands restraint: to be genuinely useful in the field, an assistant has to weigh whether its next message is worth interrupting someone’s focus.

 Consider a possible field service workflow. A technician is replacing a component and moves toward the next step before completing a required check. A visual assistant could offer a short reminder, then remain quiet as the technician completes the check. Repeating the same reminder throughout the repair would consume attention without adding value.

 Teaching AI when silence helps

 Recognizing what is happening in a video is only part of the problem. An assistant also needs to distinguish a new opportunity to help from an activity it has already addressed.

 BLIP-Live explores building large scale training data that includes both useful response moments and silence periods when the agent should continue observing quietly. This includes what happens after a response: if nothing relevant has changed, the assistant should learn that another message may be unnecessary.

 We also investigate how Agent Memory is important to help reduce repetition. The broader lesson is straightforward: an assistant needs to learn how to participate in an ongoing activity, including when to wait. BLIP-Live leverages these learning techniques to build multiple state-of-the-art video agents for exhibiting strong ambient intelligence.

 Measuring useful help, including its cost

 An evaluation that rewards only catching events can favor an assistant that speaks constantly. That creates a gap between a good score and a good experience.

 Our evaluation combines timely coverage with the share of responses that receive credit for addressing an annotated opportunity. Repeated and out-of-window messages count toward the total responses, so unnecessary speech carries a cost. An assistant that never speaks receives no credit either. The goal is to balance timely help with restraint.

 In one evaluation across 326 videos filmed from a person’s visual perspective (ego-centric), our BLIP-Live helped reduce unnecessary responses of state-of-the-art models by over 50%. Experiments also indicated clear human preference for our BLIP-Live responses. 

 What this means for business adoption

 For business leaders the need is clear: help employees resolve issues on the first visit, reduce avoidable rework, and make expertise available during critical moments without distraction. BLIP-Live solves for these needs by ensuring the decision of when to speak is part of the intelligence itself—delivering accurate, selective guidance at the exact right moment, especially for people navigating unfamiliar procedures. Next the AI research team behind this technology will be validating the right balance in live workflows, measuring both task outcomes and the experience of the people receiving help.
