 Preemptible compute is now generally available on Together GPU Clusters, on Kubernetes and Slurm clusters in all regions. Preemptible nodes give teams a lower-cost way to run interruption-tolerant work — short experiments, inference bursts, batch jobs — on the same GPU infrastructure they already use, billed sub-hourly at a flat 50% of the on-demand rate. You can add preemptible capacity to a new or existing cluster starting today.

 How preemptible compute works
 Preemptible compute adds a second compute type to Together GPU Clusters. Standard nodes are fulfilled synchronously and are never preempted. Preemptible nodes use the same NVIDIA accelerated compute, draw from un-used capacity, and can be reclaimed when that capacity is needed elsewhere.
 Preemptible nodes are billed at a flat 50% of the on-demand rate. The rate remains fixed rather than moving with a spot market.
 When a node is reclaimed, the cluster follows a five-minute drain (maximum 5 minutes) sequence:
 T+0 — the node is cordoned, a TogetherPreemptionNotified Kubernetes event fires, and your pods receive SIGTERM.
 T+0 to T+5:00 — your workload has up to five minutes ( terminationGracePeriodSeconds ) to checkpoint and exit.
 T+5:00 — the node is removed.
 The cluster retains its preemptible target after the node is removed and automatically refills toward that target as capacity becomes available. You do not need to request replacement capacity.
 Billing is sub-hourly, with usage metered every one to two minutes. A node that runs for 12 minutes is billed for approximately 12 minutes.
 Preemptible nodes are not a separate cluster type. They join your existing cluster, labeled together.ai/compute-class=preemptible , so interruptible workers run on discounted capacity while coordinators, login pods, and serving replicas stay on standard nodes.
 Where preemptible compute fits
 The five-minute reclamation sequence makes recovery behavior the deciding factor. Work that can resume, retry, or requeue can use preemptible capacity while critical components should remain on standard nodes.
 Short experiments. Ablations, config sweeps, quick fine-tunes, evals, distillation, and batch inference are good candidates when the work can resume from a checkpoint or retry after a worker disappears. Frameworks such as Ray Train and PyTorch Lightning resume from the latest checkpoint without intervention. As a reference point, a full weight model checkpoint for GLM5.3 Flash, 321B parameter model is roughly 3.5TB which would take between 22s to 4mins to write to a parallel filesystem even at degraded performance. Leaving headroom inside the five-minute drain window.
 Temporary bursts. When batch or internal inference demand spikes, add cheaper preemptible nodes to absorb the burst and non-critical workloads instead of holding standard capacity sized for your peak or risking burst standard compute availability. Interrupted requests requeue; user-facing replicas remain on standard nodes.
 The common thread: work that is chunked, checkpointed, or retryable. Multi-day runs without checkpointing and strict-SLO serving with no fallback are not a fit.
 Together has a strong culture dogfooding , several internal research, dev/test and non-critical production workloads at Together are based on our preemptible compute based GPU clusters enabling us to leverage our spare capacity across product teams.
 Before you enable it
 Treat preemptible nodes as ephemeral. There is no minimum node lifetime, so checkpoint frequently and plan for capacity to disappear at any time.
 Keep critical components on standard nodes. Coordinators, login pods, and user-facing replicas belong to guaranteed capacity — use the compute-class label to enforce it.
 Use the drain window. Set terminationGracePeriodSeconds to as much as 300 seconds, checkpoint when the pod receives SIGTERM, and exit before the node is removed.
 A cluster can run entirely on preemptible nodes. Keep at least one standard node if you need your shared storage reachable at all times. Nodes can't be converted between compute types in place.

 After the workload is prepared for preemption, setup has two parts: set the preemptible target and schedule eligible work onto the labeled nodes.
 You can set the target when creating a cluster or on a cluster that is already running, using the Together Cloud console, CLI, or API.  Amazon Robotics operates over a million robots across more than 300 warehouses worldwide. Inside a single warehouse, thousands of robots share the floor, moving inventory for order fulfillment and stock replenishment. Finding efficient, collision-free robot paths to their destinations is therefore essential for smooth operations.
 Let us formulate the underlying multiagent pathfinding (MAPF) problem a bit more precisely. Consider a typical warehouse layout as a W x H rectangular grid. At each time step, a robot can make one of five choices: move north, east, west, or south, or wait. These choices must be coordinated to avoid collisions.
 Over a planning horizon of T time steps, finding optimal robot paths involves approximately n = 5 WHT binary decision variables, giving 2 n possible combinations before accounting for feasibility constraints. Finding optimal paths that minimize the total move cost or arrival time without collisions is NP hard in general and can become computationally intractable even for moderate numbers of robots.

 More than a million Amazon Robotics robots in more than 300 fulfillment centers must coordinate their movements in real time.

 Amazon Robotics robots coordinating their movements in real time.

 In a paper we presented at this year’s International Conference on Machine Learning ( ICML ), we show that physics offers a way around this intractability: plan trajectories as if the robots were particles of gas. Modeling the robots as a cold, lazy gas, whose particles spend only the minimum effort to reach their destinations, yields optimal, minimum-energy paths. This is equivalent to treating trajectory planning as an optimal-transport problem. It yields optimal paths, but computing them may take too long for real-time deployment.
 Modeling the fleet as a hot gas, whose particles jitter randomly along the way, yields trajectories that are computationally faster but potentially suboptimal. As the temperature drops to zero, however, the jittery hot-gas paths settle onto the lazy-gas ones. Our approach uses a Schrödinger bridge — a hot-gas model — to efficiently approximate the solution to an optimal-transport problem — the cold-gas model.
 Optimal transport 

 To develop the formal connection, let us travel back to 1781 in France to meet Gaspard Monge, the father of differential geometry. Monge was looking for the most efficient way to transport excavated dirt to military fortifications. His studies gave rise to the optimal-transport problem, which asks how to transport resources from a set of sources to a set of destinations while minimizing cost, such as distance traveled or work done.
 Monge’s sources were the locations containing dirt. His formulation required all mass at each source location to be transported to one destination, producing a deterministic transport map. Although intuitive, Monge’s original formulation is a difficult nonlinear optimization problem with a potential combinatorial explosion in discrete settings. Finding an optimal map is therefore technically challenging and, in some settings, impossible.
 Roughly 150 years later, Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich relaxed this restriction by allowing mass from one source to be split among multiple destinations. His key insight was that this relaxation converts a difficult optimization over transport maps into a linear program , a class of problems for which highly efficient computer algorithms were subsequently developed. Kantorovich received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his contributions to the theory of optimum allocation of resources.
 Optimal transport connects two mass distributions, also known as marginals, through minimum-cost transport plans. This two-marginal formulation represents only the initial and final configurations and the cost of transporting between them, not the intermediate paths or collisions along those paths. Formulating MAPF as optimal transport therefore requires a multistage approach, because robots generally cannot move from their starting locations to their destinations in a single time step; their locations must also be represented at every intermediate time.
 We develop this connection in detail in our paper, using multimarginal optimal transport (MMOT), which extends optimal transport to a sequence of marginals. In other words, the additional marginals represent robot locations at intermediate times in MAPF, allowing motion to be modeled across both space and time.
 In the case of anonymous MAPF problems, where any robot can go to any destination, a key result we prove in the paper is the following:

 The MMOT formulation solves the combinatorial anonymous MAPF problem to optimality with a runtime of O(n 1.68 ) seconds in practice and with a theoretical upper bound of roughly O(n 3 ). Formally speaking, we achieve this by carefully preserving the total unimodularity of the underlying constraint matrices.

 We next provide a brief explanation of total unimodularity (TU). Consider the decision variables in a transport plan as a vector or a point in a high-dimensional space, with each coordinate specifying how much mass is transported from one grid cell to another in a single time step. The set of all feasible plans forms a geometric object called a polyhedron. Total unimodularity guarantees that every corner of this polyhedron has integer coordinates. Since a linear objective attains its optimum at a corner of the corresponding feasible polyhedron, a linear program (LP) provides optimal and integer solutions in polynomial time. Without total unimodularity, a linear program may return a fractional solution; for example, it may send half of a robot along one route and half along another.
 Going back to the scale of Amazon warehouses, a square 150 × 150 grid with 1 , 125 robots has 3 . 4 million decision variables over a planning horizon of T = 30. Our TU-preserving MMOT provides optimal robot trajectories from their starting locations to their destinations in 478 seconds. By contrast, the non-TU, NP-hard formulation often fails to find even a single feasible solution in 12 hours.
 Finding an optimal solution in 478 seconds, however, might still be impractical. A natural question is whether we can improve the solver’s runtime by accepting a slightly suboptimal solution. It is not generally clear how to formulate such a tradeoff in clean technical terms, which leads to our next contribution.
 Gases hot and cold

Schrödinger asked, Given the observed distributions of gas particles at two different times, what was the most likely evolution between them?

Instead of selecting a deterministic set of paths, the bridge casts robot transport as a shadow , i.e., the most likely way for the robot mass to flow from one state to another.

 How It Works in Practice

 The platform already powers an internal iFood security product that scans the 15,000 repositories for vulnerable dependencies and attempts to fix them. 

 The migration had a direct impact: “With our platform, the accuracy of those changes, those fixes, more than doubled,” says Coelho. 

 This use case illustrates the idea behind the platform: the security team didn’t need to build its own agentic runtime or become experts in agent orchestration. It needed to call an API. 

 Asynchronous execution gives developers their time back 

 The team emphasizes what asynchronous execution frees developers to do, rather than replacement. 

 Because the task is delegated and runs without supervision, the developer doesn’t have to stay focused on monitoring it. 

 “While they’re in a meeting, they can launch a code fix, a performance analysis, or another task that’s a little more routine—something they can delegate without having to monitor it at that moment.” 

 Routine work no longer competes for the team’s attention. That leaves time for problems that require judgment. 

 “You can unlock those productivity gains without necessarily having to monitor the work as it happens.” —Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer, iFood

 Modeled on the APIs that inspired it 

 The Remote Agents Platform was designed as an internal layer with similarities to the way OpenAI makes this type of task available to external customers. 

 “The Remote Agents Platform drew a lot of inspiration from OpenAI’s APIs, both the Batch API and the Agents API, which is an even more recent OpenAI product. We built a platform that resembles those products, but is internal to iFood.” 

 The inspiration went beyond the concept. It extends to the design of the interfaces, the organization of resources, and the approach to asynchronous execution with results retrieved later. 

 “These products directly inspired how we built our solution.” —Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer, iFood

 Results at a glance 

 The platform has completed more than 25,000 tasks, consuming more than 27 billion tokens, at an average cost of US$0.04 per run. 

 After migrating to the Remote Agents Platform, the internal security platform more than doubled both the accuracy of its fixes and the number of vulnerabilities resolved. 

 Coverage extends across iFood’s more than 15,000 code repositories. 

 All runs use GPT‑5.6 Luna, chosen for its balance of intelligence, efficiency, and speed. 

 Structured Outputs ensures that each session returns a result that follows a predefined contract, whether positive or negative. 

 Product teams have moved from building fragmented solutions to delegating agentic execution through a REST API. 

 Lessons for leaders 

 The bottleneck is often the runtime, not the use case. Several teams already knew what they wanted to automate; they lacked an environment in which to run it. 

 Combine deterministic steps with agentic execution. Cloning, publishing, and making artifacts available don’t require a model; the complex task in between does. 

 Treat cost per run as an architectural decision. At scale, the cost per task determines what is feasible to automate. 

 Require outputs to follow a contract. Without a structured response, there’s no way to know at scale whether the agent achieved its objective. 

 Asynchronous execution is the real productivity gain. The value lies in not having to monitor the work, rather than completing it faster while watching over it. 

 Tips 

 Expose agentic capabilities through an internal API so other teams can use them without rebuilding the infrastructure. 

 Isolate each run in its own sandbox, with your ecosystem’s toolchains already installed. 

 Choose a model by considering intelligence, cost, and speed together, rather than a single benchmark. 

 Use structured outputs to classify success and failure automatically. 

 Start with an internal user with a clear pain point and high volume—at iFood, that was the security platform. 

 Next steps 

 The team plans to bring the platform to many more use cases across iFood, expanding the range of internal products that delegate agentic execution to it—with cost per task determining whether that expansion can be sustained.
