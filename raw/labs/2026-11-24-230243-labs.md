For Everstone Studio, bringing more realistic lighting and materials to this wuxia world means keeping its aesthetics, recognizable characters, and carefully composed atmosphere intact.

 The team’s ongoing work with NVIDIA DLSS and Streamline provided a foundation for integrating DLSS 5. Artists and engineers evaluated its lighting and material enhancements against the game’s art direction, balancing visual gains with additional performance overhead.

 We sat down with art director Muzi Li and engine tech lead Cory Zhou to discuss the studio’s integration approach, preserving character identity, the changes that stood out in Jiangnan, and why developers should account for DLSS 5 early in their rendering budgets.

 What made DLSS 5 a fit for Where Winds Meet ? 

 The world of Where Winds Meet encompasses landscapes and towns, as well as countless details in how people interact with their surroundings. Players might be gazing into the distance from a mountain one moment, then walking down a bustling street the next. We want these scenes to be more than beautiful; we want players to feel their different weather conditions, times of day, and moods.

 What attracts us to DLSS 5 is the room it gives us to explore lighting and materials further. It builds on the image already produced by the game engine, using neural rendering to enhance lighting and material details.

 Our goal, isn’t to pursue greater visual impact for its own sake. It’s to make these details work in harmony with the original art direction: distant landscapes should retain their poetic atmosphere, while nearby buildings and characters should have a believable sense of texture. 

 What were your art team’s first thoughts of DLSS 5? 

 Our first impression was that the interplay of light and shadow had gained greater depth. When sunlight falls on buildings, fabrics, and characters, the result feels more than just a sharper image. DLSS 5 identifies and tones down overly bright highlights while bringing out more detail in those areas. It also fills in ambient-light details and subtle highlights in the shadows, and recognizes the properties of materials. 

 How did you integrate DLSS 5 into your pipeline? 

 We have consistently integrated the latest DLSS technologies and kept our NVIDIA Streamline integration up to date. As a result, our rendering pipeline already had strong support for DLSS, providing a solid foundation for adopting DLSS 5. This ongoing investment made the upgrade a natural extension of our existing technology roadmap and helped ensure a smooth integration process.

 How did artists and engineers use DLSS 5 controls to preserve art direction across characters, materials, and lighting? 

 In any given scene, we look at whether characters remain recognizable, whether materials such as fabric, leather, and metal are still visually distinct, and whether changes in lighting and shadow disrupt the scene’s original mood.

 NVIDIA provides developers with options for model selection and controls over aspects such as structural and tonal strength. For us, artists and engineers need to communicate and iterate based on what we see on screen. One setting might make the environment more striking, but if faces or the overall color palette drift away from the intended look, we need to find a different balance.

 We want to strike the right balance between visual gains, performance cost, and player preferences, while giving players choices wherever possible.

 How did you evaluate DLSS 5 image quality and performance? 

 Our evaluation focuses on three key areas: visual quality, performance overhead, and alignment with the game’s artistic direction. Our testing to date indicates that enabling DLSS 5 introduces additional performance overhead, so we assess that cost alongside the visual improvements it delivers.

 In developing Where Winds Meet , we have consistently explored different graphics technologies to support a wide range of player preferences. In our internal playtesting, DLSS 5 delivered a distinct visual experience, enhancing the overall presentation of our environments and their sense of immersion.

 Based on these findings, we plan to give players the option to enable or disable DLSS 5. Our approach to visual tuning will be guided by the art team’s creative vision, including selecting the DLSS 5 model that best matches the intended aesthetic. We also aim to make relevant visual controls available to players wherever possible, allowing them to tailor the image to their preferences.

 On balance, our evaluation has been positive, taking into account the visual improvements, performance overhead, and flexibility offered to players.

 How does DLSS 5 affect characters? 

 What matters is whether characters feel more naturally part of the world while retaining their own distinctive presence.

 For example, when a character walks from sunlight into the shade beneath an eave, changes in lighting and materials can help them feel more naturally grounded in the environment. The way different clothing materials respond to light can also give them a stronger presence. At the same time, we pay close attention to whether faces and costumes remain recognizable.

 Which scene best demonstrates the improvements? 

 We find that Jiangnan is where DLSS 5 makes the biggest visual improvement. \When adjusting the settings, we use DLSS 5 to soften highlights in the original scene lighting that can appear too harsh, while adding a greater sense of depth between buildings. Many buildings in Jiangnan feature wood and tiled roofs; DLSS 5 can fill in lifelike wood and roof-tile details.

 As for the image that best showcases the effect, it will reveal itself during each player’s own journey: perhaps at a street corner just after the rain has cleared, or at an old ferry crossing bathed in the setting sun—a quiet moment that makes you stop and linger.

 What advice would you give other developers adopting DLSS 5? 

 First, account for DLSS 5 performance requirements early in your rendering budget. For projects targeting high frame rates, reserve sufficient performance headroom and evaluate the balance between visual benefits and performance cost in representative gameplay scenarios.

 Second, work closely with the NVIDIA team throughout integration and tuning to understand the technology’s capabilities and available controls. Align that work with your project’s artistic direction and performance targets.

 This capacity has been a long time in the making. Quantum computing emerged in the 1960s and ’70s from research studying the physics underlying computation itself. Both classical and quantum computers represent a unit of information as a string of binary digits. Systems computing classical information can only manipulate a single binary string at a time, while systems capable of computing quantum information assign amplitudes to every possible bitstring at once, and then use constructive and destructive interference to change the value of these amplitudes. At the end of the computation, called a quantum circuit, these amplitudes turn into probabilities used to determine a single bitstring output.

 This form of computation provides a mathematical framework to create algorithms that would otherwise require exponentially more classical compute resources to tackle. Quantum algorithms have widespread applicability for problems across life sciences, energy, chemistry, machine learning, optimization and logistics, and more.

 Market data arrives as a stream of trades and quotes: every execution, bid, and ask, timestamped to the microsecond. Preparing this data for AI-based market forecasting requires turning price and volume histories into consistent inputs for model training and inference. A common first step is aggregating the data into bars—one row per instrument per time unit—with price, volume, and other statistics. Neural networks can learn patterns from these histories across instruments and forecast horizons.

 NVIDIA Megatron Core is an open-source library of GPU-optimized components for training transformer models at scale. Megatron-LM is a GitHub repository that combines that library with preconfigured training scripts. A preprocessing script turns intraday market bars into model-ready tensors, and a dataset provider feeds them into Megatron’s distributed training loop.

 This post shows how to preprocess market-bar data and train a model with Megatron-LM to forecast returns and trading volume across multiple horizons.

 Forecast returns and trading volume 

 Price and volume remain continuous features. A learned patch embedding turns each group of observations into a model input without converting market values into text.

 A common data interface supports training a shared forecasting model on histories from many instruments. Each training window belongs to one instrument and includes its identifier, so the model can learn instrument-specific behavior within shared parameters. You can also explore longer contexts, different sampling frequencies, and additional features through the same interface.

 The example forecasts returns and trading volume at several horizons from the same context. Quantitative researchers can use these outputs to study short-term market behavior, trading activity, or execution timing. Point and interval forecasts address different research questions while using the same input representation.

 You can change the model architecture, optimizer, or training duration while reusing a prepared dataset. Preprocessing records the patch size, context length, and forecast horizons in the saved metadata, making the data configuration an explicit part of each experiment.

 A consistent representation also separates the source feed from the model. Data from a new vendor or sampling scheme can follow the same row schema, while the dataset provider supplies the same batch fields. Figure 1 shows the pipeline from market bars to training.

 From price and volume to model-ready sequences 

 The input is a directory of Parquet files with one row per bar and Symbol, Date, Timestamp, Close, and Volume columns, plus optional numeric feature columns. Bars can come from different vendors and sampling schemes, such as one-minute or sub-minute aggregates of tick data. Keep each instrument’s trading day in one file and the files in chronological order. The script sorts each instrument’s daily observations by timestamp. The script derives within-day log returns and log volumes itself, so the overnight gap never enters the inputs. The preprocessor treats rows as observation steps and leaves gaps unchanged. Align the data upstream if you need a regular time grid.

 preprocessing.py groups consecutive bars for one instrument on one trading day into patches—the model’s tokens—and slides fixed-length context windows over them.

 Each patch retains the features of its constituent rows. For example, eight bars per patch and 32 patches per window provide 256 observations in a sequence of 32 model positions. An instrument ID identifies the series, and each patch carries its position within the trading day (Figure 2).

 Defining forecast targets 

 Each window includes future targets for every context position, so one forward pass produces forecasts throughout the context rather than only at the end. A causal forecasting model uses only the history available at each position.

 Forecast horizons can be defined in observation steps or patches.  Efficient data loading for distributed training 

 The data pipeline consists of two stages: preprocessing and data loading. Preprocessing builds the features, patches, windows, and targets offline, then writes them as PyTorch tensor files: train.pt , val.pt , test.pt , and meta.pt . Training reads these saved tensors, keeping Parquet parsing and target construction outside the training loop.

 Prepare once, reuse across runs 

 During preprocessing, the script handles files in order and uses array operations for regular bar layouts, with a per-instrument path for irregular observations. It computes each instrument’s target sequence once and reuses it across overlapping windows. Temporary memory-mapped arrays hold the completed split tensors before they are saved to disk.

 The saved metadata records instrument mappings, feature names, patch and context lengths, forecast horizons, and normalization statistics. Model and dataset settings can be checked against the same description before training.

 NVIDIA Real-Time BVH Viewer 1.0 brings live acceleration structure inspection to D3D12 and Vulkan 

 Figure 4. NVIDIA Real-Time BVH Viewer displays a running scene’s ray tracing geometry in a separate window, with controls for inspecting acceleration structures 

 NVIDIA Real-Time BVH Viewer 1.0 is now available on GitHub . The utility shows ray tracing acceleration structures while a game or renderer is running. It helps developers understand TLAS and BLAS contents, validate acceleration structure updates, and debug geometry-related ray tracing issues.

 The viewer integrates at the graphics API level through Direct3D 12 and Vulkan libraries. Developers send acceleration structure builds, copies, queue submissions, and frame boundaries to the viewer, which displays the results in a separate window. Separate windows with its own camera allows it to inspect TLAS independently from the in-game camera.

 To inspect an existing title without changing its code, developers can use the included sampler. It attaches the viewer to a running D3D12 or Vulkan process and serves as an integration sample.

 Release highlights include:

 Visualization modes for primitive, geometry, instance, and cluster IDs, BLAS addresses, instance masks and flags, and acceleration structure update state

 Per-pixel trace time heatmaps that show which parts of a scene cost the most to trace

 Support for various geometry filters: flags, types and ray culling

 Support for Clustered geometry in D3D12

 Vendor-agnostic integration with optional vendor-specific paths, plus builds for Windows x64 and RTX Spark

 RTX Path Tracing 1.9 adds an introductory sample and SHaRC integration 

 Figure 5. Step 8 of the RTX Path Tracing Intro Sample combines ReSTIR DI, SHaRC, and DLSS Ray Reconstruction in a nighttime café scene 

 NVIDIA RTX Path Tracing 1.9 gives developers a more accessible starting point for adding ray-traced lighting and studying a real-time path tracing pipeline.

 The intro sample uses a hybrid deferred renderer to introduce lighting features one at a time across nine selectable steps. Developers can progress from image-based lighting and screen-space effects to ReSTIR DI, path-traced indirect lighting, and SHaRC radiance caching.

 SHaRC is also integrated into the main real-time path tracer and enabled by default, providing additional indirect-lighting information to DLSS Ray Reconstruction. Other updates include:

 RTX Spark support

 Performance optimizations including real-time subsampling, overlapping DLSS Ray Reconstruction with the next frame’s path tracing, and GPU-side acceleration-structure instance generation

 Expanded NEE-AT light sampling support for up to two million lights and clustering of emissive triangles into light levels of detail

 A new design document describing the real-time path tracing pipeline, plus updated dependencies, fixes, and rendering improvements
