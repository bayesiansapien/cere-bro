 Model Information

 Description
 Gemini 4 is the next generation in the Gemini series, a suite of highly-capable, natively multimodal, reasoning models. Gemini 4 Argon delivers frontier performance to help in complex workflows across real-world software engineering, enterprise knowledge work like legal and finance, and cybersecurity defense.

 Model dependencies
 Gemini 4 Argon, a new frontier model, is not a modification or a fine-tune of a prior model.

 Inputs
 Text strings (e.g., a question, a prompt, document(s) to be summarized), images, audio, and video files, with a combined input-output token context window of up to 1M.

 Outputs
 Text, with up to a 1M token input + output.

 Architecture
 Gemini 4 Argon is a large scale sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) ( Clark et al., 2022 ; Du et al., 2021 ; Fedus et al., 2021 ; Jiang et al., 2024 , Lepikhin et al., 2020 ; Riquelme et al., 2021 ; Roller et al., 2021 ; Shazeer et al., 2017 ) transformer-based model ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) with native multimodal support for text, vision, and audio inputs.

 Model Data

 Training Dataset
 The pre-training dataset was a large-scale, diverse collection of data encompassing a wide range of domains and modalities, which included publicly-available web-documents, text, code, images, audio (including speech and other audio types) and video. The post-training dataset included different types of instruction tuning data, reinforcement learning data, and human-preference data. Gemini 4 Argon was trained using reinforcement learning techniques that can leverage multi-step reasoning, problem-solving and theorem-proving data.
 The training dataset also includes: publicly available datasets that are readily downloadable; data obtained by crawlers; licensed data accessed via commercial agreements; user data (i.e., data collected from users of Google products and services to train models, along with user interactions with the model) in accordance with Google’s relevant terms of service, privacy policy, service-specific policies, and pursuant to user controls, where appropriate; other datasets that Google acquires or generates in the course of its business operations, or directly from its workforce; and generated synthetic data.

 Training Data Processing
 Data filtering and preprocessing included techniques such as deduplication, honoring robots.txt, safety filtering in-line with Google's commitment to advancing AI safely and responsibly , and quality filtering to mitigate risks and improve training data reliability. This process involves, on a case-by-case basis, filtering harmful content, text, and other modalities, including filtering content that is pornographic, violent, or violative of child sexual abuse material (CSAM) laws.

 Implementation and Sustainability

 Hardware
 Gemini 4 Argon was trained using Google’s Tensor Processing Units (TPUs). TPUs are specifically designed to handle the massive computations involved in training LLMs and can speed up training considerably compared to CPUs. TPUs often come with large amounts of high-bandwidth memory, allowing for the handling of large models and batch sizes during training, which can lead to better model quality. TPU Pods (large clusters of TPUs) also provide a scalable solution for handling the growing complexity of large foundation models. Training can be distributed across multiple TPU devices for faster and more efficient processing.
 The efficiencies gained through the use of TPUs are aligned with Google's commitment to operate sustainably .

 Software
 Training was done using JAX and ML Pathways .

 Build long-horizon agents with Grok 4.7 on Amazon Bedrock

 SpaceXAI’s Grok 4.7 is now available on Amazon Bedrock, which offers a choice of foundation models (FMs) from multiple providers. According to SpaceXAI, Grok 4.7 is trained to work longer on more difficult tasks and knows when to check its own work. That matters most for agents, which carry a task through many steps and must verify what they find along the way.

 An agent that works this way has to do more than look things up. It has to form a candidate answer, check it against what it has learned, and refine it until it fits. Grok 4.7 exposes a reasoning effort setting that controls how much deliberation happens before each response, and for agent workloads a practical question is which level to run.

 In this post, we build a tool-calling agent on Grok 4.7 and evaluate it on three tasks that require search rather than lookup. The tasks are diagnosing a two-service outage from its alerts, scheduling maintenance under interacting constraints, and choosing an optimal set of migrations under a budget. Each task has exactly one correct answer, verified by brute force. We ran all three at every reasoning effort level, six times each, for 72 agent runs.

 The agent answered 67 of the 72 runs correctly. At xhigh it answered all 18 correctly, and at low it answered 15 of 18 while using about an eighth of the output tokens. That gives a clear trade-off you can tune per request, and we show how to measure it on your own tasks and how to monitor it in production.

 We report token counts rather than currency throughout. Token prices change, and the counts are what your own costs are derived from, so a measurement expressed in tokens stays valid.

 Design tasks that need reasoning

 Many agent evaluations test diligence: read a record, filter it, add it up. Those tasks are worth having, but to see what reasoning effort contributes, a task has to require search: the answer can’t be read off the data, it has to be proposed and then checked.

 We built a synthetic environment of 20 services, exposed through seven tools, and three tasks on top of it. Every task has exactly one correct answer, and each is built so that a quick heuristic is not enough to find it.

 Four practices keep a measurement like this trustworthy, and they apply to most agent evaluations.

 State every rule the grader relies on. If the prompt leaves an edge case open, a reasonable answer can be scored as wrong, and the evaluation ends up measuring the specification rather than the model. The diagnosis prompt, for example, says explicitly that a failed service raises no alert of its own.

 Prove each task has one answer. Each environment is small enough to enumerate, so we checked every candidate answer before running anything. For the schedule, that is a few lines:

 import itertools

ALL_SLOTS = sorted(set(itertools.permutations([1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4])))

def valid_schedules(services, constraints):
 return [s for slots in ALL_SLOTS
 if satisfies(s := dict(zip(services, slots)), constraints)]

assert len(valid_schedules(SERVICES, CONSTRAINTS)) == 1 # exactly one answer
assert len(valid_schedules(SERVICES, CONSTRAINTS_WITHOUT_TEAM_RULE)) > 1 # the rule matters 

 Grade a required final line, not the whole response. Each prompt asks the agent to end with a line such as FINAL: auth, checkout . The grader reads only that line, so a response that discusses other candidates while explaining its reasoning is graded on its conclusion:

 def final_line(answer):
 lines = [l for l in (answer or "").splitlines() if "FINAL" in l.upper()]
 return lines[-1] if lines else None 

 Track format separately from correctness. A missing final line is a different signal from an incorrect one. In our runs, all 72 responses included a gradeable final line.

# LFM2.5-24B-A2B-MLX-bf16

LFM2.5-24B-A2B is a reasoning model by [Liquid AI](https://www.liquid.ai/) with 24B total parameters and 2.45B active parameters per token. It always thinks before it answers, has a 128K-token context window, and is built for fast, private agents on-device.

This repository has the bfloat16 MLX export of [LFM2.5-24B-A2B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B) for Apple Silicon. The conversion used mlx-lm 0.32.0 and the source checkpoint at revision [`6b45d2eb`](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B/tree/6b45d2ebfea3fb8b29e10aac18b9890b6cd19c4b). For more information about the model, see the [original model card](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B).

## About this quantization

- The weights are in bfloat16, as in the original checkpoint. No weight is quantized.
- The MoE router gate (`feed_forward.gate`) is stored in float32, so the router computes the expert scores in float32. The change from bfloat16 to float32 keeps the values.
- The expert bias stays in float32, as in the original checkpoint.

We compared each export with the original checkpoint, run in float32 with Transformers 5.12.1. The comparison uses the next-token distributions at 11,684 token positions in 28 text sequences. Top-1 agreement is the fraction of positions where the export and the reference give the same most likely next token. Mean KL is the mean of KL(reference ‖ export) over all positions, in nats. The reference distribution is the first argument. Higher agreement and lower KL mean that the export is closer to the original model. This check measures agreement with the original model. It does not measure task accuracy.

To check the conversion, we also ran the bf16 weights in float32 with MLX: the top-1 agreement is 99.97% and the mean KL is 0.0003.

Most of the difference of the bf16 export comes from bfloat16 rounding in the router, which changes some expert choices. Transformers in bfloat16 shows a similar difference.

Size is the total size of the weight files. At run time, the model also needs memory for the context.

## Use with mlx

Use mlx-lm 0.32.0 or later. Earlier versions do not route the experts of this model correctly. These versions load the model without an error, but the outputs are different.

'''bash
pip install -U "mlx-lm>=0.32.0"
'''

'''python
from mlx_lm import generate, load
from mlx_lm.sample_utils import make_logits_processors, make_sampler

model, tokenizer = load("LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B-MLX-bf16")

messages = [{"role": "user", "content": "What is C. elegans?"}]
prompt = tokenizer.apply_chat_template(messages, add_generation_prompt=True)

# Recommended settings from generation_config.json
sampler = make_sampler(temp=0.1, top_k=50)
logits_processors = make_logits_processors(repetition_penalty=1.05)

text = generate(
    model,
    tokenizer,
    prompt=prompt,
    max_tokens=4096,
    sampler=sampler,
    logits_processors=logits_processors,
)
# The output is the reasoning, then "</think>", then the answer.
reasoning, _, answer = text.partition("</think>")
print(answer.strip())
'''

LFM2.5-24B-A2B always thinks before it answers. The chat template adds `<think>` at the end of the generation prompt, so the output starts with the reasoning. The reasoning ends with `</think>`, and the answer comes after it. In a multi-turn chat, the template removes the reasoning of the assistant turns before the last user message. Reasoning can use many tokens, so set `max_tokens` to a high value.

This is the prompt that `apply_chat_template` makes for the example above:

'''
<|startoftext|><|im_start|>user
What is C. elegans?<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<think>
'''

See the [Chat Template documentation](https://docs.liquid.ai/lfm/key-concepts/chat-template) for details.

## Tool use

Give the tools to `apply_chat_template` with `tools=...`. The template writes them into the system prompt as `List of tools: [...]`. The model writes tool calls as a Python list between `<|tool_call_start|>` and `<|tool_call_end|>`. Send the result of each call back in a message with the `tool` role. See the [Tool Use documentation](https://docs.liquid.ai/lfm/key-concepts/tool-use) for the full guide.

'''python
import json

from mlx_lm import generate, load
from mlx_lm.sample_utils import make_logits_processors, make_sampler

model, tokenizer = load("LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B-MLX-bf16")
sampler = make_sampler(temp=0.1, top_k=50)
logits_processors = make_logits_processors(repetition_penalty=1.05)


def get_weather(city):
    return {"city": city, "temperature_c": 18, "sky": "clear"}


functions = {"get_weather": get_weather}
tools = [
    {
        "name": "get_weather",
        "description": "Get the current weather in a city.",
        "parameters": {
            "type": "object",
            "properties": {"city": {"type": "string", "description": "City name"}},
            "required": ["city"],
        },
    }
]
messages = [{"role": "user", "content": "What is the weather in Paris?"}]


def chat():
    prompt = tokenizer.apply_chat_template(
        messages, tools=tools, add_generation_prompt=True
    )
    text = generate(
        model,
        tokenizer,
        prompt=prompt,
        max_tokens=4096,
        sampler=sampler,
        logits_processors=logits_processors,
    )
    reasoning, _, answer = text.partition("</think>")
    answer = answer.strip()
    messages.append({"role": "assistant", "content": answer, "reasoning": reasoning})
    return answer


answer = chat()
print(answer)  # <|tool_call_start|>[get_weather(city='Paris')]<|tool_call_end|>

# Run each call and send the result back with the "tool" role.
calls = answer.split("<|tool_call_start|>")[1].split("<|tool_call_end|>")[0]
calls = tokenizer.tool_parser(calls, tools)
for call in calls if isinstance(calls, list) else [calls]:
    result = functions[call["name"]](**call["arguments"])
    messages.append({"role": "tool", "content": json.dumps(result)})

print(chat())
'''

## Serve

`mlx_lm.server` (mlx-lm 0.32.0 or later) gives an OpenAI-compatible API at `http://localhost:8080/v1`:

'''bash
mlx_lm.server --model LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B-MLX-bf16 --temp 0.1 --top-k 50 --max-tokens 8192
'''

Without these options, the server uses greedy decoding and stops after 512 tokens, which is too short for most reasoning. The repetition penalty is a request option:

'''bash
curl http://localhost:8080/v1/chat/completions \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "What is C. elegans?"}], "repetition_penalty": 1.05}'
'''

The server returns the reasoning in the `reasoning` field of the message and tool calls in `tool_calls`. Use the model name `LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B-MLX-bf16` in the client, or leave it out. If the request has a different model name, the server tries to load that model.

You can use the server as the backend of an agent harness, for example Hermes Agent, OpenClaw or Pi. See the [Agent Harnesses guide](https://docs.liquid.ai/examples/agent-harnesses) for the setup. In the harness configuration, set the model name to `LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B-MLX-bf16`, not the short name in the guide examples.

# LFM2.5-24B-A2B

LFM2.5-24B-A2B is a 24B-parameter reasoning model with only 2B active parameters, built for **fast, private agents on-device**. It is the largest and final model in the LFM2.5 family, which now spans two orders of magnitude: from [LFM2.5-230M](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-230M) to LFM2.5-24B-A2B.

- **Best-in-class agent**: Competitive with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, a model with ~50% more total and active parameters, on agentic and tool-use tasks, scoring 48.09 on LiveClaw versus 38.19.
- **Reasoning at MoE speed**: Always thinks before it answers. With only 2B active parameters per token, each reasoning token is cheap.
- **Efficient inference**: Runs up to 1.7× faster than comparable MoEs on-device, with 184 tok/s on an Apple M5 Max and up to 21K output tok/s on a single NVIDIA B300 GPU.

Find more information about LFM2.5-24B-A2B in our [blog post](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm2-5-24b-a2b).

### Tool Use

LFM2.5 supports function calling in four steps:

1. **Function definition**: Provide the list of tools as a JSON object in the system prompt, or use [`tokenizer.apply_chat_template()`](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/en/chat_extras#passing-tools) with `tools=...`.
2. **Function call**: By default, LFM2.5 writes Pythonic function calls (a Python list between `<|tool_call_start|>` and `<|tool_call_end|>` special tokens), as the assistant answer. You can override this behavior by asking the model to output JSON function calls in the system prompt.
3. **Function execution**: Execute the call and return the result with the `tool` role.
4. **Final answer**: LFM2.5 interprets the tool output and returns a plain-text answer addressing the original prompt.

See the [Tool Use documentation](https://docs.liquid.ai/lfm/key-concepts/tool-use) for the full guide. Example:

### Training

![LFM2.5-24B-A2B post-training recipe](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-24B-A2B/resolve/main/figures/LFM2.5-24B-A2B-Training-Recipe.png)

LFM2.5-24B-A2B keeps the LFM2-24B-A2B architecture with two changes: the context window grows from 32K to 131K tokens, and the vocabulary doubles from 65K to 128K tokens through an [in-place tokenizer expansion](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/tokenizer-expansion) for better non-Latin tokenization.

- **Pre-training**: ~33T tokens, up from ~17T.
- **Mid-training**: 2T tokens at 32K context on curated, higher-quality data weighted toward reasoning, math, code, instruction following, and multilingual text, then 300B tokens of long-context data at 131K context. Tool calls and multi-turn trajectories appear in both phases.
- **Post-training**: supervised fine-tuning, teacher specialization, two multi-domain on-policy distillation stages (one non-agentic, one agentic), and agentic reinforcement learning inside popular agent harnesses ([OpenClaw](https://openclaw.ai), [Hermes Agent](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com), [Pi](https://pi.dev), [OpenCode](https://opencode.ai)), so the model learns their tools, system prompts, and interaction patterns.

 Manage private pricing offers for Amazon Bedrock with managed entitlements

 Managing AI model access across dozens or hundreds of AWS accounts creates operational friction when each workload account must subscribe and accept AWS Marketplace Private Pricing Offers (MPPOs) individually. Amazon Bedrock with managed entitlements simplifies multi-account access to third-party AI models so you can subscribe once from a central account and distribute access across your organization.

 In this post, we show you two ways to activate AWS Marketplace Private Pricing Offer (MPPO) entitlements in AWS License Manager. Use the lightweight script when you want a one-time, no-infrastructure fix for received licenses you already trust. Use the CDK automation when you want ongoing event-driven processing that verifies sellers, creates and activates distributed grants.

 Why automate grant activation

 With AWS Marketplace Private Pricing Offers, you can negotiate custom rates for third-party models on Amazon Bedrock. After an eligible private offer is accepted, AWS Marketplace creates a license in AWS License Manager. In AWS Organizations with all features enabled, organization-level grants are auto-accepted but remain Disabled until you activate them.

 Teams often manage this by checking the AWS License Manager console and activating grants manually. Manual checks work, but they add effort and are easy to miss. Until someone activates the grant, negotiated pricing does not apply. The same effort appears as teams adopt new models or renew terms.

 Solution overview

 The sample repository provides two paths:

 Path 1 – The lightweight path is a standalone script that lists received licenses and prints a dry-run plan. It only creates and activates organization-wide grants when you re-run it with --apply --confirm-account-id . It has no seller allow-list, so use it only when you trust every received license in the management account.

 Path 2 – The event-driven path uses AWS Cloud Development Kit (AWS CDK) to deploy ongoing automation in your AWS management account.

 Choose one of the following implementation paths. Use Path 1 for one-time activation without CDK. Use Path 2 for ongoing event-driven automation.

 Path 1: Lightweight script (one-time activation)

 Use this path when you need one-time activation without deploying CDK. The script lists every received license in the management account, so use the dry run as your review gate before applying changes.

 Clone the sample repository locally and move into the project directory. 

 git clone https://github.com/aws-samples/sample-bedrock-managed-entitlements.git
cd sample-bedrock-managed-entitlements 

 Run the lightweight script in dry-run mode. 

 python3 lightweight/distribute_licenses.py 

 Review every listed license and confirm that the ISV Partner issuer and account scope match the private offer you expect.

 Apply the reviewed plan from the same AWS Organizations management account. 

 python3 lightweight/distribute_licenses.py --apply --confirm-account-id ACCOUNT_ID 

 After the grant is active, validate the billing path before you rely on the private offer in production. Billing data in AWS Cost Explorer can lag, so use it to compare the effective charge for covered Amazon Bedrock usage after charges appear. For a repeatable check, adapt the sample scripts/bedrock_discount_check.py utility to match your ISV Partner issuer and confirm the expected discount path.

 Path 2: CDK automation (ongoing activation)

 Use this path when you want new private pricing agreements handled automatically. The CDK stack listens for eligible AWS Marketplace agreement events, checks your seller allowlist, creates distributed grants, and retries activation when License Manager needs more time. The solution contains three components:

 Amazon EventBridge rule : The stack listens for AWS Marketplace agreement events on the default event bus in us-east-1 . The rule matches source aws.agreement-marketplace and detail type Purchase Agreement Created - Acceptor , then invokes the AWS Lambda function.

 AWS Lambda function : The function receives the AWS Marketplace agreement event, verifies the seller allow-list, discovers the license, creates the distributed grant, and requests activation with CreateGrantVersion(Status=ACTIVE) . The function logs the outcome to Amazon CloudWatch Logs for auditability.

 AWS License Manager entitlements : The grant targets your organization, selected organizational units (OUs), or specific accounts. Negotiated pricing applies only after the grant is activated and reaches ACTIVE or WORKFLOW_COMPLETED .

 Mixture-of-Experts kernel engineering on Amazon SageMaker AI

 Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is a sparsely-activated architecture that scales large language model (LLM) capacity while keeping per-token compute low. Because its operations account for a large share of total compute time, inefficiencies there directly raise request latency and cost. Therefore, optimizing MoE operations through kernel engineering offers the largest opportunity for performance gains.

 Fused MoE is a key optimization technique for the MoE architecture. A fused MoE system merges token routing, data shuffling, and specialized network calculations into a single batched operation on the GPU. This replaces executing those routing and expert computations as separate, sequential loop operations. While this provides latency advantages, it also brings complexity in kernel implementations and debugging capability. High GPU utilization for this architecture requires hardware-specific optimizations.

 In this post, we describe the different kernel optimizations we implemented for fused MoE models and show you how you can benefit from this work. We used a representative MoE model as our primary example.

 Swap-AB for WGMMA utilization

 To maximize tensor processing throughput, the NVIDIA Hopper architecture relies on the Warp Group Matrix Multiply Accumulate (WGMMA) instruction protocol, which executes collectively across a cooperative warpgroup of 128 threads. This operand asymmetry is fundamentally an instruction set architecture (ISA)-level property. In the underlying wgmma.mma_async assembly definition, the hardware fixes the matrix row dimension (M) at a constant scale of 64 to define its parallel thread-mapping topology. The column dimension (N) remains highly variable. Operand A strictly maps across this fixed M dimension (using registers or shared memory), and Operand B maps across the variable N dimension (through a shared memory descriptor). These operand slots are therefore structurally non-interchangeable. Consequently, peak hardware tile utilization is governed by these rigid shape encodings, requiring that the M dimension equals or exceeds 64 to prevent compute execution lanes from sitting idle.

 This structural preference stems from how Hopper Tensor Cores partition memory blocks. The hardware enforces a fixed minimum layout shape for the leading dimension of the first operand, locking the block height to exactly 64 (M=64). When a mathematical matrix possesses a large, continuous leading dimension in this primary slot, Hopper’s internal hardware schedulers can cleanly map and distribute data across the physical computing grid.

 This structural asymmetry becomes a severe performance bottleneck during the decode phase of MoE networks. In a standard MoE layer, the linear algebra governing expert computation is formulated using traditional matrix notation:

 A[M,K] x B[K,N] —> C[M,N]

 Where (M) represents the token row count (or batch size), (K) represents the hidden feature dimension, and (N) represents the expansive expert output dimension.

 During the decode phase, the system processes only one token per user sequence at a time. Consequently, the batch space or token row size is profoundly thin, regularly dropping to a profile where (M < 64), and frequently down to a single row (M = 1). Under this standard structural layout:

 Matrix (A) transforms into a thin, fragmented horizontal vector (for example, 1 x 4096).

 Matrix (B) (the core expert weights) remains in a large, dense vertical block (for example, 4096 x 8192).

 Because the row count (M) is tiny, Matrix (A) is physically incapable of filling the hardware’s fixed (64)-row layout bucket for Operand A. When the WGMMA instruction executes over this layout, it leaves a vast portion of the physical Tensor Core capacity completely starved. The silicon spends clock cycles processing empty spatial slots, leading to poor hardware utilization.

 In a standard dense LLM layer, weight matrices are static, unified, and perfectly contiguous. In an MoE layer, however, the workload is fractured across a fleet of independent subnetworks (experts). This creates unique memory and layout bottlenecks that make it hypersensitive to the WGMMA constraints of the Hopper GPU. In a standard dense layer, matrix multiplication multiplies a token vector by a single layer weight matrix. The output dimension (N) is the model’s hidden size (for example, 4096).

 In a Fused MoE kernel, the GPU isn’t only multiplying against one expert. It’s grouping and running math for all active experts simultaneously to avoid launching individual kernels for each expert. For a standard dense layer, N is the hidden size (4,096). For a fused MoE layer, N is the number of experts chosen multiplied by the expert intermediate size. For a model running a topk=8 routine, the effective N dimension inside the fused kernel explodes into tens or hundreds of thousands of parameters. Because the N dimension is so disproportionately large compared to the tiny decode token size (M=1), the layout asymmetry becomes extreme. Transposing the matrices moves this large, combined MoE dimension into the primary slot, unlocking hardware prefetching at a scale that standard dense layers cannot match.

 B T [N,K] x A T [K,M] —> C T [N,M]

This transposition triggers two immediate architectural advantages on the GPU. First, it saturates the physical layout. The expansive expert dimension (N = 8192) is promoted to the first operand position (B T ), and because (N) is large it easily floods the hardware’s mandatory (64)-row threshold. The Tensor Cores then operate at maximum local density with no wasted execution cycles. Second, it activates asynchronous prefetching. Hopper’s dedicated hardware copy engines can prefetch memory addresses asynchronously from shared memory straight into registers before the math execution block fires. Placing the continuous, large weight blocks (B T ) in the leading slot lets these engines trace the data layout smoothly and keep the compute pipeline saturated without CPU intervention.  Earlier this year, IBM announced the world’s first 0.7nm node chip technology . Built on a totally redesigned transistor architecture that layers nanosheets on top of each other, the nanostack overcomes the spatial limitations of packing more transistors into a given footprint. In short, it unlocks the Z axis to reimagine logic scaling for the modern era. The result is nearly 100 billion transistors on a 150mm 2 chip, almost double the density of IBM’s 2nm node chip , with 50% better performance and 70% more energy efficiency, as well as 40% scaling in SRAM memory.

 Now, as the semiconductor industry approaches this major transition in transistor architecture, IBM is announcing plans to make available an early research and development process design kit, or PDK, for the 7-angstrom nanostack technology in the first quarter of 2027. As the industry stands on the verge of this new era in semiconductor design, the PDK is intended to give organizations an early opportunity to explore how this emerging architecture can change their offerings.

 A PDK provides the models, design rules, libraries, and other information engineers need to begin designing chips for a particular manufacturing process. IBM’s planned offering will help semiconductor companies evaluate nanostack technology well before it is ready for widespread adoption. Potential collaborators include fabless chip companies, electronic design automation (EDA) vendors, equipment suppliers, and research organizations interested in developing the technology.

 “We want to invite people to work with us on the early PDK evaluation, and then on codifying the technology together,” said Jingyun Zhang, senior technology enablement manager at IBM Research.

 Preparing for the future, together

 IBM’s nanostack chip architecture represents a substantial departure from today’s nanosheet transistors. Instead of positioning n-type and p-type devices next to each other, the architecture stacks one above the other. This approach changes the physical layout of a circuit and introduces new considerations for resistance, capacitance, thermal behavior , and interactions between the upper and lower devices.

 “The biggest difference is in the device structure,” Zhang said. “When people are trying to design a circuit now, they will need to shift their thinking from how things worked in the previous nanosheet generation, because the layout is changing from 2D to 3D at transistor level for the first time in logic foundry technology.”

 The first version of the PDK is expected to include exemplary standard-cell libraries for logic functions and an exemplary SRAM design. Circuit designers will be able to use these resources to investigate placement and routing, produce early block-level layouts, and evaluate potential density and power-performance characteristics.

 The initial release will not contain every model that a mature commercial PDK would include. Instead, it will advance collaborative research, establishing for the first time a foundation for design-technology co-optimization in a technology’s early phase on an open hardware innovation platform.

 What’s next

 For prospective partners, early access to the nanostack PDK offers more than a preview of a future transistor. It creates an opportunity to investigate how nanostack technology might be optimized for specific products. EDA companies can begin adapting their design tools to the architecture, while equipment makers can study the specialized fabrication challenges associated with its structure.

 Researchers can also explore areas such as parasitic extraction, thermal coupling, and device-to-device interactions as the technology develops.

 “The PDK will help people migrate from nanosheet to nanostack,” Zhang said. “We are standing at a point where we can help the industry get better prepared.”

 Previous shifts — from planar transistors to FinFETs and from FinFETs to nanosheets — required the industry to rethink both fabrication and chip design. By making an early nanostack PDK available, IBM aims to give companies time to begin that process again, this time with an opportunity to help co-define the technology’s design ecosystem from the outset.
