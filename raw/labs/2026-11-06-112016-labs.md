# Molmo2-4B-tb

Molmo2-4B-tb is the high-level model of [ToyBlocks](https://allenai.org/blog/toyblocks), a robot block-stacking
project. It is [Molmo2-4B](https://huggingface.co/allenai/Molmo2-4B) fine-tuned to read a stacking goal, look at the
robot's front camera and choose the next step. It states the goal's progress, names the next step and points to the
block or spot to act on. A low-level model, such as a motion planner or a learned policy, then carries out the step.

Quick links:

* 📝 [ToyBlocks blog post](https://allenai.org/blog/toyblocks)
* 💻 [ToyBlocks code](https://github.com/allenai/toyblocks)
* 🧩 [Base model: Molmo2-4B](https://huggingface.co/allenai/Molmo2-4B)

## Model details

* **Model type:** vision-language model with about 4.5B parameters, running at about 6 Hz.
* **Input:** one 640x360 front-camera image and a text goal.
* **Output:** a short plan, then the action and its target as a Molmo2 point.
* **Actions:** pick, place (on a block or the table), flip (left, right, forward, backward), rotate (cw, ccw) and done.
* **Training:** 6,000 steps at batch size 256 on simulated ToyBlocks three-block stacking episodes. We mix in 25%
  image-pointing data (PixMo-Points, PixMo-Count, CoSyn-Point) to retain Molmo's general pointing ability.
* **Files:** `model.pt` (float32 weights in the native Molmo2 format, not Transformers) and `config.yaml` (full model
  and training configuration).
* **Developed and shared by:** Ai2. **Funded by:** Fund for Science and Technology.
* **Model date:** November 2026. **Language:** English. **Fine-tuned from:**
  [Molmo2-4B](https://huggingface.co/allenai/Molmo2-4B).

## Prompt format

The goal lists the tower from top to bottom:

'''
stack A 4 B. plan. point to target. predict action.
'''

'''
now: A not on 4, 4 on B. next: move A to 4. Do: block A <points coords="1 1 367 623">block A</points> pick
'''

The question can also start with the previous step, in the same format as the answer:

'''
previous action: block A <points coords="1 1 367 623">block A</points> pick. stack A 4 B. plan. point to target. predict action.
'''

Points are in 0–1000 of the image width and height. Each target is 5 px (in a 640x360 frame) below the block's
center, so it points to the center of the block face in front of the camera.

Here is the Molmo2 output when combined with the low level [Maniflow-tb](https://huggingface.co/allenai/maniflow-tb) running on a real robot.

## Technical specifications

The training objective is next-token prediction of the plan, the action and the target point (which block to act
on).

* **Vision encoder:** SigLIP 2 so400m/14 at 378 px, a 27-layer, 1152-wide ViT.
* **Connector:** features from two ViT layers (third- and ninth-from-last), pooled with 2x2 attention pooling
  (mean query), then an MLP projector into the language model.
* **Language model:** Qwen3-4B, dense: 36 layers, width 2560, 32 query heads with 8 key/value heads (grouped-query
  attention, head dim 128), RoPE, RMSNorm with QK-norm and a SwiGLU MLP.

# ManiFlow-tb

ManiFlow-tb is the low-level action model for [ToyBlocks](https://allenai.org/blog/toyblocks), a robot block-stacking
project. It is trained on simulated Franka arm data for manipulating blocks on a table
(pick-and-place and in-place rotation of three blocks).
It is trained with the ManiFlow training code at [allenai/maniflow-tb](https://github.com/allenai/maniflow-tb) and
evaluated through ToyBlocks' policy server.

Quick links:

* 📝 [ToyBlocks blog post](https://allenai.org/blog/toyblocks)
* 💻 [ToyBlocks code](https://github.com/allenai/toyblocks)
* 🛠️ [ManiFlow training code](https://github.com/allenai/maniflow-tb)
* 📊 [ToyBlocks data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/toyblocks-data)

## Model

- Architecture: ManiFlowMultiModal with a DiTX action head (12 layers, 8 heads, width 768), 322.5M parameters,
  stored in bf16 (`safetensors`).
- Inputs: front and gripper RGB images (224 x 224, CLIP ViT-B/16 encoder), a 9-dim robot state, the task name
  (T5-small, `language_mode: action_only`) and a target heatmap token from the high-level policy
  (`target_injection_mode: token`, encoded by the shared RGB encoder, `heatmap_encoder_type: shared_rgb`).
- Outputs: a chunk of 25 executed actions (prediction horizon 50) of 9-dim absolute actions
  (`action_mode: abs`). The last dimension is the play mode (1 play, 0 pause, -1 skill done), because the model was trained
  with `pause_on_attempt: true`. The play mode needs to be fed back to the model when 1 or 0.
- Flow matching with consistency training, 12 integration steps at inference.

## Training

- Data: three ToyBlocks-generated LeRobot datasets of tabletop block manipulation.
- 200,000 steps on 8 GPUs, per-device batch 128, AdamW (lr 1e-4, betas 0.9/0.95, weight decay 1e-3), cosine schedule
  with 500 warmup steps, bf16 mixed precision. Full settings are in `config.yaml`.
- The weights are the online (non-EMA) weights of the final step (200,000).

## Files

The repository mirrors a training run directory, which is the layout ToyBlocks expects:

| file | content |
|---|---|
| `checkpoints/steps_200000_model.safetensors` | model weights |
| `config.yaml` | training config (ToyBlocks reads the model and action settings from it) |
| `stats.json` | normalization statistics of the training data |

## Usage

Download the repository:

'''bash
hf download allenai/maniflow-tb --local-dir maniflow-tb
'''

Evaluate it with ToyBlocks (the server finds `config.yaml` next to the `checkpoints/` directory):

'''bash
python scripts/launch_eval.py --policy maniflow \
  --model-path maniflow-tb/checkpoints/steps_200000_model.safetensors \
  --dataset-path <ToyBlocks eval dataset> --expert-timeout-scale 3.0 ...
'''

Training code: [allenai/maniflow-tb](https://github.com/allenai/maniflow-tb) (`scripts/train_maniflow.py`). ToyBlocks
also uses this code as its `baselines/hvla` package to load the model.

## Evaluation

Closed-loop evaluation in ToyBlocks simulation with its own eval pipeline (`scripts/launch_eval.py --policy maniflow`):

- 128 episodes from a held-out validation set of three-block tabletop tasks, each started from the initial state of a
  recorded episode.
- Oracle high-level policy: the high-level plan comes from the recorded task, so the numbers measure this low-level
  action model alone.
- Success: all goal predicates hold with the blocks settled and the gripper open. An episode ends when the
  high-level plan is complete, on a collision, or at the time limit.
- Time limit: 3x the longest expert demonstration (`--expert-timeout-scale 3.0`, 194 s here). With the default 30 s,
  most episodes time out before the task is finished (26 to 28 of 128 succeed), which measures the limit rather than
  the model.

| run | success | success at episode end | collision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 73/128 (57%) | 64 | 32 |
| 2 | 75/128 (59%) | 67 | 32 |
| 3 | 76/128 (59%) | 66 | 30 |
| 4 | 69/128 (54%) | 63 | 38 |

The four runs use the same episodes and settings; runs 1, 2 and 4 spread the episodes over 7 eval GPUs, run 3 over 1.
They differ because the simulation and batched inference are not
bitwise deterministic. The same episode can flip between success and failure from run to run: two runs agree on
82 to 97 of the 128 episodes.

 What Web Answer does

 Figure 1: Request flow from an agent through the Gateway to Web Answer and back

 Your agent sends a question to your AgentCore Gateway, a capability of Amazon Bedrock AgentCore. The Gateway authenticates the call with AWS Identity and Access Management (AWS IAM) or a JSON Web Token (JWT).  How it differs from Web Search

 Web Answer: Runs the model, the agentic retrieval loop, synthesis, and attribution for you. You get a finished, cited answer. Use Web Answer when the question is a sub-task inside a larger agent and you do not want to own a retrieval pipeline for it.

 Many customers will use both. An agent might use Web Search for broad research tasks where it needs raw results, and Web Answer for specific factual questions where it needs a quick, cited answer.

Artificial Intelligence 

 How Amazon Marketplace evaluates content quality with Amazon SageMaker AI

 A+ Content is the rich media widget on the Amazon product detail page. Through visual assets, brands communicate unique product and brand value to help customers make informed purchase decisions. Selling partners using high-quality A+ Content boost their sales by 8–20 percent compared to those without it. A wide variety of brands, from established brands with large in-house design teams to bootstrapped brands with limited resources, use A+ Content, creating inconsistency in content quality. A typical A+ Content project combines text, images, and video into a cohesive product story. Evaluating each asset in isolation misses the quality that emerges from how those assets work together. When your content spans multiple heterogeneous modules and you need a holistic quality signal rather than per-asset checks, the multi-model fusion approach described in this post can help. In this post, we examine this problem in the context of A+ Content.

 A+ Content provides creative freedom for selling partners to express their authentic selves on their product detail pages, without strict content specifications such as image ratio and text word count.

 Although this flexibility allows compelling brand storytelling, it also creates substantial variation in content composition and quality.

 With quality evaluation and improvement recommendations, brands can transform average content into high-quality content that is more coherent, relevant, and useful. This improved content helps customers make more informed purchasing decisions and build lasting relationships with brands, leading to improved revenue for selling partners.

 To achieve this goal, an evaluation system must assess the complete content experience, provide actionable recommendations beyond numeric scores, and reflect the nuances across product categories. Existing approaches struggle to meet all these requirements simultaneously, so in this post, you will see a new approach using Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to understand and evaluate content holistically, and a diffusion-based generation component that produces improved visual assets from the resulting quality signals.

 In this post, you will learn how to:

 Render complete ecommerce content as a single snapshot and evaluate it holistically with a VLM.

 Combine VLM semantic scores with OCR readability signals in a lightweight CatBoost classifier trained on expert annotations.

 Use SHAP to explain quality decisions and generate actionable improvement recommendations for selling partners.

 Implement the end-to-end pipeline on Amazon SageMaker AI, from data ingestion and annotation through model hosting, explainability, and production monitoring.

 Opportunities and challenges in VLM-based evaluation

 A vision language model (VLM) pairs an image encoder with a large language model (LLM). The encoder, often a vision transformer (ViT) , splits an image into patches and turns them into embeddings, and a small connector maps those embeddings into the language model’s input space ( Flamingo , LLaVA ). Many VLMs reuse the image encoder from CLIP , which learned to match images with their captions. Because VLMs are trained to follow instructions, you can give one an image along with a rubric of evaluation criteria and get back a written assessment without task-specific training. When you need closer agreement with human reviewers, you can fine-tune the model on labeled examples. This makes VLMs a practical starting point for checking product images against your content guidelines at scale.

 Conventional computer vision models can already score individual assets, for example flagging a blurry image or a grammar error in a bullet list. Using a VLM to grade each asset separately adds little beyond that. A product detail page, however, combines images, text, and layout modules into a single story, and its quality depends on how those pieces work together. Our approach renders the full page as a single snapshot and asks the VLM to evaluate it as customers see it.

 Consider a blender page with a hero image, a bulleted feature list highlighting blade speed and capacity, and a lifestyle photo of the blender in a modern kitchen. Checked one by one, each asset passes: the image is sharp, the bullets are grammatical, and the photo is well lit. Viewed together, problems appear. The bullets might advertise a 64-oz pitcher while the lifestyle photo shows a single-serve model, or the lifestyle photo might sit above the hero image so customers see the kitchen before the product. Because an asset’s meaning depends on where it appears and what surrounds it, these issues only show up when the page is evaluated as a whole. The snapshot gives the VLM that complete view. Using it in production adds three requirements: scores must match expert quality standards, evaluation must be accurate and efficient at scale, and each result must explain what to fix.

 As shown in Figure 1, we address these considerations through a VLM-based multi-model fusion architecture designed around adaptability, performance, and explainability. The following sections describe each component.

 Figure 1: Block diagram of the VLM-based multi-model fusion approach, including VLM, OCR, and classifier models

 Adaptive VLM content evaluation with expert annotations

 A pretrained VLM prompted with a rubric scores content from its general knowledge, and those scores don’t always match ecommerce quality standards. Fine-tuning the VLM to close that gap would require GPU training infrastructure and, with only a few thousand labeled examples, carries a real risk of overfitting. Instead, we keep the VLM and rubric prompt fixed, use the VLM’s structured outputs as features, and train a CatBoost classifier on them to predict expert quality labels. CatBoost, a gradient-boosted decision tree library, performs well on small tabular datasets like this one. Because only the classifier is retrained, updating the system as annotations accumulate or criteria change takes a short CPU-only job rather than a foundation model fine-tuning run.

 The VLM receives the page snapshot and a rubric prompt covering four quality dimensions: Visual Storytelling, Feature Presentation, A+ Layout, and Conversion Optimization. Each dimension has explicit criteria and point-deduction rules. For each dimension, the VLM returns a score from 0 to 10, a confidence value, and a natural-language rationale. For example, it lowers the score and explains why when a page lacks diverse modules or lifestyle imagery, or repeats information. The scores and confidence values become the classifier’s features, and the rationales help selling partners understand the result.

 To build the training labels, one ecommerce marketing expert and four professional annotators independently rated 5,478 product detail pages from 10 product categories as high or low quality, using the same criteria. We assessed their ratings and took the majority vote to create a consensus label. The classes are nearly balanced (49.6 percent high quality), so a classifier can’t score well by predicting the majority class.  Faster Retrieval for Better, More Efficient RAG

 Maximum inner product search (MIPS) sits at the core of recommendation systems, search, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Given a query vector, MIPS systems search within a database of millions of candidate key vectors for the one that best matches the query. The original data point associated with that best-matching key is then used to handle the query: in RAG, the document that is best suited to answer the question is typically prefixed to the query.

 As the number of keys grows to millions and vector dimensions reach thousands, the computational cost of running an exhaustive comparison of the query to all keys grows dramatically. Numerous approximate methods have been proposed to trade off accuracy for speed using clustering and quantization. While these baseline methods focus on the properties of the set of keys to build up clever indexing structures, their geometric arguments loosely assume that the set of queries itself will resemble that found in keys, or bypass any considerations about what the queries will look like. This can be problematic on two fronts: In the crucial RAG case, queries arise from short user-generated questions, whereas keys represent long, informative, and curated pieces of text, making keys and queries substantially different. Additionally, most MIPS use cases deployed in production target an existing base of users with a stable, well-characterized query distribution â a signal that generic, query-agnostic methods leave untapped. Crafting methods that work best for a likely source of inputs â rather than aiming for good performance on all possible inputs â is a central tenet of modern machine learning that helps redirect compute resources more efficiently.

 In Amortizing Maximum Inner Product Search with Learned Support Functions , a paper presented at ICML 2026, researchers from Apple train small neural networks (NNs) â once, offline, for a fixed database of keys â that learn how to route queries toward their best match faster. Their approach trades off an upfront amortization cost to understand how typical queries tend to be matched in exchange for faster, more accurate retrieval at inference time through these small NNs.

 The Basics of Maximum Inner Product Search 

 A fundamental principle in MIPS is to leverage the geometry of the set of keys to speed up search: Typical MIPS systems split the set of keys into k clusters â k is typically much smaller than the total number of keys n â keeping track of a representative centroid for each cluster. When receiving a new query, the system compares it with these k centroids. The query is then routed toward the cluster of the representative centroid it is closest to, and subsequently compared exhaustively with all keys stored in that cluster. If the database has n keys and k evenly distributed clusters, the effective number of keys that will be considered in that second step is on the order of n / k â a factor-of- k speedup when compared to the n comparisons done in the naive exhaustive approach. Unfortunately, this routing mechanism can result in errors, since centroid routing is not an exact approach, as illustrated in Figure 1 below.

 MIPS as a Support Function 

 What makes MIPS interesting from a machine learning perspective is that it is built on a mathematical structure that had been largely overlooked before this work: The function that maps a query to the largest inner product with any key in a set is the support function of that set, a well-studied object in convex analysis. This function has two important properties: First, it is convex, so it can be approximated by a class of neural networks specifically designed for convex functions, input convex neural networks (ICNNs) . Second, the gradient of that support function at any query returns the best-matching key itself.

 The broad idea behind this work is to learn a neural network that can mimic the ground-truth support function (or its gradient directly) for a much lower computational cost. This can be achieved through a classical ML technique known as regression: one simply needs to precompute a large number of training examples formed by sampling input vectors (user queries) and pair them with the relevant output signal produced by MIPS (either the optimal score or the optimal key vector itself). All that is then needed is to train a suitably parameterized neural network to replicate the output given the input.

 Two Ways to Learn Maximum Inner Product Search 

 If the regression problem were to be solved perfectly, according to the mathematical approach outlined above, the same neural network that is trained to mimic the support function could also output the optimal key by evaluating its gradient (through automatic differentiation). In practice, we observe that training separate, specialized networks for each of these two tasks works best:

 The SupportNet neural network architecture can learn the support function directly, using a (loosely constrained) ICNN. The approach is mathematically principled and aligns with the convex structure of the problem. We find that this approach can shine when used to compute one support function per data cluster to improve routing quality.

 KeyNet models learn to directly output the best-matching key vector when fed a query. A consistency loss derived from Eulerâs theorem for homogeneous functions keeps their predictions geometrically grounded in the underlying support function, even though no convexity is explicitly enforced.

 Both models are trained on examples drawn from the relevant query distribution: for each sampled query, the ground-truth best match is computed once via exhaustive search and stored, creating a supervised dataset that captures the MIPS solution landscape for the distribution of queries that actually matter.

This provides a cheap yet coarse rule that can be prone to mistakes because a centroid alone cannot account for the spread of its cluster. Indeed, a cluster can hold a key that sits far from its own centroid yet close to the query, and when the query is near such an outlier, centroid routing scores the cluster low and skips it, missing the true best match.

 If one were given access to the support function of each cluster, then one would instead be able to do exact routing, since each support function quantifies exactly to what extent any key within each cluster can match the query with a large score. This is illustrated in Figure 2 , in which the support function of each of the four clusters is plotted for a query that varies in the space of embeddings.

 Leaning on this theoretical insight, we propose to learn these k support functions jointly with a trained SupportNet that can output k scores at once.  AI assistants are rapidly evolving beyond text and image chat boxes into real-time, vision enabled copilots for the physical world. By processing live video streams to guide frontline teams, technicians, and everyday users through multi-step workflows, visual agents have the power to transform productivity. From helping an employee configure a complex device to guiding a manufacturing line worker through a precise operational routine, visual copilots bridge the gap between static instructions and active, hands-on execution. This reflects an ongoing shift from AI systems that are reactive and prompt-induced to systems that are proactive, ambient , and context-aware.

 Recent developments from frontier teams including Thinking Machines , Meta , and ModelBest reflect an industry-wide push to build assistants that collaborate with humans in real time. This momentum is also being translated into the enterprise where Agentic Field Service is equipping mobile technicians with real-time guidance to resolve complex jobs faster. Yet for enterprise leaders preparing to deploy vision-language agents into mission critical operations, a fundamental evaluation question remains:

 Does an AI copilot’s real time advice actually improve operational task completion? 

 Traditional benchmarks are unequipped to answer this. Most legacy evaluation frameworks rely on passive, pre-recorded video clips, grading an assistant’s text outputs against a static answer key. In static testing, however, cause and effect are completely decoupled: if an AI issues a critical warning, the person in the pre-recorded video proceeds to make the mistake anyway. Because static benchmarks lack a closed feedback loop, organizations cannot verify whether an assistant’s guidance mitigates real world errors or derails execution.

 To solve this blind spot, Salesforce AI Research developed Interactive Visual Gym (IVG) —a simulation framework designed to evaluate and train visual AI assistants in dynamic, closed-loop environments.

 Redefining Evaluation Through Dynamic Simulation

 Interactive Visual Gym recreates real-world physical workflows, ranging from culinary prep to device system configurations, and pairs them with an autonomous simulated user.

 Unlike static video playback, IVG operates on an active feedback loop: the AI assistant analyzes the user’s video feed and provides spoken intervention. The simulated user then alters its actions and decisions in direct response to that advice, altering the state of the digital twin in real time.

 Three structural capabilities distinguish IVG from conventional robotic simulators:

 Human-Level Semantic Interaction 
 Standard robotic simulators mandate micro-level physics controls, such as joint velocities or degree coordinates. IVG operates at the high-level semantic layer where humans naturally communicate—directing an assistant to “move to the prep station with the ingredients” rather than calculating navigation paths.

 Programmed Out-of-Plan Disruptions 
 Real world execution rarely proceeds without friction. The IVG harness can schedule unpredictable operational deviations, such as procedural missteps, mid task plan alterations, clarification questions, or memory check-ins at precise workflow milestones. This stress tests an assistant’s capacity to detect anomalies and course correct a live user under pressure.

 Multidimensional User Personas 
 Frontline workers exhibit varying operational patterns. IVG models diverse workforce archetypes across four behavioral axes:

 Domain Expertise: From novices with limited domain knowledge and planning capabilities to experienced users who can anticipate needs and seek minimal intervention.

 AI Trust: Quantifying how quickly a user accepts or questions agent recommendations.

 Execution Velocity: Assessing the preferred pace of task execution, ranging from cautious and deliberate to quick and rapid.

 Interruption Tolerance: The degree of receptiveness to assistant guidance and intervention, ranging from step-by-step support to necessary-only interruptions.

 What today’s assistants still find difficult

 To establish a rigorous enterprise benchmark, we created IVG-Bench to evaluate 14 leading visual AI assistants across 315 complex scenarios in three operational environments. Even the strongest, Gemini 4 Argon, achieves only 53.4% task success within the time limit, averaged across the three environments, exhibits significant weaknesses in factual grounding and actionable guidance, and is often too slow to prevent errors when response latency is considered. Open-weight models—available to download and run locally—generally trail the leading frontier models. We also find that better task success does not necessarily mean better guidance interactions along the way.

 The Human Reality Check

 To check whether our evaluation reflects human experience, we invited participants to step into the simulator, complete cooking tasks with the help of AI assistants, and rate their interactions. When real humans tried the assistants, their preferences matched our automated scores nearly 80% of the time, confirming that the benchmark accurately predicts human satisfaction.

 How Simulation Accelerates Model Performance

 Our simulated environments do more than measure AI performance, they actively improve it. In an early proof of concept, we used the simulator to train an open-source model (Gwen 3.5-4B), incentivizing it to prioritize task completion and error prevention. The results were immediate: the model’s success rate jumped from 33.3% to 42.2% on entirely new tasks it had never seen during training. Beyond the raw numbers, the assistant became noticeably better at delivering the right advice at the right time, stepping in proactively before mistakes happened.
