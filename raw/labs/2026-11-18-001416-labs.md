 Over the past month, Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 4 Carbon brought frontier physical reasoning to robotics : understanding 3D scenes, reconstructing real environments in simulation, practicing tasks in sim before running them on hardware, and directly controlling robot arms from camera images. Last week, we introduced Gemini 4 Hydrogen , which brings strong spatial and embodied reasoning to a much faster model built for speed and efficiency.
 To help developers put Hydrogen to work, today we're launching the Robotics plugin on Google Antigravity : a set of skills for hardware onboarding, simulation and robot control turning Antigravity into a one-stop robotics workbench. Together, they enable a developer to go from a robot in a box to one that can perform a working application in an afternoon. To install the plugin, open Antigravity, go to Customizations and search for Robotics on the Build With Google shelf.
 Hydrogen for agentic robotic control
 Gemini 4 Argon and Carbon showed that a general model can drive a robot arm directly, and Gemini 4 Hydrogen preserves that ability. Give it a goal in plain language and a camera view, and it plans the task, writes and runs the control code, and keeps adjusting until the job is done. On the Artificial Analysis Robotics 0.1 benchmark , Gemini 4 Hydrogen controls a real 6-axis arm through eight everyday tabletop tasks, from pouring a drink to packing blocks into a box. Hydrogen scores 58 , similar to Argon at a lower cost per token.
 Embodied reasoning in a fast generalist
 Hydrogen brings the specialized skills of our Gemini Robotics ER 2 (Embodied Reasoning) models into the core Gemini 4 model series, and it beats them on nearly every axis. On the public ERQA benchmark , Hydrogen outperforms Gemini Robotics ER 2 on spatial reasoning tasks such as multi-view correspondence and 3D trajectory reasoning. For developers using Gemini Robotics ER 2 today, we recommend trying out Gemini 4 Hydrogen for these workloads. 

 Antigravity becomes a robotics workbench
 A fast, capable model is only half the story. Building a robot application has always meant gluing together drivers, calibration scripts, simulators, perception code, and a UI, which requires robotics expertise and a lot of time. The new Robotics plugin for Google Antigravity packages that work into skills Gemini can use on your behalf:

 Robot bring-up: Installs drivers, discovers cameras, calibrates the arm, and verifies safe motion, starting from a USB cable.
 MuJoCo in Antigravity: Builds and previews simulated scenes and assets inside Antigravity, so you can try ideas in sim before touching hardware.
 Perception and control: Uses Gemini’s spatial understanding to find objects, plan grasps, and control the robot in a closed loop.
 Application building: Leverages Gemini’s coding skills to generate the controller, simulation, and complete a full Real-to-Sim-to-Real application development in a seamless, one-stop shop environment.

Artificial Intelligence 

 Reason over time-series data with OpenTSLM on Amazon SageMaker JumpStart

 Across industries, decision-making starts with understanding what is changing and why. Industrial organizations collect data that informs decisions across their operations. This data helps prevent production stoppages, diagnose vehicle faults, explain drops in data-center performance, and adjust pricing as supply and demand shift. However, interpreting that data still requires specialist time, and collecting more data does not mean teams can act on it. General-purpose language models like Claude or ChatGPT can help analyze data. However, processing long histories across hundreds of operational metrics as text or images can consume large numbers of tokens, which increases cost.

 To help organizations act on this data, Aionic Labs is developing temporal foundation models, including OpenTSLM , an open source family of time-series language models (TSLMs). These models interpret operational measurements, from equipment sensor data to supply and demand, alongside context such as maintenance records, market news, and an engineer’s observations. In doing so, TSLMs help systems recognize emerging problems in recent measurements. Experts can then ask follow-up questions to understand what changed and assess how to respond. TimeNet , the shared data infrastructure for time-series data, provides one format and API to find, build, and load the datasets used to train and evaluate these models. Together, they help organizations understand how their systems change and act before problems become costly.

 OpenTSLM is now available in Amazon SageMaker JumpStart , so you can deploy the model in your own AWS environment. This post explores how Nexthink and Aionic Labs are bringing this capability to proactive IT assistance, then shows how to deploy OpenTSLM and adapt it to your own data using TimeNet.

 Figure 1. Time-series language models combine numerical measurements with text context and questions. Potential applications span physical and digital systems, with adaptation and evaluation for each task.

 A customer example: Proactive IT support at Nexthink

 Nexthink and Aionic Labs are building toward a digital workplace where employees get help before they need to ask for it. At the center is Spark , Nexthink’s personal IT agent, enhanced with time-series language models that connect device telemetry and context to the problems employees experience. Spark turns that understanding into action: it checks the likely issue, guides the employee through an appropriate fix, and follows up on the result. Proactive assistance becomes part of keeping work moving.

 The workflow combines recent device time-series, such as CPU activity, memory use, available disk space, application stability, and connectivity, with timestamped events and device and software context. The TSLM receives the numerical measurements alongside this text context and interprets how the signals change over the observation window.

 The TSLM provides Spark with three outputs:

 State summary: Explains the likely issue and the supporting signal patterns, connecting device behavior with the employee’s experience.

 Recommended action: Specifies checks to confirm the issue and a practical action for Spark to offer the employee.

 Expected outcome: Describes the intended improvement and how Spark checks the result.

 Device telemetry and context support investigation of a range of issues, including slow performance under high CPU or memory load, recurring application freezes or crashes, and files that fail to synchronize. Resource usage, application events, storage measurements, and connection activity provide different pieces of evidence. Combined with device configuration and software versions, they give the TSLM a broader basis for explaining the employee’s experience and directing Spark’s next checks.

 In the illustrative low-disk example in Figure 2, the TSLM connects declining free space, a rising disk queue, and more frequent application hangs with likely employee symptoms, such as a slow computer or difficulty saving files. Spark checks how much space is left and whether temporary files can be removed using an IT-approved cleanup. If the checks confirm that a cleanup is appropriate, Spark shows the employee what it proposes to remove and asks for confirmation. After the employee confirms, Spark runs the cleanup, checks the space now available, and asks whether the problem has improved. If the issue persists, Spark offers to pass the findings to IT support.

 TSLMs understand how systems change and help people act on that understanding. For Spark, this means recognizing emerging IT problems and guiding employees toward a solution. The same approach brings time series intelligence to manufacturing, energy, and connected products, turning measurements and context into explanations and intelligent decisions.

 Figure 2. Illustrative proactive support workflow for low disk space at Nexthink. OpenTSLM generates a state summary, a recommended action, and an expected outcome from device history and context. Spark checks the device, runs an IT-approved cleanup after employee confirmation, and follows up on the result.

 Solution overview

 The architecture in Figure 3 provides a reusable deployment pattern for time-series applications. SageMaker JumpStart supplies the model discovery and deployment path.

 SageMaker JumpStart is a machine learning (ML) model hub that accelerates your ML journey. With SageMaker JumpStart, you can evaluate, compare, and deploy pretrained foundation models (FMs), including the newly available OpenTSLM model. As part of the AWS AI offerings, SageMaker JumpStart provides customizable ML solutions which you can deploy to Amazon SageMaker AI inference endpoints within your AWS infrastructure. It complements Amazon Bedrock , which offers serverless API foundation model inference along with features like customizations, knowledge bases, agents, and guardrails.

 Key technical benefits of SageMaker AI and SageMaker JumpStart include:

 Pre-built inference containers – AWS Deep Learning Containers with GPU acceleration, so you can serve the model without building your own image.

 Automatic scaling endpoints – Managed infrastructure scales compute resources based on request volume and latency requirements.

 Inference API – SageMaker real-time inference endpoints with standardized API calls, SDK, and AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM) integration.

 Integrated monitoring – Built-in metrics for throughput, latency, and resource utilization with an Amazon CloudWatch integration.

 Enterprise security – Amazon Virtual Private Cloud (VPC) deployment with IAM access control and network isolation options.

 The model in SageMaker JumpStart is OpenTSLM-Flamingo . It combines the Gemma 3 4B language model with a time-series encoder and cross-attention layers that let the language model attend to the encoded series. The model has 5.24 billion parameters in total, stored in bfloat16, so its weights take about 10.5 GB of GPU memory.

 An application prepares numerical time series and text context, invokes the SageMaker endpoint, and receives the model’s response. The application can present that response to an expert or use it as input to another workflow.

 This separation allows the application to control the observation window, select relevant signals, and validate the response before using it. It also makes the model independently adaptable: a new checkpoint can be evaluated against representative cases before the application switches to it.

 In addition, we show a tutorial that fine-tunes a pretrained OpenTSLM checkpoint from Hugging Face using ARFBench data loaded through TimeNet. The notebook submits a SageMaker training job that loads ARFBench through TimeNet and fine-tunes the model. Amazon Simple Storage Service (Amazon S3) stores the resulting model artifact, which is then deployed to a separate SageMaker real-time inference endpoint for evaluation.

 Figure 3. A reusable architecture for deploying OpenTSLM through SageMaker JumpStart and invoking it from applications that combine time-series data with text context.

 Prerequisites

 For the deployment walkthrough, use an AWS account with an Amazon SageMaker AI domain and a SageMaker AI execution role . The role must be able to create SageMaker models and endpoints in a supported Region, for example a role with the AmazonSageMakerFullAccess managed policy. The quick setup for single users creates both. The managed policy grants broad permissions to get started. For production, use a policy that grants only the permissions you need, as described in Amazon SageMaker AI identity-based policy examples . OpenTSLM runs on a single GPU with at least 24 GB of memory and bfloat16 support, and we recommend 32 GB of host memory. The default instance type is ml.g6.2xlarge (one NVIDIA L4 GPU with 24 GB of memory, 32 GB of host memory). In SageMaker JumpStart, OpenTSLM also supports ml.g6.4xlarge , ml.g6.8xlarge , ml.g5.2xlarge , ml.g5.4xlarge , ml.g5.8xlarge , ml.g6e.xlarge , and ml.g6e.2xlarge . In the Service Quotas console , check your quota for “ ml.g6.2xlarge for endpoint usage”, or for the instance type you choose. You need a quota of at least 1. If it’s lower, request an increase . See the OpenTSLM JumpStart model card for the model access requirements.

 For fine-tuning, use JupyterLab in Amazon SageMaker Studio or a local Jupyter environment with AWS credentials configured to run the accompanying notebook, which submits the training job. The notebook uses the SageMaker default S3 bucket and an execution role. Fine-tuning runs on ml.g7e.2xlarge , so also check your quota for “ ml.g7e.2xlarge for training job usage”. Training runs on the job’s managed GPU instance. The notebook itself does not need a GPU. Obtain access to the selected Hugging Face checkpoint where required.

Artificial Intelligence 

 How LoanPro built an agentic AI loan servicing assistant with Amazon Bedrock AgentCore

 This post is co-written with Mariah Miller from AWS and Colin Terry from LoanPro 

 Have you ever wondered what the experience is like for the person on the other end of the phone when you call your bank for support? When a borrower calls a lender, the answering agent must pull account data from dozens of fields, transaction histories, and workflow queues to orient themselves before they can help the borrower. All the while, the clock runs. Assembling that context by hand means longer calls, inconsistent service quality, and agent frustration. A loan servicing assistant built on agentic AI changes that.

 LoanPro , the operating system for modern lenders, set out to change that. Working with AWS Partner AllCloud and the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center , LoanPro built a multi-agent AI assistant that gives servicing agents immediate access to synthesized borrower context and actionable next-step recommendations. Early results show customer call times reduced by up to 15 percent, according to AllCloud’s published case study.

 In this post, we walk through the business challenge, the agentic AI architecture built on Amazon Bedrock and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore , and the outcomes LoanPro achieved. Amazon Bedrock is a fully managed service that offers a choice of high-performing foundation models from leading AI companies through a single API, along with the capabilities you need to build generative AI applications with security, privacy, and responsible AI.

 About LoanPro

 LoanPro is a modern, API-first lending and credit platform that automates the entire loan lifecycle from origination to payoff. Founded in 2015, LoanPro serves as the system of record for lenders of every type: fintechs, banks and credit unions, and corporates that offer lending as a value-added service. According to LoanPro, the platform serves over 450 lenders, with loans managed on its platform reaching about 31 percent of US households. The platform powers loan management at enterprise scale across the financial services industry.

 LoanPro isn’t a lender. It’s the operating system that lenders run on. That distinction matters because LoanPro’s AI strategy is designed to benefit the institutions it serves across the platform, not a single lending operation.

 The challenge

 One of LoanPro’s core user groups is servicing agents: the people who manage borrower accounts, handle inbound calls, process payments, and resolve issues. During a live customer interaction, an agent might need to review:

 The borrower’s complete payment history, including missed and returned payments.

 Active loan terms, fees, and current balance.

 Prior interactions and open support tickets.

 Available workflow actions such as payment plans, deferrals, or escalations.

 Before the AI assistant, agents navigated this information manually, moving through multiple screens and tabs to assemble the full picture. Less experienced agents took longer, and outcomes varied depending on who picked up the phone. LoanPro needed a solution that could:

 Instantly synthesize account data into a coherent summary.

 Recommend the right next action based on account context.

 Deliver more consistent servicing quality across agents of different experience levels.

 Solution overview

 With the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center, LoanPro partnered with AllCloud to design and build a multi-agent AI assistant using Amazon Bedrock . The assistant runs on Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5 , Claude Haiku 5.5 , and Claude Opus 5.5 on Amazon Bedrock (see Models at a glance in Amazon Bedrock ). Model availability varies by AWS Region. For model availability by Region, refer to Regional availability by models in Amazon Bedrock . It’s orchestrated with Amazon Bedrock AgentCore , a platform to build, connect, and optimize agents at scale, with any framework or model. Agent logic is built with the Strands Agents SDK , an open source framework for building AI agents with tool use. The system coordinates specialized agents through a composer that routes each request to the right specialist, so each agent handles a focused task and can be evaluated and improved independently. The team chose these managed services over building the agent runtime and model access in-house.  Amazon Bedrock prompt caching strategies

 Amazon Bedrock prompt caching reduces latency and input token costs by caching a contiguous prompt prefix from the start of a request through a cache checkpoint. A later request with the same prefix can read cached tokens at a reduced rate instead of processing them again. Cache entries have a time-to-live (TTL) that resets after a successful cache hit.

 Caching is prefix-based rather than block-based. Request sections are evaluated in sequence—tools, system, then messages—so changing an earlier section invalidates matching content that follows it.

 Cache checkpoints (explicit control)

 With explicit cache checkpoints, you can choose the exact prefix boundary. Supported Claude models allow up to four checkpoints per request.

 Place each checkpoint after a stable, reusable prefix.

 Meet the model-specific minimum prefix size. For Claude Sonnet 4.5 the minimum is 1,024 tokens (see supported models and Regions ).

 Checkpoints are evaluated in this order: tools, system, and messages.

 Changing an earlier section invalidates cache matches for later sections.

 The default TTL is 5 minutes. Select models support a 1-hour TTL by setting "ttl": "1h" . Longer-TTL entries must precede shorter-TTL entries.

 Simplified cache management (automated)

 With simplified cache management , you place one checkpoint after static content. Amazon Bedrock searches backward from that point for the longest matching prefix, up to about 20 content blocks.

 One checkpoint can locate the longest matching prefix automatically.

 Use explicit checkpoints when stable content extends beyond the lookback range.

 Agentic workloads, long documents, and large codebases are pushing context lengths from tens of thousands of tokens to hundreds of thousands. At those lengths, attention becomes the most expensive part of a transformer layer. Dense attention scores every query against every key, so its cost per query grows with the context length N, and at 128K tokens it can take most of the prefill time in a layer.

 The model-level solution is sparse attention, a transformer technique in which each query attends to only a small subset of the context, chosen per query, instead of all of it. This cuts both the compute and the memory traffic of attention on long context.

 This post is a practical primer for model developers designing sparse attention that runs well on NVIDIA GPUs.  What is sparse attention and why does it matter? 

 Sparse attention is an AI optimization technique that limits which prior tokens each query attends to. Instead of attending to the entire context, each query attends to only the \(k\) most relevant keys, typically a few thousand. Core attention then stops scaling with context. But those \(k\) keys have to be found, and the component that finds them, the indexer, still scores all \(N\) keys. The cost does not disappear; it moves into the indexer. National programs in North America, Europe, and Asia are driving the installation of quantum processing units (QPUs) alongside high-performance computing (HPC) systems. Every supercomputer will eventually be a hybrid quantum-classical device, drawing on quantum processors to expand what’s possible in scientific research.

 The scientists that will become the first power users of quantum-GPU supercomputing already run their simulations and workflows on HPC systems. Chemistry, weather modeling, engineering simulation, and other traditionally HPC-centric fields stand to benefit from quantum acceleration for certain workloads. For these scientists, the QPU is simply another accelerator, alongside the CPUs and GPUs that supercomputing centers already provide.

 Making quantum computers readily available, easily programmable, and familiar to access, reserve, and execute at scale is imperative for scientific researchers to be able to adopt quantum processors into workflows. Until now, a key missing piece has been a workload manager. Slurm 26.11 changes that with Hierarchical Resources (HRES) plugins, which allow a site to schedule a QPU as a native, cluster-level resource through a short, portable Lua plugin.

It then explains the solution: how HRES models a QPU, and what HRES plugins add in Slurm 26.11, including the API callbacks and capabilities that middleware teams build on.  Building quantum-GPU supercomputing 

 Integrating a QPU at a supercomputing center requires more than just its physical installation. The QPU must be networked with the existing computing infrastructure, and users must be able to request it at job submission the same way CPUs and GPUs are requested. Its usage must be tracked, monitored, and accounted for, and it must abide by site-specific policies. In other words, the scheduler, or workload manager, must manage the QPU like it would any other computing resource.

 Until now, schedulers have had no native way to model a QPU, so most integrations are repeatedly custom-built for each new supercomputing site or QPU builder. The cost of that fragmentation falls on everyone involved:

 Users face a different interface at every site.

 Administrators must maintain custom code that complicates upgrades.

 QPU vendors rebuild their integration for each customer, with no portability.

 Global systems integrators (GSIs) cannot deliver a repeatable deployment.

 On the QPU side, open source efforts such as QRMI and QDMI are unifying how such devices are described to, and controlled by, HPC software. What has been missing is a unified interface in the scheduler used by supercomputing centers for integrating QPUs into HPC deployments.

 Why existing Slurm resource models don’t accommodate QPUs 

 A QPU is typically a cluster-wide accelerator, not part of a node. Slurm generic resources (GRES), the mechanism used for GPUs, belong to individual compute nodes. Modeling a QPU as a GRES binds it to one node, so only jobs on that node can use it. If that node fails, for example, the QPU stays unreachable until an administrator reconfigures the cluster.

 Many integrations instead combine licenses, which represent cluster-wide resources, for scheduling, and use SPANK plugins for the user interface and node setup. This approach works, but it has several problems:

 Idle nodes: Without a license, SPANK only acts after Slurm has allocated nodes, so a job can hold compute nodes idle while it waits for the QPU.

 Two-flag requests: Nothing stops a job from passing device options without holding the license.

 Multiple QPUs: Hosting several QPUs on one cluster is difficult, because their SPANK plugins compete for the same command-line options.

 Upgrade risk: In practice, many SPANK plugins tie third-party code to Slurm internal APIs, so every Slurm upgrade risks breaking the integration.

 No device awareness: Slurm cannot tell on its own when a QPU is recalibrating or offline, so it keeps starting jobs that cannot use the device until it is back online.

 How HRES plugins in Slurm 26.11 enable integration 

 Slurm 26.11 introduces an HRES plugin interface to address these gaps, so that the HPC and quantum communities can integrate with Slurm without changing Slurm itself. Its first implementation, hres/lua , runs a short Lua script. The plugin reports device health to the scheduler, accepts device options as part of the resource request, and runs setup and teardown code at each stage of the job.

 A site describes the QPU as a cluster-level HRES in resources.yaml and points it at the plugin. The two files divide the work. resources.yaml holds site-specific details and policies, such as endpoints and which nodes can reach the device, under the system administrator’s control. The plugin holds the device logic, so the community or a QPU vendor can write it once and ship it to every site that deploys Slurm 26.11.

 Modeling a QPU as a hierarchical resource 

 HRES was added to Slurm 25.05 as a beta feature and is the evolution of licenses. Like a license, an HRES is a count of a resource that does not belong to any single node. Unlike a license, that count is associated with a specific set of nodes: the ones allowed to use it (Figure 1).
