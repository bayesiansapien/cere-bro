### [AWS Machine Learning] Smart Approvals in Amazon Quick: Human oversight where it matters
- url: https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/smart-approvals-in-amazon-quick-human-oversight-where-it-matters/
- published: 2026-11-04T16:33:03+00:00
- via: feed

```text
Artificial Intelligence 

 Smart Approvals in Amazon Quick: Human oversight where it matters

 How should security leaders preserve human oversight as AI agents take on more work? Permissions establish which actions agents can take, but a permitted action can still warrant a person’s judgment. Requiring approval for every step might seem like the safest approach, yet multi-step tasks can produce repeated prompts , even for routine actions. Research on security warnings shows that attention and adherence can decline with repetition. The challenge isn’t to require more approvals, but to preserve attention for decisions that need human review.

 Smart Approvals in Amazon Quick addresses this challenge by reviewing eligible actions within your existing controls. It evaluates each proposed action against the user’s request and either approves it automatically or returns it to the user for review. It does this without granting additional permissions or removing required human approvals. Security leaders retain control over which actions must always go to a person, while Smart Approvals is designed to reduce unnecessary interruptions in routine work.

 Figure 1: Policy determines eligibility. Smart Approvals approves eligible actions or returns them to the user for review. Policy denials remain blocked

 Policy sets the boundary

 Quick evaluates each proposed action against Custom Permissions, Quick Guardrails, connector action permissions, and the user’s settings before automated review. Denied actions remain denied, and mandatory human approvals still apply. With Smart Approvals enabled, an action is eligible only when the user’s setting is “Ask each time” and no other control requires human approval. Connector action permissions, configured when a connector is created, can require human approval for specific actions.

 Administrators use Custom Permissions to determine who can use Smart Approvals. It’s on by default, but profiles with deny-by-default enabled must explicitly allow it. Users can also turn Smart Approvals off for themselves. That choice applies across conversations and scheduled runs. When it’s off, actions otherwise eligible for automated review require the user’s approval, and no account-level setting can turn it back on for them.

 How eligible actions are reviewed

 Smart Approvals uses a separate model, the classifier, to evaluate eligible actions against the user’s request and a bounded view of the conversation. It considers what the action does, where data goes, and who it affects. It can consult the user’s knowledge graph, which contains people and working relationships drawn from connected sources. This helps it understand how recipients relate to the user, such as whether a recipient is in the user’s reporting chain. This lookup stays within your Quick account. Quick doesn’t use the content the classifier reviews, including knowledge graph data, to train models or improve the service.

 An action can match the request and still warrant human review. Examples include sending content outside the organization or to senior leaders in the user’s reporting chain, changing access, deleting records, or initiating payments. This list isn’t complete, and the classifier also weighs the surrounding context. Reviewing actions against the user’s request adds a check against indirect prompt injection, where instructions in retrieved content attempt to redirect the agent. Quick instructs both the agent and classifier to treat that content as information, not instructions. Model review can still make mistakes, so actions that must always receive human approval, including those that move sensitive data, should require it through connector action permissions.

 The classifier provides judgment. The permission service enforces authority. Each automatic approval is single-use, applies only to the exact action and parameters reviewed, and grants no ongoing permission. Quick labels automatic approvals in the conversation. Actions the classifier doesn’t approve return to the user, as do timeouts and other review failures, which never grant automatic approval. These enforcement boundaries limit what an approval authorizes. They don’t establish that the classifier’s judgment was correct.

 When an action goes beyond the request

 An account manager asks Quick to prepare a quarterly business review brief for AnyCompany using her team’s account notes and last quarter’s email. She then asks Quick to send it to two colleagues she names. Assuming the send is eligible for automated review, Smart Approvals evaluates the proposed action against her request and the available conversation context.

 Now suppose an account note says the brief should also go to an all-sales distribution list of about 2,000 people. That would change the audience beyond the recipients she specified, so Smart Approvals is designed to return the proposed send to her for review, where she can decline it. An approval to send to the original recipients can’t be reused for the wider audience.

 Audit evidence and investigation

 The Quick permission service records permission updates associated with Smart Approvals in AWS CloudTrail in your account. Each event identifies the user, connector, action, and time, and marks the request as associated with Smart Approvals. That marker doesn’t establish that the permission service accepted the grant or that the action ran. Quick also records permission-check verdicts, including denials from connector action permissions, and changes to a user’s Smart Approvals setting.

 Investigators can correlate these events by user and time with other Quick activity in CloudTrail and logs delivered through Amazon CloudWatch Logs . Chat logs capture the user’s request, system response, and conversation context. Agent metadata logs record changes to agents. Records from the destination system, such as a sent email or updated ticket, confirm whether the action completed. Together, these sources help reconstruct what happened without treating approval as proof of execution. CloudTrail Event history retains events for 90 days, so configure a trail for longer investigations. Because chat logs can contain sensitive content, restrict access and consider CloudWatch Logs data protection policies to mask sensitive data.

 Where to start

 Because Smart Approvals is on by default, begin by confirming that actions requiring human approval are configured through connector action permissions. Set up a CloudTrail trail and delivery of chat logs and agent metadata logs to CloudWatch Logs to support investigations. Chat logs capture only activity after delivery is configured, so earlier approvals will lack that conversation context. With approval requirements and logging in place, use Custom Permissions to limit Smart Approvals to a pilot team working on a defined workflow before extending it to other teams.

 Before the pilot, define what success looks like: fewer prompts for routine actions, with consequential actions still returning to a person for review. During the pilot, review a sample of automatic approvals and test high-impact cases, such as sending content outside the organization or adding a recipient introduced by a document. Confirm that these cases come back to the user. Confirm that your team can trace each action from approval through execution. Expand to more teams and workflows only when the results meet those criteria, and reassess as actions and policies change.

 The measure of effective oversight isn’t how often an agent asks for approval, but whether people retain control over consequential decisions. For security leaders, the case for expanding Smart Approvals should rest on evidence from the pilot. Fewer prompts are valuable when they preserve attention without weakening accountability.

 For setup steps, see Smart Approvals in the governance section of the Amazon Quick User Guide.

 About the author
```

Artificial Intelligence 

 Four layers of context engineering for production AI agents: How Deep Insight applies them in working code

 When production AI agents handle tasks such as data analysis and report generation, context management can become a major bottleneck. A 200K-token window sounds generous until a real-world task burns through most of it in minutes. Data analysis and report generation leave the agent failing mid-execution, losing accuracy, or driving up costs. A body of published guidance already addresses this, but the individual techniques are easier to read about than to apply. Applied together, they make a measurable difference: in the reference implementation this post walks through, a single write-and-execute tool cuts the tokens each script adds to context by about 95 percent, and loading skills on demand shrinks the system prompt by about 97 percent.

 This series organizes that guidance into practices you can apply directly. Anthropic’s engineering posts, together with features built into the Strands Agents SDK, converge on four layers of context engineering: isolate context at the architecture level, control it through prompts, externalize it to the filesystem, and verify it with independent agents and safeguards. In this post (Part 2), you will learn how each layer works and what it looks like in working code. We applied these techniques in Deep Insight , an open source multi-agent data analysis solution built on the Strands Agents SDK and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, which serves as the reference implementation throughout.

 Post series

 Part 1: 5 production challenges in multi-agent systems and how Deep Insight solved them. 

 Part 2: Four layers of context engineering for production AI agents: How Deep Insight applies them in working code.

 Part 3: Harness engineering in practice: moving Deep Insight from dev to production. (Coming soon!)

 The first post defined five core challenges that production multi-agent systems must solve. It introduced how Strands Agents SDK , Amazon Bedrock and Bedrock AgentCore , and AWS Fargate each address different layers of the problem, with code examples. This second post takes the four layers of context engineering practice that sit on top of that architecture and shows each one implemented. The third post guides you through deploying and operating agents in a secure private network on Amazon Virtual Private Cloud (Amazon VPC), covering everything needed for real-world operations.

 What is context engineering?

 Context engineering is the discipline of optimizing the information in a large language model (LLM)’s limited context window so agents perform at their best. Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.5, available on Amazon Bedrock, offers a 200K context window, but complex production tasks quickly exceed it. For example, a seemingly straightforward request like “ Analyze sales data and create a DOCX report ” runs through several stages: data loading and exploration (5 min), category-based sales analysis (3 min), generating five charts for time-series analysis (10 min), statistical validation (3 min), and final report generation (5 min). Together they consume over 150K tokens across 15 minutes.

 Processing such tasks in a single context causes problems: the window overflows mid-task, stale context degrades performance, and errors require re-running the whole pipeline. As context grows, attention to important information drops, and inefficiency raises cost under per-token pricing.

 “As the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model’s ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases (Context rot).” 

 — Anthropic, “ Effective Context Engineering for AI Agents ” (2025.09)

 Context engineering solves exactly these problems, and it is not only a collection of techniques. Spanning architecture design, prompt writing, tool implementation, and verification, it’s a layered discipline where each layer compensates for what the previous ones miss. This post details the four-layer approach you can apply to manage context efficiently.

 How to handle tasks that exceed the context window

 Anthropic has published several techniques for handling long-horizon tasks. Deep Insight groups these techniques into four layers, with each layer addressing the limitations of the others. The following table sets out the four layers.

 The sections that follow take each layer in order, stating what the layer prescribes, why the preceding layers alone are insufficient, and how the practice is implemented in Deep Insight.

 1. Isolating context through multi-agent architecture

 Give each specialized agent its own context window and pass only compressed results to the orchestrator. Deep Insight does this with a hierarchy of Coordinator, Planner, Plan Reviewer, Supervisor, and Tool Agents (Coder, Validator, Reporter, Tracker, Auditor).

 With a single agent, all information, from user requests through data exploration, analysis, chart generation, validation, and reporting, accumulates in one context and easily exceeds the window. Giving each agent its own context keeps even the Coder, which uses the most, under 25K tokens total. Each agent receives only what it needs and signals others with a completion notice and brief summary.

 The key to isolation is minimizing shared information. Deep Insight uses a Python dictionary, shared_state , that stores only four things:

 messages : The previous agent’s final message (not the full conversation history).

 clues : Compressed summaries collected from each agent.

 full_plan : A checklist generated by Planner and updated by Tracker.

 history : A brief log of agent names and messages.

 The clues use a fixed CLUES_FORMAT: instead of each agent’s full conversation (tens of thousands of tokens), only compressed core results are passed.

 # coder_agent_tool.py -- context passing format
CLUES_FORMAT = "Here is clues from {}:\n\n<clues>\n{}\n</clues>\n\n"
RESPONSE_FORMAT = "Response from {}:\n\n<response>\n{}\n</response>\n\n*Please execute the next step.*"

# After Coder completes: add only compressed result to clues
clues += CLUES_FORMAT.format("coder", response["text"]) 

 This pattern is the one recommended in Anthropic’s “ How We Built Our Multi-Agent Research System ” post, which describes an orchestrator-worker pattern in which a lead agent delegates to specialized subagents, with each agent working only within its own context. You do not need to build it yourself: the agents-as-tools pattern in the Strands Agents SDK provides this isolation at the framework level.

 2. Controlling context inflow through prompts

 Layer 1 isolation alone is not sufficient: even with independent contexts, an agent that generates long responses inflates the Supervisor’s context. Layer 2 limits each agent’s output through prompts.

 For Deep Insight, we use prompts to limit how much information each agent returns to the Supervisor. Three rules do most of the work, as Deep Insight’s prompts show.

 “Start with the strongest model and a simple prompt. Then incrementally add specific instructions based on actually observed failure cases.” 

 — Anthropic, “Effective Context Engineering for AI Agents” (2025.09)

 Implementation detail 2-2: Self-contained code philosophy

 The Coder agent’s prompt explicitly states “variables do not persist between scripts.” Every script must include all necessary imports and load data directly. The prompt provides both incorrect and correct examples:

 # WRONG -- Attempting to use a variable from Turn 1 in Turn 2
# Turn 1:

# Turn 2:
print(f"Total: {total}") # NameError: name 'total' is not defined

# CORRECT -- Every script is self-contained
import pandas as pd
df = pd.read_csv('./artifacts/cache/df_main.csv') # Load from cache
total = df['Amount'].sum()
print(f"Total: {total}")

There are Cohere’s most powerful retrieval models yet, delivering leading performance on enterprise documents and complex retrieval tasks. If you’re already using OpenRouter, you can get started immediately - using the same API key and credits as your existing models, with no additional setup.

RAG grounds an LLM's answer in your own data by fetching relevant chunks before it writes. Embedding search provides a fast and effective first-stage approximation. A reranker then reads the query and each candidate together, adding precision to what’s been retrieved.

Two features of the Cohere models shape this design:
 Embed 5 Pro and Fast share one embedding space. Index with embed-5-pro for quality, then query with embed-5-fast for latency.
 Rerank 5 scores are calibrated. A score means the same thing across queries and domains, so one relevance threshold can filter out weak context before it reaches the LLM.
 By the end you'll have a short Python script that answers questions over your docs with scored citations. This same approach can be applied to virtually any retrieval use case, from enterprise search and customer support to knowledge management and document discovery.
 A RAG pipeline has two halves: an offline job that builds a searchable index, and an online path that runs for every question. Knowing which calls happen once and which happen per request help explain most of the pipeline's cost and latency. Indexing runs once per corpus.  Retrieval finds the chunks whose vectors sit closest to the question's vector. This acts as a representation of semantic similarity between the two pieces of content.

 From Static to Polymorphic Inference
 One of our motivations behind writing this blog post is that a lot of the discourse around inference systems remains quite focused on the legacy Static Inference paradigm.

 We take some of the blame here! For a long time, it has been possible to write kernels that are optimal for a variety of settings. FlashAttention, near and dear to our hearts, is still one of the best ways to run training workloads and many prefill workloads.

But through the experience of putting some of the first megakernels into production for our customers last year, we learned an interesting lesson: megakernel fusion is actually not the right design goal for modern Polymorphic Inference! In the era of Polymorphic Inference, every variation – from model architecture (e.g. MoE), to workload (dynamic workloads larger than batch size 1) – requires different technical approaches.
 Optimizing Polymorphic Inference for diverse, rapidly-changing conditions
 At Together, we are systems and AI researchers at heart – so we view this as an incredibly exciting time to be rethinking our research agenda for this bright new future.
 ‍ So, what’s the solution when your production traffic becomes ever more diverse? Get the AI agents to optimize themselves. This is the RSI moment for OSS models! 
 Achieving Polymorphic Inference requires solving a number of technical challenges in how we serve models. An ideal Polymorphic Inference experience is one that responds to the rapidly changing landscape of model architectures and workloads.
 For example:
 If your traffic shape changes – either through a product change or a change in user behavior – you should expect your inference system to adapt quickly.
 If you are deploying workloads on a new model – you should expect your inference provider to approach ideal hand-tuned baselines as soon as possible.
 If you have an existing product with stable user traffic, and you deploy it on a new hardware platform – you should expect performance to approach hardware bounds as quickly as possible.
 These are lofty goals, but we believe that they are worth striving for. For us, one of the keys to achieving these goals is getting AI agents to help optimize themselves . The RSI loop is here for OSS models!
 Continuous Compilation with Together Compiler
 One of the projects that we’re announcing today as a first step towards achieving our goals for Polymorphic Inference is Together Compiler , a rapid AI-powered performance optimization RSI loop. Today, Together Compiler powers Continuous Compilation optimization loops across many of our top production inference workloads.
 Together Compiler takes the classic challenge of optimizing inference workloads and recasts it from a traditional compiler translation problem to an agentic search problem. Together Compiler identifies a core set of key optimization patterns and pairs a swarm of agents with a high-fidelity persistent engine for fast iterations. This work was the result of many months of work by Together’s Frontier Performance team, notably from Reyna Abhyankar and Dylan Lim!

 Together Compiler is running in production today on some of our most popular serverless endpoints, where it has found further gains on top of already highly optimized code.
 Excitingly, Together Compiler gives us the ability to quickly adapt to rapidly changing model architectures and traffic workloads. We call this capability Continuous Compilation . Whereas previously new workloads would require new sets of hand-written kernels and optimizations, Continuous Compilation driven by Together Compiler means that we can automatically adapt to new traffic shapes and workloads.

 Shape-shifting speculative decoding 
 Inference engine configurations proposed in open-source repositories, VLLM, InferenceX, AgentX launch the engine with a fixed speculative decoding draft model and lookahead parameter. Speculative decoding needs to adapt per-domain, per-request, per-token and as a function of the traffic load.
 ThunderAgent Router: Routing across heterogeneous deployments 
 Serverless inference endpoints serve an enormous number of users, each bringing their own traffic shapes and SLAs. This heterogeneity requires us to dynamically route requests across workers and deployment configurations across our clusters. We released ThunderAgent as an ICML 2026 Spotlight paper to share some early insights and have expanded on these foundations in production.
