```text
 AI for Science grantees Jakob Macke , Daniel Gedon , and Stefan Wahl are researchers at the ELLIS Institute Tübingen and the University of Tübingen, where they build AI tools for developing scientific models in neuroscience and beyond. In this guest post, they discuss how AI is starting to change scientific modeling. 
 In 1951, Cambridge University’s computer went out of action for six months. This was inconvenient for physiologist Andrew Huxley, who, alongside his colleague Alan Hodgkin, had just built a set of equations to describe how a particular squid nerve cell fired. The scientists wanted to understand the “action potential” of these nerve cells: the electrical pulses they use to communicate, which underlie everything brains do, from perception to intelligence. The only way to find out whether the equations worked was to solve them and compare the solutions to data.
 Huxley had hoped to use the newfangled computer to solve the equations. Instead, he was forced to solve them using a hand-cranked mechanical calculator. It took him over three weeks.
 The basic structure of their model was dictated by known facts about the cell. Hodgkin and Huxley modeled its membrane as an electrical circuit. One part of the model, however, was just an educated guess. In order to make the curve fit the data they had measured, they found that they had to raise one variable to the fourth power—that is, to multiply it by itself three times. At the time, they did not know why. They just knew that otherwise the model didn’t predict the observed data as well.
 They did offer a hypothesis, however. They cautiously suggested that the variable in question, which governed how particular gates in the cell membrane opened, might require the fourth power because multiple charged particles in the membrane had to move into place before the gate would open.
 Decades later, long after both Hodgkin and Huxley had won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1963, another team found the structure of a related potassium channel protein . And they discovered that, yes, it had four identical pieces arranged around a central pore . The model was fit to the data in 1951. Even then, it predicted how fast the impulse travels relatively accurately. More than that, it turned out to have helped predict the structure of potassium ion channels themselves, which it had never been fit to.
 Today, an AI system could plausibly find an equation like Hodgkin and Huxley’s in an afternoon, and perhaps it would fit the experimental data even more closely. But the point of the story is that simply knowing the fourth power did the job was not enough; what mattered was knowing that a simpler version of the model did not work. An AI system that merely hands you the model might give you an answer, but it does not give you understanding. Reproducing the data with a model is insufficient, because any number of models could do the same. What matters is being able to make claims about the real-world system itself, and ideally about other, similar systems. To do so, you need to know which features the model shares with other models that work, and which set it apart from models that don’t. As the mathematician Richard Hamming wrote in 1962, “The purpose of computing is insight, not numbers.”
 A three-step program
 More than 70 years on, the Hodgkin-Huxley model remains the standard description of electrical activity in neurons. The same kinds of equations, with different constants, describe nerve cells across the animal kingdom, from squids to humans, even though our evolutionary lineages separated more than half a billion years ago.
 Even more important than its longevity, however, is what the Hodgkin-Huxley model tells us about how models work. Hodgkin and Huxley’s approach can be broken down into three steps, and most scientific modeling involves some version of them. 
 The first is building the model. This means deciding what the model is made of and what shape its equations take. In Hodgkin and Huxley’s case, that meant the parts of the electrical circuit—the membrane and the gates—and the form of the equations, such as raising the underlying variable to the fourth power.
 The second is constraining the model—tuning its adjustable parameters to fit real measurements. For Hodgkin and Huxley, that included settings like how often the gates open under different voltages.
 The third is interpreting the model—asking what the model says about the real system, such as suggesting that the fourth power meant the gate had four moving parts.
 In practice, scientists go through these steps repeatedly . If the numbers fail to fit the measurements, or if the interpretation makes no sense, they go back and revise.
 All three steps were once done by people, but AI systems are increasingly involved in each of them. Machines have arguably made the most progress in constraining models, so we’ll start there.
 Splitting the bill, or how to constrain models
 When Hodgkin and Huxley constrained their model by eye and intuition, they ended up with a single set of numbers. This told them that the model could match the data. It didn’t tell them whether other numbers would have worked just as well or even better.
 Imagine you know that a group dinner added up to $120, but not who ordered what. Many different combinations of orders could have added up to that amount—perhaps everyone ordered a mid-priced burger, or perhaps one person had steak and lobster while everyone else had soup. Models of nerve cells work on the same principle: different combinations of gate parameters can produce near-identical electrical activity. Simply showing one combination that fits well hides all the other plausible parameter settings, and other combinations might have led to different predictions. This is true of almost any phenomenon being modeled. Many different sets of parameters can explain the observed data equally well.

 An illustration of constraining the Hodgkin-Huxley model. (1) A measurement from a nerve cell. (2) How likely the measurement is for each combination of potassium and sodium channel settings. Darker means a better fit. The blue dot is interactive. (3) Simulations of the model at three settings, compared to the measurement (gray). Some fit well, while others do not.

 The solution is a statistical method called Bayesian inference. It gives you every set of numbers that works reasonably well, as a probability distribution that takes into account their fit to the data and your prior knowledge about the parameters. The distribution takes the shape of a curve, with the best fits at the peak and the weaker ones at the fringes.
 But this method has usually been impractical for complex models, because it involves calculating how likely your measurements would be under every possible set of numbers. In Hodgkin and Huxley’s model, that would still be feasible, but for anything more complicated, it would be computationally impossible. For example, if each of the thousands of ion channels opens and closes at random, you would have to work out every combination. Returning to our restaurant bill analogy: it would be easy to answer, “If person A had the burger and person B had the steak, what would the bill come to?” but much harder to show which combinations could have led to the final bill, especially as the number of people and menu options grows.
 A different approach called simulation-based inference (SBI) overcomes this reverse-engineering problem with sheer computation. You pick random parameters, run the model, and record the output. You do it again and again— perhaps 100,000 times for a model like Hodgkin-Huxley , and millions of times for larger, more complex ones. Then you train a neural network on the results so that, given an output, it can return the range of parameters that could have produced it.
 All SBI requires is that you can run the model: put in a set of parameters and get back the kind of data you would expect to see. A model you can run like this—parameters in, data out—is called a simulator , hence the name “simulation-based inference.” You never need to use a formula to calculate how likely your measurements are, which is the step that makes standard Bayesian inference often impractical. These days a simulator is usually a computer program, but it doesn’t have to be: Huxley, cranking through the equations by hand, was running one. Many simulators are easy to run forward but impossible to reverse. Some can’t even be written out as a tidy equation—their assumptions are built into the rules they follow—but they are models nonetheless. For many complex phenomena, from networks of neurons to colliding galaxies, simulators are often the only kind of model we have. SBI lets us constrain those models with data.
 Running all those simulations is expensive and can take weeks. But once it is done, a trained network can perform inference on new data in seconds, without requiring further simulations. That speed has changed what’s possible. When two neutron stars collide , SBI can work out roughly where in the sky the collision happened, fast enough to point telescopes at it while the signal is still detectable. The same approach has been successfully applied to problems in cosmology , structural biology , geophysics , and many other domains.
 And SBI has been applied to nerve cell models , including the Hodgkin-Huxley model . Because it returns a distribution rather than a single fit, we can see which parameters are well constrained by the data, where more than one explanation is plausible, and where more data—and thus another experiment—is most needed.
```

```text
Related articles
 Harness design for long-running application development 

 View more 

 Søren Kierkegaard wrote that life can only be understood backward , but it must be lived forward . We’ve encountered a similar bind when designing memory for AI agents.
 As humans, we encode memory constantly as we go about our lives. The hippocampus plays a central role in this, capturing experiences in the moment so that memories form quickly and are immediately available. But only some experiences are consolidated into long-term knowledge.
 Much of that consolidation appears to happen when our brains are offline. As we sleep, and during quiet rest , the hippocampus replays our recent experiences. This is thought to gradually distill those experiences and integrate them into the neocortex—the part of our brains that stores most of what we think of as knowledge.
 Agent memory can use a similar division of labor. Agents capture much of their work in logs or transcripts. Researchers at Letta and UC Berkeley proposed sleep-time compute as a way for models to use idle time between interactions to reorganize their existing context. The same idea can help agents organize their memory by reflecting on logs to fix mistakes or produce new notes and representations. This process is often called dreaming (Figure 1).
 We’ve found that dreaming has been a useful tool for helping us give Claude effective memory. In this post, we share how we’ve come to think about agent memory and walk through several evaluations we’ve used to explore agent memory, including the use of dreaming, and what each component contributes to memory organization.
 Claude can use dreaming to organize memory. This can correct mistakes, create notes, build new memory representations, or shape what’s preemptively loaded into future sessions.

 Memory notes
 By “agent memory,” we mean knowledge that Claude gains from experience—including interactions with users and tasks it’s been assigned—and that it retains between sessions, saved as context it can read later. It excludes other ways models can learn or manage context, such as changes to their weights (the internal parameters set during training), in-context learning (knowledge gained within a single conversation), and compaction (summaries written by Claude that replace its older turns so it can continue working in a new context window).
 In the human brain, chemical signals released before, during, or after an experience can mark a memory as important. Marked memories tend to be replayed more often than unmarked ones during offline periods. This process, called replay , helps stabilize and integrate them into long-term memory.
 Similarly, Claude can tag salient events in a session log as they happen, so they’re easier to find later, or it can write them to a memory store, such as a folder of memory notes (Figure 2). But how can Claude tell what’s salient in the moment, before it knows what’s coming next?
 Claude can write notes to memory.

 Memory can be a prediction about what the future will need
 Memory is, occasionally, a prediction. By writing down a rule, an agent predicts that the pattern it’s describing will be relevant again. For instance, writing down a verified fact is a prediction that an agent in a future session will want to rely on that fact without double-checking it. Over time, Claude has become much better at making these kinds of predictions.
 Consider a task from Continual Learning Bench (CL-Bench) 1.0 , a benchmark that measures an agent’s ability to use memory in order to tackle long-horizon tasks. One particular task asks Claude to answer a series of questions by looking up information in a database. After Claude gives an answer, it’s told the correct solution and can write a memory to help answer future questions.
 Memory can support a learning loop here. If Claude gets a question wrong, it can record the failure, come up with theories about what went wrong, test those theories, and turn what it finds into a rule about how to answer in the future.
 We ran the CL-Bench 1.0 task using Claude Managed Agents . Claude started each question in a fresh session, with one exception: it could retrieve files from a memory directory that persisted from one session to the next.
 Different models achieved different levels of success at creating their own learning loops. Claude Sonnet 4.6 got as far as generating theories about why an answer was wrong. Its memory directory contained a scattering of files, each listing several untested guesses.
 CL-Bench database task, comparing memory with a stateless baseline across models.

 Claude Opus 4.7 consolidated its notes into a single reference document and began marking which facts it had checked and which were still unconfirmed. Claude Sonnet 5 marked each answer as confirmed after the grader had checked it, and wrote a correction when it had answered wrong.
 Claude Opus 5 was the first of these models to close the loop. Most of its entries included a rule (and also how it knew the rule was true), answers to earlier questions, and clear warnings about traps it had discovered along the way (Figure 3).
 Like Opus 5, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 both closed the loop, using memory to capture validated rules about how to best answer future questions. With memory, both scored about the same as Opus 5, though their baseline performance without memory was slightly higher.
 Claude has learned to judge salience
 We also evaluated Claude’s ability to play Pokémon Red , a game that challenges Claude to navigate complex long-horizon tasks, as a test of Claude’s judgment about what to save to memory. At the start of each run, we gave each model a folder of memory files that it could read from and write to while it played (Figure 4).
 We compared the memory folders from one run each of Claude Sonnet 3.7 and Claude Fable 5, when each was about 10,000 steps into the game. A key difference we noticed was what each model thought was worth remembering. Sonnet 3.7 assumed everything might matter and wrote to memory indiscriminately. Its memory folder contained an archive of encounters, often with a separate file for each battle it fought, each item it found, and each non-player character it met. Ultimately, Sonnet 3.7 wrote 413 memory files across 225 folders and earned one badge (badges mark a player’s progress through the game).
 Fable 5, by comparison, wrote nine files in two folders, plus a single top-level file called global.md , and earned four badges.
 Claude’s memory directories for Sonnet 3.7 versus Fable 5 while playing Pokémon.

 Sonnet 3.7’s approach was a problem mainly because the game state of Pokémon changes constantly—Sonnet 3.7’s detailed record was continuously going out of date. As a result, as Claude progressed, it had to spend more time and resources combing through its memories rather than playing the game. Around step 10,000, for example, Sonnet 3.7 started its fourth folder of “master” memory files. Over the next 17 steps, it created six files, deleted nine others, and took no actions in the game itself.
 Fable 5’s approach used memory for things the game didn’t explicitly tell it. Some of the kinds of information Sonnet 3.7 wrote to memory—such as how much money the player has or where the player is on the map—can be read off the screen every turn, so storing it is redundant. By contrast, memory files about strategy, plans, and tested rules exist only if Claude works them out, and they disappear unless Claude writes them down.
 Fable 5 organized its memory accordingly. Its global.md file acted as a dashboard, holding the current goal, a prewritten plan for tackling gyms (the challenges where badges are won), and the model’s own rules for using memory. A locations folder held plans for navigating particular routes, and a gameplay folder held strategies based on what it had learned about how the game works. For example, after seven Poké Balls in a row failed to catch a Magikarp (one of the easiest Pokémon to catch), Claude wrote, “If 3+ balls fail in a row, VARY the timing (insert extra ‘wait’ presses between menu steps) - the catch landed right after doing this.”
 With a much smaller memory folder, Fable 5 progressed much further in the game than Sonnet 3.7. Opus 5.5 took a similar approach: it kept a small folder of memory notes that tracked its current goal and next steps, rewrote them as it played, and beat the game.
 Steering Claude to write memory
 We’ve found that it helps to tell Claude what memory is for, but not how to use it. On CL-Bench, we simply told Claude that a memory folder was available. That general guidance was enough for Opus 4.7 and Opus 5 to use memory effectively, and it gave both a noticeable boost in performance (Figure 3).
```

```text
 During reinforcement learning (RL) post-training, training trajectories are generated on the fly by running inference with the current policy model. For training efficiency, it is common to run inference and training in parallel on separate GPU resources in a disaggregated setup. Although the policy model is updated only on the training GPUs, the system must periodically refresh the weights on the inference GPUs with the latest parameters generated on the training GPUs. This transfer of model weights between training and inference GPUs is called refit .

 Refit involves two defining characteristics that pose challenges. First, inference and training have different computational requirements, so the weights are distributed differently across inference and training instances by having different parallel mappings (data, context, tensor, expert, and pipeline parallelism). 

 Second, because refit must update all model weights on the inference GPUs, the communication payload is on the order of the total size of the model’s weights. This payload is already very large for the latest frontier models and is expected to continue growing. Together, these characteristics mean that refit requires high-performance communication across a wide variety of distributed source and destination layouts. 

 This post explains how AI training frameworks can address these refit challenges using the Reshard collective, a new type of collective communication primarily designed to accelerate RL weight refit. NCCL M2N Reshard, an open source reference implementation of the Reshard collective, is available through the NVIDIA/nccl-extensions GitHub repo.

 Challenges of current weight refit methods as models scale 

 Existing refit implementations commonly use a gather-and-broadcast approach to handle differences between training and inference layouts.

 The typical gather-and-broadcast weight refit sequence for every model parameter is structured as follows:

 Training GPUs all-gather their local shards to reconstruct the complete parameter.

 The complete parameter tensor is broadcast to the inference GPUs.

 Each inference GPU retains the shard required by its local layout.

 Using the complete tensor as an intermediate representation makes it straightforward to transfer weights between different layouts. However, an inference GPU receives the full parameter even when it needs only a shard. This process creates redundant communication in both the gathering and broadcasting phases, which is suboptimal.

 The size of this communication grows with the model size, which continues to scale. For example, Kimi K3 contains 2.8 trillion parameters , while Qwen 3.8 2.4T A95B contains 2.4 trillion parameters. Assuming BF16 weights at two bytes per parameter, their weight sizes are approximately 5.6 TB and 4.8 TB, respectively.

 Accounting for both the all-gather and broadcast stages gives an estimated logical payload of approximately twice the size of the model’s weights (Table 1).

 For today’s largest open weight models, the communication payload already reaches around 10 TB per refit, which must be performed at every training step. As LLMs continue to scale, the overhead associated with this large payload will become even more significant. As a result, a more sophisticated approach is needed to perform weight refit that reduces the actual payload size while properly accounting for diverse weight layouts.

 Reformulating refit as a DTensor-to-DTensor transfer problem 

 To overcome these limitations, start from the principle that refit should transfer only the theoretical minimum amount of weight data. However, achieving this presents several challenges. Training and inference instances can use different parallelism configurations and GPU counts, producing different shard boundaries. A destination shard may therefore need to be assembled from portions of several source shards, and vice versa.

 Replication further complicates the mapping since the same weights may be available from multiple source GPUs or required by multiple destination GPUs. It is nontrivial to determine which source GPUs should send which tensor regions to each destination while achieving high performance across all possible configurations.

 The key to addressing this challenge is to identify the right abstraction for this unique communication pattern. The team turned to the existing PyTorch distributed tensor (DTensor) abstraction, which is designed to represent tensors that are sharded or replicated across GPUs under different forms of data and model parallelism. The key insight is that weight refit can be reformulated as resharding a DTensor from the training layout to the inference layout, regardless of the parallelism configurations used by the two instances.

 A DTensor layout is described by the tensor’s global shape, a device mesh specifying the logical arrangement of GPUs, and placements specifying how the tensor is sharded or replicated across that mesh. Refit can use the source description to specify where the updated weights reside and the destination description to specify which weights each inference GPU needs.

 For example, consider an 8 x 8 weight matrix split by rows across two training GPUs and by columns across four inference GPUs. Assuming the tensor is sharded in the row dimension on the training GPUs and sharded in the column dimension on the inference GPUs, each training GPU holds a 4 x 8 shard, while each inference GPU needs an 8 x 2 shard. Each destination can obtain its required shard by receiving a 4 x 2 region from each source. These regions follow directly from the overlap between the source and destination shards, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Like all-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, broadcast, and so on, reshard defines a communication pattern that applications can invoke as one operation. It transfers the values of a logical tensor from its source distribution into its destination distribution.

 A Reshard call can be specified simply by the source and destination DTensor descriptions. This abstraction not only simplifies the refit problem, but also encapsulates the communication logic in a library, allowing the underlying algorithms to evolve and improve independently of the application.
```
