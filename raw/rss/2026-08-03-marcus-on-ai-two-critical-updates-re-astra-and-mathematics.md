# Two critical updates re: Astra and mathematics

<p>First, abstracting from all the words <a href="https://open.substack.com/pub/garymarcus/p/openais-amazing-but-vastly-oversold?r=8tdk6&amp;utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&amp;utm_medium=web">in my long analysis on OpenAI Astra yesterday</a>, the overall point was that Astra may not be the breakthrough that OpenAI wants you to think it was. I expressed concerns about how little they revealed about what they did. Moreover, I thought I didn&#8217;t stress it there, we all know they are facing huge economic and competitive pressures; they need a win. I am often left with the feeling that their public facing communications are better marketing than they are science.</p><p>My jaw dropped a couple hours after my own post, reading this from <a href="https://alpo.ge/">Levent Alp&#246;ge</a>, an accomplished mathematician who works at Anthropic:</p><div class="twitter-embed"></div><p><em>One </em>(obviously smart) guy at Anthropic was able to &#8220;replicate&#8221; (as best he could given OpenAI&#8217;s very sparse report of what they actually did) half of OpenAI&#8217;s results within 24 hours.  And not with Astra or some other new model, but with the already publicly-released Fable.</p><p>I had a couple reactions to this.</p><p>a. Alp&#246;ge&#8217;s fast partial replication made me wonder whether OpenAI&#8217;s real advance here was finding (through AI) <em>which open problems were amenable to a certain kind of search and verify technique</em>.  OpenAI&#8217;s Noam Brown acknowledged, without much detail, that there were failures on some (and maybe many) others:</p><div class="twitter-embed"></div><p>Unfortunately, beyond that OpenAI does not report which problems they tried and failed at. They have given us a numerator without a denominator, always a worrisome sign.</p><p>This doesn&#8217;t take away from OpenAI&#8217;s accomplishment - if the system solved a bunch of open problems that&#8217;s very impressive &#8211; but it again raises questions about what the scope is: all math? A certain subset of problems that can be searched in a certain way? </p><p>Maybe once that subset has been flagged, maybe many systems can do them. That&#8217;s kind of what Levent Alp&#246;ge&#8216;s partial replication shows.</p><p>And many what was most critical was not some innovation in Astra per se but the idea (probably from a human) to do this particular search. </p><p>b.  Alp&#246;ge&#8216;s fast partial replication undermines the widespread presumption that Astra is a major breakthrough. Astra is an obviously an advance to some degree, but that degree may well turn out to be merely incremental relative to other recent models.   </p><p>Related to this, I was interested to read in <em>The Information</em> this morning that OpenAI hasn&#8217;t decided whether to call Astra (which was an internal code name) GPT 6 or GPT 5.7. If OpenAI truly thought Astra was a quantum leap,, I doubt that would be such a hard choice. </p><p>How good Astra is in open-ended real-world problems very much remains to be seen.</p><p style="text-align: center;">&#167;</p><p>Second, a longtime reader of this newletter, Ihor Gowda, pointed me to <a href="https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/age-of-ai-icm-2026.pdf">a fantastic lecture given by Terence Tao</a>, by any measure one of the world&#8217;s leading mathematicians; many see Tao as <em>the</em> leading mathematician. His lecture is lucid, accessible, and genuinely important.</p><p>What&#8217;s more you don&#8217;t need to be a professional mathematician to read it. It should be mandatory reading for anyone thinking about how AI will affect mathematics,  and strongly recommended for anyone who cares about AI&#8217;s impact on the world.  </p><p>One of the issues that he raises he calls &#8220;proof indigestion&#8221;; what happens if AI creates a lot of stuff, much of it true, but not necessarily <em>useful</em>:</p><div
