# GDPx: Measuring Frontier Model Capabilities Across the Most Valuable Domains

<p><a href="https://gdpx.semianalysis.com/">GDPx</a> is a benchmark that covers the most valuable domains for frontier intelligence. It aims to be the world&#8217;s best benchmark for measuring general agentic capabilities.</p><p><strong>We believe the first trillion dollars of AI revenue will be concentrated in sectors that can deploy frontier intelligence at high ROI</strong>, not broadly diffused throughout the whole economy. Building a general-purpose benchmark based on share of GDP or the SOC/O*NET occupational taxonomy is therefore a mistake. <strong>If your goal is to track the progress of frontier models, you should focus on the domains where they will be most impactful.</strong></p><p>Specifically, we cover the following domains:</p><ul><li><p>Chip design</p></li><li><p>Cybersecurity</p></li><li><p>Biology</p></li><li><p>Finance</p></li><li><p>Legal</p></li><li><p>Quant trading</p></li><li><p>Recursive self-improvement (RSI)</p></li><li><p>Hardware design</p></li><li><p>Materials science</p></li><li><p>Alignment</p></li><li><p>Software engineering</p></li></ul><p>And intentionally forgo industries like government, To explore the data yourself, checkout the <a href="https://gdpx.semianalysis.com">official website</a>.</p><h2>A collaboration with 14 specialized data partners</h2><p>Domain-specific benchmarks have gotten significantly better over the past year largely thanks to the rise of specialized RL environment startups. Yet OpenAI&#8217;s <a href="https://openai.com/index/gdpval/">GDPval</a> (along with its derivatives like <a href="https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/gdpval-aa">GDPval AA v2.1</a>) remains the most popular general purpose benchmark still reported on major <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5">new</a> <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-1">model</a> <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5">release</a> <a href="https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-6/">cards</a>. This is despite being released over a year ago on September 25, 2025&#8212;an eternity in AI land. GDPval may have been marketed as a &#8220;knowledge work&#8221; benchmark, but agents weren&#8217;t really even a thing yet. This had a huge impact on its task design, and as we&#8217;ll later explain, makes GDPval woefully inadequate for evaluating today&#8217;s frontier models.</p><p><strong>Our goal with GDPx is to create the world&#8217;s best benchmark for general agentic capabilities.</strong> However, as soon as we started working on this project, it became clear why we&#8217;ve yet to see a worthy GDPval successor. Models like GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5 are already pushing the boundaries of human knowledge. If you want to make a finance or cyber task these models can only solve &lt; 20% of the time, you need real domain expertise to ensure the task is hard for the right reasons. Many <a href="https://x.com/EpochAIResearch/status/2100704765332394255?s=20">popular benchmarks</a> sidestep this problem by shipping tasks that are downright <a href="https://openai.com/index/separating-signal-from-noise-coding-evaluations/">unfair</a>. This typically manifests in a grader that checks for requirements impossible to infer from the task description or environment. For example, a coding problem may ask the model to fix a bug that a human engineer would agree has two valid solutions&#8212;one using &#8220;module A&#8221; and another using &#8220;module B&#8221;. However, a poorly designed grader may require the AI to use &#8220;module A&#8221; and unfairly give 0 reward when the AI chooses &#8220;module B&#8221;. Correct all such issues, and the benchmark often ends up being <a href="https://preferencemodel.com/blog/terminal-bench-4-is-saturated/">saturated</a>.</p><p>Making high quality tasks for a single domain you have expertise in is already hard enough&#8212;there&#8217;s a reason why labs are willing to pay $5k+ for a single high quality task. Finding one company with sufficient expertise in both AI evals and enough domains to make a broad-based GDP benchmark is downright impossible.</p><p>To solve this problem, SemiAnalysis partnered with specialized RL environment startups for each domain:</p><ul><li><p>Chip Design (<a href="https://ariadata.ai/">Aria</a>, <a href="https://arcverex.io/">Arcverex</a>, <a href="https://ergodex.ai/">Ergodex</a>)</p></li><li><p>Cybersecurity (<a href="https://arithmeticlabs.ai/">Arithmetic</a>)</p></li><li><p>Biology (<a href="https://latch.bio/">Latch Bio</a>)</p></li><li><p>Finance (<a href="https://livingcontextai.com/#data-partnerships">Living Context</a>)</p></li><li><p>Legal (<a href="https://www.chakra.dev/">Chakra</a>)</p></li><li><p>Quant trading (<a href="https://thirdmomentresearch.com/">Third Moment</a>)</p></li><li><p>RSI (<a href="https://extrapolate-ai.com/">Extrapolate</a>)</p></li><li><p>Hardware design (<a href="https://www.beautifulmachines.ai/">Beautiful Machines</a>)</p></li><li><p>Materials science (<a href="https://valenceforge.com/">ValenceForge</a>, <a href="https://emulated.so/">Emulated</a>)</p></li><li><p>Alignment (<a href="https://www.fleetai.com/">Fleet</a>)</p></li><li><p>Software engineering (<a href="https://datacurve.ai/">Datacurve</a>)</p></li></ul><p>All partners were subject to a rigorous QA process we detail in the methodology section. For some domains, we had multiple partners who wanted to be exclusive and ran a process to select the one with the highest quality tasks.</p><p><strong>We would like to give a huge thank you to all of our partners for contributing to GDPx.</strong> They&#8217;re among the best vendors for their respective domains and it would&#8217;ve been impossible to create such a broad-based benchmark without their help.</p><p>Additionally, we&#8217;d like to thank <a href="http://fireworks/">Fireworks</a> for providing inference for all the open source models, <a href="https://modal.com/">Modal</a> for providing the sandbox infra needed to run our tasks, as well as <a href="https://x.com/abhijaymrana">Abhijay Rana</a> for helping source some of the partners and assisting with early QA.</p><h1>Problems with OpenAI&#8217;s GDPval</h1><p>To be clear, we believe <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04374">GDPval</a> was impressive for its time and was the first benchmark to evaluate AI on such a broad range of tasks. It&#8217;s not OpenAI&#8217;s fault that a benchmark created before the rise of agents does a poor job evaluating them today.</p><p>Every GDPval <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/openai/gdpval">task</a> is precisely specified and non-interactive, with all the necessary context directly spoon-fed to the model in the prompt. This is closely related to a demand model we&#8217;re building for <a href="https://semianalysis.com/tokenomics-model/">Tokenomics subscribers</a> which projects OpenAI and Anthropic revenue in 2027 and 2028.</p><p>We believe <strong>it&#8217;s a mistake to build a benchmark for general agentic capabilities based on share of GDP or the SOC/O*NET occupational taxonomy</strong> <strong>because AI diffusion won&#8217;t be equally distributed and not all domains will need frontier intelligence</strong>. Thus, <strong>if your goal is to track the progress of frontier models, you should focus on the domains where they&#8217;ll be most impactful.</strong></p><p>From there, we reached out to 30+ RL environment companies that have expertise in chip design, cybersecurity, biology, RSI, materials science, etc. Every potential partner was subject to the same rigorous agentic QA (quality assurance) process which checks for consistency, completeness, cheating, unfair verifiers, and much more. <strong>Our QA process was based on Andrew and Max&#8217;s previous experience co-founding an RL environment startup</strong> <strong>that sold data to multiple frontier labs.</strong> <strong>When we run this same QA process on popular benchmarks like Terminal Bench 4.0, it flags nearly 50% of tasks as broken.</strong> Of course, we also manually reviewed hundreds of trajectories to verify what our automated QA was flagging and to catch things that automated QA normally wouldn&#8217;t. We also perform failure mode analysis on our tasks to ensure our set is diverse and that even within a task, models fail for a variety of reasons.</p><p>However, we are also well-aware that any QA system can be gamed given sufficient iteration. If a task only passes QA after 3-4 rounds of feedback, it becomes very difficult to tell whether the task actually became good, or if the provider simply tricked your QA process.</p><p>For this reason, <strong>all partners were required to pass our automated QA first try with only minor mistakes at worst</strong>. Of course, if our QC flagged serious issues, we gave potential partners the chance to explain why we were incorrect. This strict requirement&#8212;along with provider reputation backchanneling&#8212;allows us to be confident in the quality of GDPx despite not having in-house expertise in every domain.</p><p>Without naming any names, we dropped many potential partners (including some very prominent ones) who failed to pass our QA first try.</p><p>Some domains also had multiple partners who wanted to be exclusive. In these cases, we ran a process to choose the provider with the better tasks. As these capabilities improve, the research process itself becomes increasingly automated, creating the feedback loop necessary for recursive self-improvement.</p><h3>Built by frontier AI researchers</h3><p>Extrapolate is a neolab for data focused on building the research environments and data flywheel needed to automate recursive self-improvement.</p><p>The team brings firsthand experience from xAI, Scale AI, and Meta AI. The benchmark spans RL debugging and reward hacking analysis, pretraining, post-training, data curation, KV-cache optimization, and improving complex AI systems.</p><p>One of their main design principles is that real frontier research rarely starts from scratch. Researchers inherit existing codebases, model checkpoints, training logs, and experiments completed by other researchers. All our tasks inherit an already built complex codebase and logs the agent needs to comb through first before improving it further.</p><h3>Sample task: Debugging a broken large-scale RL run</h3><p>During Grok 4.5 development, Qasim and others spent two weeks debugging a single large-scale RL training run because Grok kept exploiting subtle failures in the training harness.</p><p>Inspired by this experience, Extrapolate recreated a similar problem using a large-scale GRPO run containing approximately 1,000 rollouts on private RL tasks. shows, even today&#8217;s strongest models don&#8217;t come close to saturating a realistic enterprise task in high finance or transaction accounting. While certain capability greenfields exist (i.e. memory, context windows, tooling), frontier models predominantly lack enterprise-grade financial reasoning and judgment that would make them useful in the workplace.</p><p>For example, <strong>1) </strong>Models often weigh sources based on how explicitly they state a figure rather than which document is authoritative, allowing an outdated adviser schedule to take precedence over an executed agreement; <strong>2)</strong> Models often correctly perform a requested calculation while overlooking the second-order implications a senior reviewer would instinctively examine, such as a contingent obligation that becomes debt at closing, taxes triggered by a payment, or an ownership stake that does not represent full economic entitlement; <strong>3) </strong>Models often scrutinize their arithmetic rather than their underlying assumptions, allowing internally consistent answers built on incorrect premises to pass their own review; <strong>4) </strong>Surprisingly, models can even fail basic mechanics in ways a junior analyst would readily catch: recognizing a tax liability on a cash rather than accrual basis, double-counting an add-back, or overwriting a linked model with hardcoded values.</p><h3>Their public task: &#8220;Bellwood-QoE&#8221;</h3><p>Living Context&#8217;s public task&#8212;Bellwood-QoE&#8212;exemplifies the failure modes described above. Their current focus is on building chip design evals/RL environments as well as accelerated EDA tooling. You can reach them at <a href="mailto:team@ariadata.ai">team@ariadata.ai</a>.</p><p>For GDPx, Aria contributed tasks covering both verification and PPA optimization. All private tasks are seeded from real, taped-out silicon and production software. The model is asked to create or verify a subsystem with an architecture spec or reference model, governing standards, and a simulation and synthesis flow mirroring what an engineer would have at that stage.</p><p>In the verification tasks, the model works as a DV engineer, writing a block&#8217;s sign-off regression from its architecture specification. The regression is graded on how well it separates correct implementations, which vary in timing and microarchitecture, from buggy ones. These implementations are written by engineers with over a decade of experience creating leading silicon and include bugs that slipped past the original design teams and shipped in the source chips.</p><p>In the PPA optimization tasks, the model works as an RTL designer. It implements a block that matches a production reference model bit for bit across hundreds of hidden workloads, then optimizes it for speed, area, and energy under real timing and area constraints.</p><p>Select domains include networking, storage, memory systems, ML acceleration, graphics and video, security, and on-chip data movement. Common observed failure modes include</p><ol><li><p><em><strong>Correctness under PPA pressure.</strong></em> Models&#8217; shortcomings in verification are exacerbated by PPA optimization, causing implementations to fail silently. Across Aria&#8217;s internal benchmark, a model&#8217;s broad chip design capability is highly correlated with its ability to write comprehensive, efficient testbenches.</p></li><li><p><em><strong>Architectural reasoning.</strong></em> Architectural tradeoffs are difficult for models to reason about at a high level, and they iterate locally after achieving a functionally correct implementation. The original architecture often establishes a local optimum for the end product.</p></li><li><p><em><strong>Design closure.</strong></em> Models misjudge how many iterations a design needs to reach functionality and struggle to budget a fixed compute allocation across slow simulation, synthesis, and layout runs.</p></li></ol><h4>Reimagining chip design with AI</h4><p>Today, chip design follows a standardized flow from architecture to tapeout, with each step largely owned by a separate team. Aria believes these stages are less a fundamental property of the chips themselves and more a reflection of enterprise organizational structure, EDA tooling, and the maximum context length a human engineer can reason about. Current public chip design benchmarks, as well as Aria&#8217;s private tasks, inherit this same abstraction structure.</p><p>Aria strongly believes that as LLMs become superhuman at chip design, these abstraction barriers will erode. Their public task, Tensor Graph Machine, is an example of what models can do when given complete freedom over the chip design process.</p><h4>Their public task: Tensor Graph Machine</h4><p>Tensor Graph Machine treats the chip itself as a black box, prescribing only:</p><ul><li><p>Pins: a fixed port list, with a command interface to the host and a 32-bit memory interface to DRAM.</p></li><li><p>Function: bit-exact agreement with TensorFlow Lite&#8217;s int8 reference kernels on every input, under any memory timing.</p></li><li><p>Sign-off: a routed Nangate45 layout that passes timing, DRC, LVS, IR-drop, and formal equivalence checks.</p></li></ul><p>The model gets seven small example graphs and is scored on fourteen held-out int8 networks drawn from real edge-inference models for audio and image tasks.</p><p>Scores range from 0 to 1. The grader runs synthesis, then functional tests and physical sign-off, then gate-level simulation, which measures energy. A design that fails any stage, or takes more than 100 minutes to grade, scores 0. You can reach him at <a href="mailto:contact@valenceforge.com">contact@valenceforge.com</a>.</p><h4>Frontier AI already has the math for materials science, it&#8217;s just missing the judgement</h4><p><a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/millions-of-new-materials-discovered-with-deep-learning/">Previous efforts</a> in AI for materials science have often focused on using heavy computational methods to propose and simulate numerous candidate materials. But in the long run, these efforts rarely produce anything very novel or useful. Real material science progress with AI requires the full arc of:</p><ul><li><p>Proposing candidates to achieve some goal</p></li><li><p>Choosing promising ones with calibrated confidence</p></li><li><p>Proposing viable synthesis steps for each (or recognizing there is none)</p></li><li><p>Providing a dispositive characterisation of whether it was successfully synthesised</p></li><li><p>Iteratively learning over the data after making each candidate</p></li></ul><p>The first step is the most open to naked computational attack, and so receives a disproportionate amount of attention. But without synthesis steps, it is impossible to actually make the computationally predicted materials. And on the occasions that a material is actually synthesised, it is sometimes surprisingly mischaracterised. These basic steps of the materials science workflow are being missed.</p><p>The good news is that the AI scientist&#8217;s instinct of relying on more computation is correct. For a human, doing highly mathematical techniques like Rietveld refinement with powder X-ray diffraction data is difficult because we cannot visualise all aspects of our models, and what effect each of the parameters has on them, particularly when encountering local false minima, profile asymmetry, and peak overlaps. But a frontier LLM like GPT-6 Astra essentially saturates tasks of mathematical difficulty, even for a materials-specific method like Rietveld refinement.</p><p>Furthermore, there are many mathematical shortcuts that are as-yet unexploited because the search space is so broad and nobody has had time to try them. In 2019, as part of an initiative Bryan made minor contributions to, <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-019-0356-8">Kristen Severson et al</a>. found data features in the discharge voltage curves in the first 100 cycles of a battery charging and discharging that can predict its eventual cycle life (of up to 2300 cycles), speeding up the process of gathering empirical data up to 20x. The search for these kinds of mathematical shortcuts and the writing of such complicated software packages can now be automated, opening up whole unexplored avenues in materials science discovery and experimentation.</p><p>The next challenge for frontier models is to pair this mathematical ability with the ability to make judgement calls on experimental methods, rooted in properly discerning structural and chemical characterization, understanding the physics behind what the experiments show, and then optimizing over available resources and equipment to iteratively plan the path of acquisition. Fortunately, these are reasonably workable as reinforcement learning environments.</p><h4>Making the tasks for GDPx</h4><p>ValenceForge&#8217;s GDPx tasks are designed specifically to test the model&#8217;s judgement. The bar is never scaled down, so a strategy cannot win by shrinking into a small, low-variance sliver of trades either. The score targets held-out daily P&amp;L because every intermediate metric a pipeline emits, such as R&#178;, validation Sharpe, or information coefficient, can be satisfied by a strategy that still loses money in production.</p><h3>Although models are superhuman at coding, they&#8217;re still poor quants</h3><p>As GDPx shows, even today&#8217;s strongest models rarely find edge that survives out of sample, even with a full research stack and eight hours. When the chart didn&#8217;t support what the task prompt asked for, models closed the gap themselves.
