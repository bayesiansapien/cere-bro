# I contributed to AIUC-1's latest white paper on Mythos

<p><em>I am Ken Huang. I was one of the contributors to "After Mythos: Defending at Machine Speed," the AIUC-1 Consortium's 2026 whitepaper, alongside more than 100 security leaders. Here is what it says, and the piece I would add from the agentic-AI side. Full credits at the end.</em></p><p>The unsettling part of the paper is a single claim about how we got here. Mythos, Anthropic's April 2026 model, was not built to be an offensive cyber weapon. The capability fell out of training a frontier model to write code well. If elite offensive cyber is a side effect of good coding, then it is not one lab's story. It is the whole industry's trajectory, and it arrives on the schedule of the next training run, not the next budget cycle.</p><p>I sat in the review for this work, and I read it through the lens I spend my days in. I lead the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic AI, so I care about a question the paper raises and leaves open: when offense moves at machine speed, the agents you deployed to move fast become the softest way in. I will get to that. First, what the paper says: what changed, what breaks, and what an operator does on Monday.</p><p>A note on how this article splits: everything through the acknowledgments is free, the analysis, the three imperatives, and the agent failure modes I would add. <strong>The paid section at the very end is the operator's playbook, a 30-day sequence ranked by leverage, a readiness checklist you can run against your own program, and a straight-answer FAQ.</strong></p><h2>What changed</h2><p>Offensive cyber stopped being scarce. The UK AI Security Institute pegs frontier cyber capability as doubling every 4.7 months, down from 8 months in late 2025. In its early-access cohort, Mythos surfaced thousands of high-severity flaws across every major operating system and browser, including an OpenBSD bug that had survived 27 years of expert review and decades of fuzzing. On reproducibility benchmarks it wrote a working exploit on the first try for more than 83% of vulnerabilities, against a near-zero rate for the prior generation.</p><p>This is not one model. Within weeks, the UK AISI rated GPT-5.5 a touch ahead of Mythos on hard cyber tasks. And you do not need a frontier model at all: April 2026 research wrapped a mid-tier open-weight model in a multi-agent harness and produced ten unknown zero-days in Chrome, two of them confirmed sandbox escapes (CVE-2026-5280 and CVE-2026-6297). CrowdStrike's 2026 report already counts an 89% year-over-year rise in AI-enabled adversary operations.</p><h2>Why your program is calibrated for a world that is ending</h2><p>Every control you run was tuned for human-paced attackers. Patch SLAs, detection latency, blast-radius tolerance: each assumed that finding a high-severity bug in hardened software took a skilled person days or weeks, and that you had a reaction window between disclosure and exploitation. AIUC-1 polled 52 security leaders, Fortune 500 CISOs, federal leaders, banking and healthcare and defense. They rate their readiness for a Mythos-class incident at 4 out of 10 today, and hope to reach 6.7 in a year. Two in three already run AI agents in production. One in five run agents on business-critical paths. The gap is not a knowledge gap. It is an operating-speed gap.</p><h2>The three imperatives</h2><p>The paper does not chase novelty. cycle</h3><p>Measure patch SLAs in hours to days for the assets that matter, and tell the business about them as exposure windows rather than compliance checkboxes. The deeper move is to patch less under fire by catching bugs before they ship. Put an AI security review on every changed line, and push the same demand onto vendors at procurement time. Here is what the gate looks like in a pipeline.</p><pre><code>on: pull_request
jobs:
  ai-security-review:
    steps:
      - run: git diff origin/main... &gt; changes.patch
      - run: llm-review --policy secure-sdlc changes.patch --out findings.json
      - run: test "$(jq -r .blocking findings.json)" = "false"</code></pre><p>The diff is the unit of review, so the model reads only what changed, which keeps it fast and cheap. The last line is the teeth: if the reviewer marks any finding as blocking, the test fails and the merge stops. Drop that assertion and you have a linter that writes opinions nobody has to obey. The point is to move the work left, so the patch you never have to ship is the cheapest one.</p><h3>Design for breach</h3><p>Assume the attacker gets in, and make that fact boring. Zero trust is the frame: no implicit trust by network location, every request authorized against least privilege, the environment segmented so one foothold does not become free movement.</p><div The 30-minute <code>ttl</code> means a stolen credential rots fast. The <code>alert_on</code> line turns a denied action into a signal, because an agent reaching for scope it never had is the tell that someone else is driving it. The matching control on the host is binary allowlisting: even when the model finds a bug and writes the exploit, the second-stage payload it wants to run is not on the list, so it does not execute. An allowlist does not negotiate.</p><h3>Defend at machine speed</h3><p>If detection and response run in days, you lose, because an attacker who lands anywhere can reach the data that matters in the time your SOC triages one alert. So the SOC has to run on the attacker's clock. The shape that works is tiered: cheap models do high-volume alert triage, a correlation layer aggregates, and a frontier model sits on top for the hard contextual calls. Past triage, AI can write the production fix that closes the incident before a human wakes up.</p><p>That last step is where I would slow down, and the paper agrees. A remediation agent with write access to production is a high-value target. Subvert it and you have handed the attacker the keys, at machine speed. So it gets governed like any other high-privilege agent: least privilege, mutual authentication, segmentation, allowlisting. The defense and the risk are the same mechanism pointed in opposite directions.</p><h2>The numbers worth keeping</h2><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 y2="14"></line></svg></button></div></div></div></a></figure></div><h2>What I would add: the agent is the soft way in</h2><p>The paper calls agents the fastest-growing population of credentialed actors and tells you to put them on a tight leash. I agree, and from the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic AI work I would push the point harder. A Mythos-class adversary does not always need a fresh exploit. If you have deployed an agent with broad tools and a standing credential, you have pre-positioned an insider, and hijacking it is cheaper than breaking in.</p><p>Three failure modes sit underneath the paper's advice, and they are where I see real programs lose.</p><p>The first is excessive agency. An agent scoped for convenience instead of need is a blast radius waiting for a trigger. The least-privilege policy I showed earlier is not paperwork. It is the difference between a stolen agent that can read one ledger and one that can move money. Scope the tools, not just the data.</p><p>The second is the untrusted input the agent reads. A remediation agent ingests a ticket, a log line, a pull request, and any of those can carry an instruction planted by the attacker. The paper flags the remediation agent as a high-value target. I would make the rule explicit: treat every byte an agent reads as attacker-controlled, and put provenance on its inputs the same way you put least privilege on its actions. Prompt injection is not a model quirk. It is remote code execution against the agent's intent.</p><p>The third is memory. An agent that carries state forward also carries a compromise forward. Plant one instruction in its long-term memory and it re-runs your attack on a schedule, long after the foothold looks closed. Containment in a machine-speed world has to include the agent's memory and its tool-call history, not only its network segment. When you design for breach, design for a breached agent, not just a breached host.</p><p>One practical note on speed. The paper is right that AI should write the production fix before a human wakes up. The control that keeps that from becoming the attacker's dream is where you place the human. Do not gate every action, because that rebuilds the latency you are trying to kill. Gate the irreversible and the high-blast ones, and let the rest run. Approval fatigue is its own failure mode.</p><h2>My take</h2><p>The honest tension in this paper is that its answer to AI-speed offense is AI-speed defense, which means the blast radius of a compromised defense tool is now the whole program. You are not removing the dangerous capability. You are pointing it at your own environment and praying your governance holds. That is the right move, because the alternative is losing on tempo, but anyone who reads "deploy AI throughout the SOC" as risk reduction has skipped a step. You are trading a speed risk for a concentration risk, and you had better instrument the concentration.</p><p>The other thing I keep sitting with: none of the three imperatives is new. Patch fast, assume breach, detect in minutes. The paper says as much. What is new is that the penalty for not doing them went from "you might get breached" to "you will be breached and find out from someone else." The 4-out-of-10 self-rating is the real headline. The controls have existed for years. The adoption has not. If your honest readiness is a 4, the work this year is not research. It is closing the distance between what you already know and what you run.</p><p>The CISO's question is no longer whether adversaries get these tools. They have them. The question is whether you can operate at the same speed when they use them.</p><p><strong>Before we get deep into paid content, here is the acknowledgements to people who contributed to this work.</strong> </p><h2>Acknowledgments</h2><p>This article summarizes the work of the AIUC-1 Consortium and its members.
