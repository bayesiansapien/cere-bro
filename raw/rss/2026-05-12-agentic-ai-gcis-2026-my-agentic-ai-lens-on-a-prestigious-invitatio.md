# GCIS 2026: My Agentic-AI Lens on a Prestigious, Invitation-Only Cyber Summit Near Washington

<div class="preformatted-block"><label class="hide-text" contenteditable="false">Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when published</label><pre class="text"><strong>
A Note Before We Begin
</strong>
I wrote these takeaways in late February, weeks before Anthropic's April announcement of Claude Mythos Preview&#8212;a frontier model so capable at autonomous vulnerability discovery and exploitation that Anthropic refused a public release and launched Project Glasswing instead. Re-reading my GCIS notes now is instructive: nearly every theme that room was signaling under Chatham House Rule&#8212;machine-speed exploits, the defender productivity gap, recursive self-improvement, auto-defenders as necessity, non-human identity governance&#8212;has since been confirmed empirically by Mythos and the industry response to it. The signal was there in February. What follows is what it sounded like.

The event I was invited to is <strong>Global Cyber Innovation Summit (GCIS)</strong> on <strong>February 25&#8211;26, 2026</strong> in Baltimore&#8212;an <strong>exclusive, invitation-only</strong> gathering often described as the &#8220;<strong>Davos of Cybersecurity,</strong>&#8221; held near the nation&#8217;s capital and built for candid, senior-level exchange. The eventwhich attracts a true &#8220;who&#8217;s who&#8221; mix of cyber and AI security leaders across government, industry, and investment. y2="14"></line></svg></button></div></div></div></a></figure></div><p></p><p>A quick note up front: <strong>everyone will walk away with different takeaways</strong> depending on their role&#8212;CISO, builder, researcher, policy maker, investor. <strong>My takeaways lean heavily toward agentic AI and what it changes</strong>: the speed of offense, the future of defense, the identity crisis created by non-human actors, and the economic implications of recursive self-improvement.</p><p>Because of the Chatham House Rule, this post contains <strong>no quotes and no attribution</strong>&#8212;only themes and implications.</p><h2><strong>My top 10 takeaways</strong></h2><h3><strong>1) A trillion agents are coming&#8212;and our infrastructure and security aren&#8217;t ready</strong></h3><p>This is the headline. We&#8217;re moving toward the agentic era: agents writing code, operating workflows, coordinating inside collaboration tools and scaling output by orders of magnitude.</p><p>The constraint won&#8217;t be &#8220;can we build agents?&#8221; but whether our <strong>infra, security controls, and governance</strong> can survive agent-scale autonomy.</p><h3><strong>2) Weaponized models can collapse the time from &#8220;known vuln&#8221; to &#8220;working exploit&#8221; &#8212; and that should scare us.</strong></h3><p>One of the most alarming threads for me was the idea that attackers can increasingly <strong>weaponize models</strong> to turn public vulnerability information into actionable exploitation <em>fast</em>&#8212;sometimes by having the model recognize a newly publicized <strong>CVE</strong> and generate exploit logic with minimal iteration. That is no longer a &#8220;future risk&#8221;; it&#8217;s already being demonstrated in realistic <a href="https://red.anthropic.com/2026/cyber-toolkits-update/?utm_source=chatgpt.com">cyber evaluations</a>. <br />Separately, we&#8217;re also seeing just how compressed post-compromise timelines have become: CrowdStrike reported an average breakout time under 30 minutes, with the <strong>fastest observed breakout at 27 seconds</strong> (from initial access to lateral movement). That &#8220;27 seconds&#8221; metric is about <strong>breakout speed</strong>, not CVE-to-exploit&#8212;but paired with AI-accelerated exploit development, it paints the same direction of travel: attacks are moving toward <strong>machine speed</strong>, and human-paced defense will struggle to keep up. (<a href="https://www.darkreading.com/cyber-risk/attackers-now-need-just-29-minutes-to-own-a-network?utm_source=chatgpt.com">Dark Reading</a>)</p><h3><strong>3) We have more defenders than attackers&#8212;for now. AI can flip the balance.</strong></h3><p>The optimistic near-term truth: defenders outnumber attackers. The uncomfortable question: what happens when attackers fully leverage AI to multiply output per operator? Even if headcount stays favorable, productivity parity can collapse.</p><p>There&#8217;s a second imbalance that matters even more:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Defenders</strong> must move through change management, compliance gates, approvals, rollout windows, and regression risk.</p></li><li><p><strong>Attackers</strong> don&#8217;t have those restraints.</p></li></ul><p>So defense isn&#8217;t only about better detection&#8212;it&#8217;s about reducing organizational friction so remediation can happen at the speed the environment now demands.</p><h3><strong>4) Recursive self-improvement (RSI) in models/agents points toward near-zero marginal cost software</strong></h3><p>If agents can improve their own workflows, prompts, testing scaffolds, code generation quality, and tool use over long-running loops, the marginal cost curve of software changes dramatically. Not &#8220;free,&#8221; but closer to <strong>software at near-zero variable cost</strong> for many categories&#8212;especially internal apps where proprietary data and workflow context are the advantage. This isn&#8217;t just developer productivity&#8212;it&#8217;s a business model reset.</p><h3><strong>5) Sovereign data + on-prem is back&#8212;driven by both security and economics</strong></h3><p>The cloud conversation is no longer ideological. It&#8217;s pragmatic: sovereignty, control, predictable cost curves, and deciding where the blast radius lives. For many organizations, hybrid isn&#8217;t a transition state&#8212;it&#8217;s the operating model.</p><h3><strong>6) The &#8220;data center in a rack&#8221; can be a good architectural primitive</strong></h3><p>There&#8217;s momentum toward rack-scale building blocks: compute + storage + networking + security posture packaged as a deployable unit. If this becomes common, security has to become <strong>rack-native</strong> (and automation-native), not a retrofitted layer.</p><h3><strong>7) We need &#8220;auto-defenders&#8221; because humans-in-the-loop will become the bottleneck</strong></h3><p>Agentic systems compress timelines. If response and remediation still rely on humans approving every step, we create a permanent bottleneck right where speed matters most. <strong>It&#8217;s not widely built today&#8212;but it needs to be.</strong></p><h3><strong>8) First-principles test for any new technology: what becomes newly solvable&#8212;and what becomes a new problem</strong></h3><p>The right framing isn&#8217;t &#8220;is this good or bad?&#8221; It&#8217;s:</p><ul><li><p><strong>What problems could we not solve before that we can now solve?</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>What problems didn&#8217;t exist before that now become problems?</strong></p></li></ul><p>That second question matters more than people admit. New capability creates new attack surfaces, new failure modes, and new incentives.</p><h3><strong>9) We never fully solved IAM in the past and now we&#8217;re adding non-human Agent identities at massive scale</strong></h3><p>Identity and access management has been a chronic pain point for decades. Agentic AI makes it harder: agents aren&#8217;t employees, and they don&#8217;t map cleanly onto traditional service-account governance either. We&#8217;re walking into a world with <strong>no stable &#8220;human identity&#8221; anchor</strong>, and the consequences touch everything: authorization, audit, separation of duties, accountability, and incident response. We discussed this at Cloud Security Alliance white paper <a href="https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/artifacts/agentic-ai-identity-and-access-management-a-new-approach">here</a></p><h3><strong>10) OT breaks the classic vulnerability management model</strong></h3><p>Traditional vulnerability management doesn&#8217;t map cleanly onto operational technology. Patch tolerance, scanning tolerance, uptime constraints, and vendor dependencies change the whole playbook. The gap between &#8220;what we know how to do&#8221; and &#8220;what we need to do&#8221; is still too wide.</p><h2><strong>Thank you to the organizers and speakers (as listed on the GCIS agenda)</strong></h2><p>GCIS works because the organizers keep it senior, candid, and discussion-first.
