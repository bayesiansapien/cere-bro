# The Billionaire Tax Might Fail—For Now

<p><b>Welcome, Weekenders!</b></p><p>Next Tuesday promises to be something of a doozy.&nbsp;</p><p>At the moment, the Democrats <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-elections/midterms">are poised</a> to take back both the Senate and the House of Representatives and clean up nicely in gubernatorial races across the country.&nbsp;</p><p>And for those of you in California who haven’t decamped to <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/articles/billionaire-tax-refugees-flock-ritzy-nevada-lake-town?rc=sslhyj">the pine-studded shores of Nevada’s Incline Village</a>, we’ll find out whether you’ll need to find a good financial planner. Or a sharkish attorney.&nbsp;</p><p>Yes, Tuesday is billionaire tax judgment day, with California voters getting a chance to decide on Proposition 40, the ballot proposal that hopes to enact a one-time 5% tax on billion-dollar fortunes to fund healthcare and education in the state. The plan has caused great agita among the tech elite. And while I can’t imagine a tremendous number of the people reading this newsletter would favor the proposal, I suggest anyone on the fence—or already in support—should read what Rippling founder Parker Conrad <a href="https://x.com/parkerconrad/with_replies?lang=en">has been saying on X</a> to understand exactly why the idea seems somewhat harebrained.&nbsp;</p><p>The outcome looks tight. While polls from earlier this year <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/polls/california-wealth-tax-polls-2026.html">showed fairly strong support</a> for Prop 40, more recent ones suggest voters have turned more negative on the idea. A <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/21/california-billionaire-tax-loses-ground-new-poll-01086260">fresh poll</a> by Politico found 45% of likely voters in favor, 43% against it and 12% undecided.&nbsp;</p><p>Prediction-market traders take a dim view of Prop 40’s odds. Polymarket <a href="https://polymarket.com/event/billionaire-one-time-wealth-tax-passes-in-california-election-2026">scores</a> just 23% in favor, while Kalshi <a href="https://kalshi.com/markets/kxcawealthtax/will-the-california-billionaire-wealth-tax-initiative-pass/kxcawealthtax-26">shows</a> just about the same. Granted, prediction markets weren’t great leading indicators lately for election results in <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-10-28/brazil-s-election-shows-how-prediction-markets-can-deceive">Brazil</a> and <a href="https://www.timesofisrael.com/as-polls-show-deadlock-betting-markets-strongly-favor-eisenkot-to-replace-netanyahu/">Israel</a>.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>The weakening support among voters comes after a slew of Democratic-aligned groups and trade unions have voiced opposition to Prop 40, including Planned Parenthood, the California Teachers Association and the California Medical Association. Those organizations <a href="https://calmatters.org/politics/2026/08/billionaire-tax-california-democrats-split/">have varied criticisms</a> of Prop 40—one being that they think state lawmakers won’t actually get the money to healthcare and education. California Gov. Gavin Newsom has also said he opposes Prop 40.</p><p>Should Prop 40 fail, it’ll be a victory for the Sergey Brins and Patrick Collisons of the world, who’ve spent months campaigning against the proposal and committing enormous sums of money to ads and other measures to defeat it. The best-funded group dedicated to torpedoing Prop 40 is Building a Better California, which itself <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/27/technology/proposition-40-california-billionaire-tax.html">has raised</a> $330 million from Brin, Stripe’s Collison, former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, venture capitalist Vinod Khosla and Kleiner Perkins’ John Doerr, among many others. And even if Prop 40 passes, both supporters and opponents <a href="https://www.ntu.org/foundation/detail/a-rundown-of-the-legal-troubles-california-can-expect-from-prop-40">expect</a> a lengthy series of court battles over its constitutionality.&nbsp;</p><p>Prop 40 has been a constant matter of discussion in the conversations that Cooper Teboe, an indefatigable Democratic operative in California, has with wealthy donors. “It comes up 20 times a day,” he said. “Folks really, really care about it.”&nbsp;</p><p>The prospect of Prop 40’s approval has led some wealthy Californians to hesitate to make political donations to Democrats at a time when the newly reascendant party might hope to bring the tech elite back into the fold. “It has made some of them throw up their arms and just say ‘Fuck it’ to the Democrats,” Teboe said. “I’ve heard from a few people who have stopped giving money because they’re like, ‘OK, this wealth tax thing is going to wipe me out. I’m going to save every single dollar I can for this tax.’”&nbsp;</p><p>Even if Prop 40 fails, Teboe believes its mere existence is a harbinger of things to come and expects to see similar measures pop up around the country over the next few years. Teboe finds that many of the donors seem disconnected from the general unease most Americans feel about the state of the economy—a discontent over high inflation and the uncertainty of AI’s impact on jobs. The most contact those donors have with more ordinary mortals, Teboe said, is in “the conversations that they have with staff on the way to their Porsche.”<i>—Abram Brown (</i><a
