AI OVERUSE INDUCES MENTAL FATIGUE</h3><p>A recent <a href="https://www.bcg.com/news/5march2026-when-using-ai-leads-brain-fry">Boston Consulting Group study</a> published in <a href="https://hbr.org/2026/03/when-using-ai-leads-to-brain-fry">Harvard Business Review</a> found that over-engineered workflows are a prevalent mistake.</p><p>Workers using four or more AI agents <em>simultaneously</em>&#8212;often doing complex looping tasks with harnesses&#8212;reported the opposite: brain fog and a net feeling of being <em>less</em> productive than before. It leads to &#8220;employee errors, decision overload, and, ultimately, intent to quit.&#8221; Workers using three or fewer, in contrast, reported meaningful productivity gains.</p><p>BCG calls it &#8220;AI brain fry&#8221; (also known as AI-induced mental fatigue). Our biological brains have a limit to how much information they can hold at once. If you surpass this limit you will experience a fog that makes you unable to think clearly, marked by difficulty focusing, slower decisions, and headaches that force you to physically walk away from the screen.</p><p>This happens to me a lot nowadays. I&#8217;m researching a new topic every day, or asking Claude to explore a new idea. I have several tabs open with agents running, some doing loops on my archive (e.g., what topic have I not covered in a while), others doing a complex analysis task (e.g., what do my best articles have in common?). At some point&#8212;this happens every single day&#8212;I give up <em>control</em>. My mind &#8220;goes blank,&#8221; and I feel as if I were speeding down the hill riding a bike, but my feet are no longer on the pedals. Some days, I try to get my mind back on track, but others, I let the bike reach the bottom of the hill, forgetting in the process half the things I&#8217;m doing.</p><p>I have more Claude skills than I need, more ongoing chats/projects than I can possibly remember, and nearly 1,000 drafts sitting in my draft folder with half-baked ideas that I will never revise. The consequence is that I leave more tasks unfinished every week than I used to start at all before agents were a thing. I could read this as: &#8220;My productivity is so high that my short-term memory can no longer hold as many items in the mind at the same time.&#8221; Or I could say: &#8220;I&#8217;m saturating my capacity to keep track of what I&#8217;m doing.&#8221;</p><p>Agents drove to zero the friction to start, but not the friction to keep going.</p><h3>II. A FINITE POOL OF SHARED RESOURCES</h3><p>BCG surveyed 1,488 full-time US workers across industries and roles. The study measured several dimensions of how people use AI&#8212;number of tools, degree of oversight required, whether AI replaced tasks or added to the workload&#8212;and cross-referenced those against cognitive measures. The single strongest predictor of mental fatigue was <em>how much oversight the AI requires.</em></p><p>The people who had to monitor, verify, and babysit their AI tools were the ones getting fried. There&#8217;s just &#8220;too much going on for you to reasonably comprehend,&#8221; said a user of an agent-orchestrating platform. Fourteen percent of the AI-using workers in the study reported experiencing brain fry: 33% more decision fatigue, 39% more major errors at work, and a 39% increase in intent to quit.</p><p>You can always not monitor at all, yes, but that&#8217;s how you collect insane amounts of technical debt if you&#8217;re a developer or intellectual debt if you&#8217;re a writer, like me. Do you really want to put your name on something you ship to production without human supervision? AI agents are not there yet.</p><p>This is separate from using too many tools, a related but distinct problem <a href="https://www.thealgorithmicbridge.com/p/how-to-get-more-from-ai-by-using">that I covered recently</a>. The core problem is that intense AI supervision of simultaneous processes demands exactly the cognitive resources&#8212;sustained attention, working memory, executive control&#8212;that have hard biological limits. You may be running 10 agent instances, but your brain&#8217;s capacity can&#8217;t be divided. Nor can it be doubled, like you&#8217;d spin up another 10 instances with a bunch of clicks.</p><p>But wait, isn&#8217;t this the good ol&#8217; burnout? Not really.</p><p>Burnout is emotional exhaustion, the kind that builds over months from a bad boss or a toxic culture or work you find meaningless. It can happen with AI if you have to constantly edit wrong outputs or if you&#8217;re forced to use it to meet company standards or if you are exposed to too much &#8220;<a href="https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity">workslop</a>.&#8221;</p><p>Brain fry is, in contrast, an acute cognitive strain. It comes from marshalling attention and working memory beyond their capacity. It happens in hours, sometimes minutes.</p><p>Actually, the study found that when AI is used to replace repetitive tasks, burnout scores&#8212;but not brain fry scores&#8212;go <em>down</em> by 15%. The same technology can reduce emotional exhaustion while simultaneously causing cognitive overload.</p><h3>III. IS BRAIN FRY GOING AWAY?</h3><p>I think we&#8217;re in a temporary phase in terms of current practices of AI use, particularly agents, but I want to be precise about what makes it temporary (and what might be permanent).</p><p>The temporary part: we haven&#8217;t yet developed the habits and best practices for managing multiple agents (to be clear, I don&#8217;t think we have them for one agent either; we&#8217;re learning as we go). AI management is essentially trial and error, a sort of &#8220;AI alchemy,&#8221; which means we oscillate between too much control (e.g., &#8220;nothing ships without my supervision&#8221;) and too little (e.g., &#8220;the only metric should be how many tokens you&#8217;re spending&#8221;).</p><p>But I&#8217;m not worried about this: the best practices will come, the way they came for email and phones, and firearms and cars and every other tech that used to overwhelm us.</p><p>The part that might not be temporary, however, and thus worries me a bit, is that the ceiling on how many agents you can oversee is set by a &#8220;fixed&#8221; human biology, and AI capability is growing faster than human adaptability. (It&#8217;s not fixed in absolute terms, but natural selection is damn slow.)</p><p>What I mean is that we are not octopi. We didn&#8217;t evolve to cope with a swarm of eight moving parts running independently of a central processing unit. Having one agent do one task is mostly manageable. Having a swarm doing several things at once while you try to keep the whole picture in your head will run you into a memory wall and a processing power wall and an energy wall, etc.</p><p>Working memory and attentional bandwidth and metabolic rate all have hard biological limits, and unlike AI capability, those limits don&#8217;t improve with the next model release.</p><p>This is exactly the opposite of what I imagine happens to smart people when their brain goes faster than their hands or their mouth, and so they can&#8217;t write or speak as fast as they&#8217;d like; it feels like a serious bottleneck. What we&#8217;re experiencing is the same thing <em>in reverse:</em> the hands and the mouth now go faster than the brain, which is, in turn, the bottleneck.</p><p>AI is pushing our brains to their biological limits.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2"
