# Amazon’s Trainium Takes a Strike at the Training King

<p>Trainium has already proven itself in the realm of inference. Anthropic is running production inference and RL rollouts on Trainium2 and Trainium3. Historically, Trainium has been great for inference but not the best fit for research training. In inference, the model architecture changes at a much slower pace compared to training, where researchers iterate on many different experiments and ablations of model architecture and need a stack that covers a broad API surface. In the past, that is where Trainium fell short.</p><p>The AWS Neuron team is aggressively working to change this pitfall with the biggest shift being moving towards a first-class, native PyTorch training experience. Gone are the days of sprinkling xm.step() through your training loop and wrestling with a bespoke XLA-style execution model. Trainium now runs PyTorch the way PyTorch users expect, in eager mode, with torch.compile and the standard APIs.</p><p>The SemiAnalysis engineering team has been trying out the Trainium3 racks for the past couple of weeks and has found the experience to be great &#8211; we love the overall direction of where it is heading. To be clear, it isn&#8217;t perfect yet, and we have personally run into a couple of bugs ourselves. It&#8217;s nowhere close to the maturity of the CUDA ecosystem, but the direction is right. Their team is making rapid progress with the help of agentic coding.</p><p>Furthermore, unlike other accelerator companies, where we had to fight tooth and nail to get their engineers a reasonably stable CI fleet, Trainium uses up to 80,000 physical cores allocated towards automated quality control.</p><p>In this article, we walk through our experience on the Trainium3 TorchTitan and TorchNeuron stack, along with our experience of Trainium&#8217;s Neuron Explorer profiler, which is way better than both Nvidia&#8217;s and AMD&#8217;s profilers. TorchNeuron represents a native PyTorch experience instead of duct-taping Trainium onto PyTorch through the PyTorch/XLA project. The new backend is aimed at everyone who isn&#8217;t a frontier-lab kernel engineer. AWS has even hired the lead of Google&#8217;s native Torch on TPU effort to work on native Torch for Trainium. It also didn&#8217;t support PyTorch&#8217;s native distributed APIs (torch.distributed.*) or its native parallelism APIs (DTensor, SimpleFSDP, DDP). Users had to rely on out-of-tree XLA SPMD APIs such as torch_xla.experimental.spmd_fsdp and torch_xla.distributed.spmd. popular open-source pre-training frameworks, and we tested Neuron&#8217;s support to gauge the user experience of a researcher training a non-trivial model with Trainium. Specifically, we trained a Qwen3-32B on 16 Trainium2 chips, 64 LNC=2 logical cores in total, and we also tried doing it on a rack-scale Trainium3 UltraServer with 64 physical chips.</p><p>Overall, we liked the user experience, though there were some rough edges that the Amazon team already has on its roadmap to fix.</p><p>While we will provide the Trainium2 MFU below for completeness, the part to focus on is that for ML research ablations, what matters most isn&#8217;t a speed-of-light-optimized stack but decent performance and broad support across model architecture modifications, so researchers can iterate without fighting the stack.</p><p>With Neuron&#8217;s official TorchTitan image, we were able to launch the run without any issues. We measured <strong>11,099 tokens per second in average training throughput on eager mode, at 23.89% MFU</strong>. For compiled mode, we saw <strong>14,156 tokens per second and 30.47% MFU, a 1.28x throughput improvement over eager mode.</strong> We tried changing the model architecture slightly in the modeling code and it worked great also.</p><p>Neuron also offers training recipes for more modern model architectures, so we tested its gpt-oss 20B training recipe. Neuron supports gpt-oss training through integration with <a href="https://github.com/pytorch/torchtitan/blob/main/torchtitan/experiments/graph_trainer/README.md">GraphTrainer</a>, TorchTitan&#8217;s experimental trainer module. Specifically, Neuron adds Trainium-compatible configurations of SimpleFSDP, a compilation backend for PyTorch computation graph capture, and custom NKI kernels for attention and MoE operations. In terms of user experience, the recipe worked out of the box, and everything went well.</p><p>One thing that was not yet implemented using the upstream APIs was TokenSwitch. In the age of MoE, for inference and training, MoE combine and MoE dispatch are among the most important comms ops and take anywhere from 60-70% of the communication volume, and TokenSwitch is PyTorch&#8217;s first-class API for them. Trainium doesn&#8217;t support it yet and instead relies on a custom TorchTitan class called NeuronEPTokenDispatcher. Moving it onto the TokenSwitch API looks like a simple fix, and the AWS team has committed to collaborating with the Meta PyTorch team to modify the TokenSwitch ABC such that it can support Trainium too.</p><p>The part where it was slightly disappointing was the Neuron compiler took a long time to compile, leading to a long wait in the first step. Concretely, the first step took 184.6 seconds for eager mode (255.7 seconds for compiled mode), which is 30-55x longer than the average step time, excluding the first step. For gpt-oss, this training run came at a ridiculously high compilation time of 27 minutes and 17 seconds, which leads to a terrible user experience, especially for researchers who experiment with new model architectures. The reason for this is that historically the Trainium compiler stack has mainly been focused on inference, where compile time doesn&#8217;t matter because compilation is done offline and cached once per image and doesn&#8217;t affect production inference, whereas in training it does. While this is the right call for inference at the time, for researchers iterating on model architecture, that&#8217;s a meaningful friction point.</p><p>The Neuron team is already addressing this directly. Upcoming TorchNeuron releases introduce a new compiler backend with a broad set of precompiled NKI kernels and a compile stack re-architected for fast compilation, targeting up to a 2 orders of magnitude reduction in time-to-first-step for both eager and compiled modes, with minimal or no performance regression.
