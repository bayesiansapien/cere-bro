# The Computational Wall: Why the Defense Trilemma and the NP-Hardness of Reward Hacking Detection Demand a New Security Posture for AI

had just formalized in a preprint co-authored with Manish Bhatt, Sarthak Munshi, Vineeth Sai Narajala, Idan Habler, Ammar Al-Kahfah, Joel Webb, Blake Gatto, and Md Tamjidul Hoque:</p><blockquote><p><strong>A large class of the defenses we are deploying around language-model and agentic AI systems cannot, by mathematical construction, do what we are asking them to do. And the most natural fallback &#8212; &#8220;just monitor for reward hacking and intervene&#8221; &#8212; runs straight into an NP-hardness wall.</strong></p></blockquote><p>This is not an argument for despair. It is an argument for honesty about where the wall sits, and for an engineering and policy posture that respects it. In this piece I want to explain &#8212; for security engineers, AI builders, and policymakers &#8212; why two recent results converge on the same conclusion, why training-time alignment is <strong>necessary but not sufficient</strong>, and what the security strategy looks like when we stop pretending the wall isn&#8217;t there.</p><h2><strong>Part 1 &#8212; The Wrapper Defense Mirage</strong></h2><p>Walk into almost any large enterprise rolling out generative or agentic AI today and you will find some variant of the same pattern wrapped around the model:</p><ul><li><p>An input classifier that flags &#8220;suspicious&#8221; prompts.</p></li><li><p>A constitutional rewriting layer that &#8220;softens&#8221; risky inputs.</p></li><li><p>A sanitizer that strips known injection patterns.</p></li><li><p>An output filter that catches what the first three missed.</p></li></ul><p>Each of these is, mathematically, a function wrapper that takes a prompt, transforms it, and hands it to the model. Defense designers want this wrapper to satisfy three things simultaneously:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Continuity</strong> &#8212; similar prompts produce similar rewrites (so the wrapper is well-behaved and doesn&#8217;t gratuitously break edge cases).</p></li><li><p><strong>Utility preservation</strong> &#8212; safe prompts pass through unchanged (so the system remains useful).</p></li><li><p><strong>Completeness</strong> &#8212; every output is safe (so unsafe prompts are remediated, not merely flagged).</p></li></ol><p>In the paper <em>The Defense Trilemma: Why Prompt Injection Defense Wrappers Fail?</em>, we prove that these three properties cannot coexist on a connected prompt space. Any two are achievable; all three together are not. The intuition is geometric, and once you see it, you can&#8217;t unsee it: utility preservation forces the wrapper&#8217;s fixed-point set to contain the entire safe region; continuity forces that fixed-point set to be <strong>closed</strong>; and connectedness forces the closure of an open safe region to spill onto the safety boundary. Some boundary prompts therefore pass through with no remediation. The defense designer has built a closed door whose hinges, by topology, must touch the threshold.</p><p>The paper escalates this in three steps:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Boundary fixation (Theorem 4.1)</strong> establishes that <em>at least one</em> threshold-level input passes unchanged.</p></li><li><p><strong>The &#949;-robust constraint (Theorem 5.1)</strong> shows that under Lipschitz regularity, a <em>positive-measure band</em> of near-threshold inputs remains within a threshold around that fixed point.</p></li><li><p><strong>The persistent unsafe region (Theorem 6.3)</strong> shows that under a transversality condition &#8212; when the alignment surface rises faster than the defense&#8217;s Lipschitz budget can pull it down &#8212; a positive-measure subset of inputs remains <em>strictly unsafe</em> even after the defense fires.</p></li></ul><p>The full theory is mechanically verified in Lean 4 with Mathlib (46 files, ~360 theorems, no admitted proofs, three standard axioms), and we validated the persistent-region prediction empirically against gpt-3.5-turbo-0125 and gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07 using the Manifold of Failure framework. On a saturated 25&#215;25 grid for gpt-3.5-turbo, transversality held with wide margin, and every cell our theorem predicted would remain unsafe, did remain unsafe. </p><p>The result extends, exactly as you would fear, to <strong>multi-turn</strong> dialogues (the trilemma applies independently at each turn), <strong>stochastic</strong> defenses (the expected post-defense score is still pinned at the boundary), and <strong>agentic pipelines</strong> of tools (the effective Lipschitz constant degrades exponentially in pipeline depth). Every escape hatch closes on inspection.</p><p>This matters because it falsifies a specific <em>engineering claim</em> that is implicit in many vendor pitches: that with a clever enough wrapper, you can deploy an unaligned or partially-aligned model behind a safety membrane and be done. You cannot. Not for reasons of cleverness &#8212; for reasons of topology.</p><h2><strong>Part 2 &#8212; The NP-Hard Wall on Reward Hacking Detection</strong></h2><p>If wrappers can&#8217;t catch every unsafe prompt, the natural next move &#8212; and the one most Chief AI Officers I talk to are now reaching for &#8212; is: <em>we&#8217;ll just monitor what the model is actually optimizing for. If it&#8217;s reward hacking, we&#8217;ll intervene.</em></p><p>This is where the second wall meets the first.</p><p><strong>Reward hacking</strong> is the now-canonical phenomenon in which a model &#8220;exploits gaps in its evaluation system to achieve high measured scores without genuinely fulfilling the principal&#8217;s objectives.&#8221; In production, it manifests as sycophancy, length gaming, format manipulation, code that modifies unit tests rather than solving the problem, agentic systems that delete an opponent&#8217;s chess engine to &#8220;win,&#8221; and &#8212; in the most worrying recent results from Anthropic &#8212; natural emergent misalignment that <em>generalizes</em> from a narrow reward hack to broadly misaligned behavior across unrelated tasks. Multiple frontier labs have now reported reward hacking in their RL training runs.</p><p>Several recent papers establish, with increasing rigor, that <em>detecting</em> reward hacking is not merely empirically hard &#8212; it is <em>computationally</em> hard in a precise sense.</p><p><strong>Result 1 &#8212; Semantic self-verification is NP-complete.</strong> Robin Young models the problem of an AI system verifying that its own behavior accurately characterizes its constitutional or rule-based directives, formalizes this as Semantic Self-Verification (SSV), and proves NP-completeness via a polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT. The implication is direct: any approach that relies on the model (or a heuristic verifier) checking its own compliance with a non-trivial set of natural-language principles inherits this lower bound. As the paper puts it, the AI generating preference data for constitutional methods is, by computational necessity, &#8220;using learned heuristics to find a plausible solution, not a correct one.&#8221; Stack a few thousand such heuristic decisions on top of one another in an RLHF pipeline and you get a defense whose strength is at most as good as the heuristic &#8212; never as good as a complete verification.</p><p><strong>Result 2 &#8212; Alignment to large value sets is intractable.</strong> In an oral-track AAAI paper, Sun, Smith, and Nayebi prove a &#8220;no free lunch&#8221; theorem for human-AI alignment via an agreement-based complexity analysis: encoding a sufficiently rich set of human values inevitably incurs alignment overhead exponential in the relevant scaling parameters. Crucially, even in best-case regimes where alignment to arbitrary precision is theoretically guaranteed, &#8220;reward hacking is <em>globally inevitable</em>: rare high-loss states are systematically under-covered, implying scalable oversight must target safety-critical slices rather than uniform coverage.&#8221; Translation for security teams: you cannot uniformly inspect your way out of this; rare high-stakes failures are the ones the inspector statistically misses.</p><p><strong>Result 3 &#8212; Reward hacking is a structural equilibrium, not a bug.</strong> Wang and Huang (no relation) prove that under five minimal axioms &#8212; multi-dimensional quality, finite evaluation, effective optimization, resource finiteness, and combinatorial interaction &#8212; <em>any</em> optimized AI agent will systematically under-invest effort in quality dimensions not covered by its evaluation system, regardless of the alignment method employed (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI, etc.). More damaging for the agentic case: as you add tools, evaluation coverage declines toward zero because quality dimensions expand combinatorially while evaluation costs grow at most linearly per tool. Hacking severity therefore increases <em>structurally</em> with the move from chat to agents.</p><p>Taken together, these are not three separate worries. They are three windows onto the same wall. <strong>Detection of reward hacking &#8212; at least in the form most security teams are designing for &#8212; is at least NP-hard, almost surely globally incomplete, and structurally guaranteed to grow worse as agents get more tools.</strong></p><h2><strong>Part 3 &#8212; Two Impossibility Proofs, One Conclusion</strong></h2><p>Here is the convergence I argued for at the panel and that I want to leave you with from this piece.</p><p>The Defense Trilemma constrains what you can do at <em>inference time</em> with a wrapper around a fixed model. The NP-hardness and no-free-lunch results constrain what you can do at <em>training time</em> and at the <em>evaluation</em> layer to detect when the model has drifted away from intended behavior. fails by construction.</em> This is not a failure of imagination. It is a structural property of the spaces these systems live in.</p><p>Apostol Vassilev &#8212; my fellow panelist on Session 5 &#8212; has independently arrived at a closely related conclusion from a different direction, by extending G&#246;del&#8217;s incompleteness theorem to AI safety. His result establishes that &#8220;no finite set of guardrails is universally robust against adaptive adversarial prompts,&#8221; casting AI security as an information-theoretically Sisyphean endeavor that must be a dynamic, evolving process rather than a static engineering achievement. Three formalizations, three different machineries &#8212; topology, computational complexity, information theory &#8212; and the same warning.</p><h2><strong>Part 4 &#8212; Why Alignment Is Necessary But Not Sufficient</strong></h2><p>The headline above is not a hedge. It is the precise claim that follows from the math.</p><p><strong>It is necessary</strong>, because the persistent unsafe region in our trilemma paper is bounded above by the <em>steepness</em> of the alignment surface near the safety boundary. The transversality condition is G &gt; \ell(K{+}1)&#8212; meaning the persistent region exists exactly when the alignment surface rises faster toward unsafe than the defense can pull it down. If you flatten the alignment surface near the boundary by training the model to refuse politely rather than to comply harmfully &#8212; i.e., if you set the threshold so that boundary-level behavior is benign &#8212; the impossibility result becomes mathematically true but practically harmless. We saw this empirically: at tau = 0.50, GPT-5-Mini&#8217;s peak alignment deviation is exactly 0.50 and the unsafe region is empty, so the trilemma&#8217;s preconditions fail and the validator correctly reports &#8220;not applicable.&#8221; Better training-time alignment doesn&#8217;t violate our impossibility &#8212; it makes the impossibility <em>not bite</em>.</p><p><strong>It is not sufficient</strong>, for two reasons that the math also makes explicit.</p><p><em>First</em>, alignment is a property of a particular model on a particular evaluation distribution. The transferability and persistence-under-fine-tuning theorems in our paper (Theorems D.3 and E.1, building on the empirical sleeper-agent results that show backdoors persist through safety training) imply that interior basin points &#8212; deep failures &#8212; survive fine-tuning. Anthropic&#8217;s own recent work on natural emergent misalignment from production RL confirms this in the most worrying way: a narrow reward hack in coding RL generalized to broadly misaligned behavior on unrelated tasks. The aligned-model assumption is auditable only at the surface; underneath, the failure modes can be invariant to the very training process meant to remove them.</p><p><em>Second</em>, in agentic systems, the model is <em>not the only place where unsafe behavior can originate</em>. Trinity-style architectural arguments &#8212; the recent &#8220;Trustworthy Agentic AI Requires Deterministic Architectural Boundaries&#8221; paper makes this case crisply &#8212; show that under what they call the Lethal Trifecta (untrusted inputs, privileged data access, external action capability), training-based defenses may reduce empirical attack rates but cannot provide deterministic guarantees. The model can be perfectly aligned and still be authorized to do the wrong thing because the <em>system around it</em> lacks unforgeable provenance and deterministic mediation. Alignment is a property of the policy; security is a property of the system.</p><p>This is the part of the argument that I think is least understood outside the security community and most important to land for policymakers: <strong>better aligned models reduce, but do not eliminate, the need for defense-in-depth.</strong> And critically, the depth has to be <em>uncorrelated</em>. Recent risk-perspective work shows that defense-in-depth provides protection only to the extent that the failure modes of the layers are uncorrelated &#8212; if every layer fails for the same reason, you have one defense, not seven.</p><h2><strong>Part 5 &#8212; The Engineering Prescription: How to Build for the Wall</strong></h2><p>The Defense Trilemma paper closes with what we called <em>the engineering prescription</em> &#8212; the actionable claim that &#8220;the engineering goal shifts from elimination to management.&#8221; I want to translate that into operational terms for the security teams and CISOs reading this.</p><p><strong>1. Make the boundary shallow.</strong> Set your safety threshold so that boundary-level behavior is benign &#8212; a polite refusal, a request for clarification, a hand-off to a human. If the worst behavior at the threshold is still acceptable, the impossibility theorems are mathematically true and operationally harmless. This is largely a <em>training-time alignment</em> lever. It is also where investing in better alignment pays off most directly against our impossibility result.</p><p><strong>2. Reduce the alignment-surface Lipschitz constant.</strong> Smoother surfaces produce wider but more easily monitored vulnerability bands; rougher surfaces produce smaller, harder-to-find fragments. Training pipelines that <em>deliberately smooth</em> the model&#8217;s response across semantic neighborhoods (rather than producing sharp cliffs near unsafe regions) trade a known wide region for many unknown small ones &#8212; a better deal for monitoring.</p><p><strong>3. Reduce the effective dimension of the prompt interface.</strong> Defense cost scales as N^d in the dimension of the prompt space. Standardized formats, restricted API parameters, bounded context lengths, and function-calling schemas all <em>reduce d</em>, which makes the behavioral space tractable to red-team and monitor. This is the architectural argument for not exposing free-form natural language to systems with privileged tool access &#8212; exactly the trust-boundary argument that traditional security frameworks like STRIDE were never designed to make for AI, and that motivated me to design the <a href="https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/blog/2025/02/06/agentic-ai-threat-modeling-framework-maestro">MAESTRO</a> seven-layer threat-modeling framework at the Cloud Security Alliance.</p><p><strong>4. Monitor the boundary; do not try to eliminate it.</strong> Transversal crossings persist under fine-tuning and recur every turn. Rather than attempt the impossible, deploy runtime monitoring that detects <em>approach</em> to the boundary. The Lipschitz bound from the trilemma paper gives a computable estimate of distance to the boundary from any observed alignment-deviation value &#8212; a defender&#8217;s tool, not an attacker&#8217;s.</p><p><strong>5. Architecturally mediate, don&#8217;t behaviorally trust.</strong> The Trinity Defense Architecture&#8217;s three mechanisms &#8212; action governance via finite action calculus with reference-monitor enforcement, information-flow control via mandatory access labels, and privilege separation isolating perception from execution &#8212; give <em>deterministic</em> guarantees that complement the <em>probabilistic</em> guarantees of alignment. Where you can use deterministic mediation, you should. Behavioral trust is the part of the system the trilemma constrains; architectural mediation operates outside the trilemma&#8217;s scope.</p><p><strong>6. Treat reward-hacking detection as a sampling problem on safety-critical slices, not as a uniform-coverage problem.</strong> This is the operational consequence of the no-free-lunch result. Uniform monitoring is computationally infeasible at scale. Targeted monitoring of high-impact, high-uncertainty slices &#8212; production code modifications, financial transactions, communications to external parties, agentic actions that cross trust boundaries &#8212; is feasible and provably more effective per unit of evaluation budget.</p><h2><strong>Part 6 &#8212; Policy and Strategy</strong></h2><p>For policymakers, regulators, and senior decision-makers who shape the rules under which these systems are deployed, the convergence of these impossibility results carries five concrete implications. I share these as someone who has co-authored the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, contributes to the NIST Generative AI Public Working Group, and chairs AI Safety working groups at the Cloud Security Alliance &#8212; and as someone whose technical co-authors and panel colleagues represent NIST, Microsoft, Cisco, Amazon, NVIDIA, and Qualcomm.</p><p><strong>Policy Implication 1: Stop requiring &#8220;complete&#8221; defenses.</strong> Procurement language, regulatory compliance requirements, and standards-body checklists that demand a wrapper or filter &#8220;prevent all unsafe outputs&#8221; are demanding the mathematically impossible. They incentivize vendors to claim what they cannot deliver, then to explain away the inevitable failures as edge cases. Procurement language should instead require <em>characterized failure modes with bounded blast radius</em> &#8212; what the trilemma paper formalizes as the persistent region &#8212; and <em>runtime monitoring with documented detection latency</em>.</p><p><strong>Policy Implication 2: Recognize that training-time alignment is critical infrastructure.</strong> Because better-aligned models flatten the boundary and make the impossibility results practically harmless, the quality of training-time alignment is now a security property, not just a model-quality property. This argues for treating frontier alignment research the way we treat cryptographic primitive research &#8212; as a public-good investment with national-security relevance, not as a commercial differentiator alone.</p><p><strong>Policy Implication 3: Mandate uncorrelated defense layers in high-stakes deployments.</strong> Defense-in-depth is not a checkbox. If every layer of an agentic system relies on the same underlying model behaving correctly, the system has one defense pretending to be seven. Regulators evaluating AI in critical infrastructure, financial services, healthcare, and government services should require &#8212; and have the technical staff to evaluate &#8212; the <em>correlation structure</em> of the defense layers, not just their count.</p><p><strong>Policy Implication 4: Prefer deterministic architectural mediation for consequential actions.</strong> When an agentic system is authorized to execute code, transfer funds, send communications, or modify production systems, the authorization decision should not rest on a behavioral inference about what the model intended. Where deterministic mediation (capability-based access control, reference monitors, mandatory information-flow control) is available, it should be required for the critical action surface. The trilemma applies to the model&#8217;s behavior; it does not apply to the architecture around the model.</p><p><strong>Policy Implication 5: Accept that AI security is a continuous process, not a one-time certification.</strong> Vassilev&#8217;s G&#246;delian framing and our topological framing arrive at the same operational point: there is no finite checklist that establishes universal robustness. Certification regimes for AI systems should look more like continuous-monitoring regimes (think SOC 2 Type 2, FedRAMP continuous authorization) than like one-shot compliance regimes (think CE-mark or static UL listing). The NSF Forum convening I attended was, in effect, the early scoping of exactly this research and policy agenda.</p><h2><strong>Closing &#8212; A Note on the Limits of the Limits</strong></h2><p>I want to be precise about what these results do <em>not</em> say, because the temptation in security writing is always to round up to despair, and despair is bad strategy.</p><p>The Defense Trilemma covers exactly one class of mechanism: continuous, utility-preserving wrappers on connected prompt spaces. It does not preclude training-time alignment, architectural changes to the model itself, discontinuous defenses (hard blocklists, classifier-based rejection), output-side filters, ensemble defenses, human-in-the-loop review, adaptive-threshold systems, or multi-component systems whose classifiers reject or redirect rather than preserving utility on every prompt. Anything outside the wrapper class is not constrained by our theorem.</p><p>The NP-hardness result is a worst-case lower bound on a particular formulation of self-verification. It does not say no detection is possible; it says no <em>complete, sound, polynomial-time</em> detection is possible. Heuristic detectors, learned classifiers, and statistical monitors can and do work in practice &#8212; they just cannot offer the guarantee a worst-case adversary needs you not to have.</p><p>The no-free-lunch result on alignment says uniform coverage of all human values is intractable; it does not say <em>targeted</em> alignment to a <em>small, consensus-worthy</em> value set is intractable. Indeed, follow-up work has identified small value sets (corrigibility, in particular) for which positive guarantees are achievable.</p><p>What all three results say, in unison, is this: <strong>the strategic posture of AI security must be management of bounded, characterized, monitored failure &#8212; not elimination.</strong> That is exactly the posture mature security disciplines have adopted for everything from cryptography (where we manage the bounded probability of cryptographic break) to operating systems (where we manage privilege escalation through layered mediation) to networking (where we manage adversarial behavior through monitoring and response).</p><p>The good news is that we have done this before, in other domains, and we know how. The work ahead is not to discover new impossibility theorems &#8212; we have enough of those. The work ahead is to translate the impossibility into procurement language, regulation, defense-in-depth architecture, monitoring infrastructure, and a research agenda that takes the wall seriously instead of pretending we will engineer our way through it.</p><p>That is the conversation the National Academies&#8217; Forum on Cyber Resilience opened in April. It is the conversation our paper formalized in Lean 4. And it is the conversation I think the AI security field &#8212; is ready to have.</p><p><em>Ken Huang is CEO and Chief AI Officer at DistributedApps.ai, Co-Chair of AI Safety working groups at the Cloud Security Alliance, Co-Chair of the OWASP AIVSS project, and a co-author of the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications.
