# Brief #31: What China’s new AI export controls really mean (spoiler: not the end of open weight)

<p><strong>In this issue, we focus on one big topic: the intricacies of China&#8217;s proposed AI export controls. We close with a few other notable developments, including how Moonshot AI filters cyber offense data, and how the government aims to address AI-driven unemployment.</strong></p><h2>The proposed AI export controls</h2><p><strong>What happened:</strong> China has proposed rules that would give the government a say over whether certain AI technologies can be exported. A draft revision of the <em><a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2026/art_0a31a3c9ac404f7a9b0e917ce3412997.html">Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited or Restricted from Export</a></em> released by the Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) and the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) on October 16 would add 40 items to the Catalogue, of which 23 are AI-related, covering certain large language models, agents, chip designs, compute clusters and offensive cyber tooling (the remaining 17 items relate to biomedicine and photovoltaics).</p><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/subscribe?"><span>Subscribe now</span></a></p><p><strong>These rules are complicated, and we unpack all the details below. But first, our high-level take:</strong></p><ul><li><p>Some Chinese frontier models and agents will likely need a license to be exported. Crucially, this is not the end of Chinese open models, for which MOFCOM promises &#8220;automatic licenses.&#8221; The immediate impact on frontier developers could be surprisingly limited.</p></li><li><p>MOFCOM explicitly cites AI-enabled cyberattacks and misuse by non-state actors as motivations for the update.</p></li><li><p>The bigger story is that this would introduce a new regulatory tool. Until now, China&#8217;s binding AI rules have come mainly from the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) and regulated AI <em>as a service offered to the public within China</em>. This catalogue would regulate the technology itself (model weights, checkpoints, architecture, agent harnesses) at the point where it leaves China, and would bring MOFCOM and MOST into the picture.<a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-anchor-1" target="_self">1</a></p></li><li><p><strong>In short: China is moving to add a new tool to its AI regulatory toolbox. That doesn&#8217;t mean it will use that tool as a hammer to crush frontier open models anytime soon.</strong></p></li></ul><h4><strong>Now, into the details.</strong></h4><h4><strong>Is this even about AI safety?</strong></h4><p><strong>Yes. MOFCOM <a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfb/xwfyrth/art/2026/art_d7661dbf62794345a221404f82a25959.html">explicitly said</a> the proposed rules are motivated by AI safety, in particular misuse by non-state actors and malicious entities:</strong></p><blockquote><p>&#8220;The various inherent and derivative risks arising from AI have already drawn a high degree of attention from countries around the world. Among the AI technologies proposed for control on this occasion, technologies such as AI-driven autonomous generation and mutation of malicious code can be used to attack critical information infrastructure, defense and military industry, core government affairs and other sensitive systems, or to bypass the national security defense system. Were they to be acquired by non-state actors or malicious entities, they could cause harm to national security and to the common security of humanity.&#8221; (emphasis added)</p></blockquote><p>This follows months of growing concern about AI-enabled cyber risks inside the Chinese bureaucracy, voiced, for example, in the <a href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/i/208293360/chairs-statement-names-specific-frontier-risks-including-cybersecurity-and-terrorist-misuse">Chair Statement of the World AI Conference</a> and a <a href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/brief-28-chinas-ai-regulator-flags">press conference of China&#8217;s lead AI regulator</a>. <strong>While those were statements of intent, the draft MOFCOM rules would put regulatory teeth behind concerns about AI-enabled cyber risks.</strong></p><p><strong>AI safety is likely not the only motivation, though. </strong>Chinese <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/jJ36kwHrzC-TQSxJ4kXDmQ">legal commentators</a> also frame the revision in terms of economic and supply-chain security and protecting technologies of strategic value.<a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-anchor-2" target="_self">2</a> A CCTV-affiliated <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/hoeaIXEFagSHugSgMjFe9g">commentary</a> noted that as China reaches the frontier in more fields, managing its own technology has become a new priority after years of defending against foreign blockades.</p><h4><strong>What does the process actually look like?</strong></h4><p><strong>What is already in effect now: </strong>Since the draft&#8217;s release, exporters must register (&#30331;&#35760;) any technology that is on the Catalogue. This is just a temporary mechanism for regulators to gather information until the Catalogue is finalized. While some Chinese lawyers <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/UsuQHDO5xiHAS_1p_RSynA">suggest</a> authorities could use this registration process to suspend exports in especially sensitive cases, this is <strong>different</strong> from the eventual licensing process. According to a <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/hoeaIXEFagSHugSgMjFe9g">state-affiliated social media account</a>, DeepSeek and MiniMax have already &#8220;smoothly&#8221; completed this registration (presumably for their <a href="https://huggingface.co/MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-M3.1-Flash">latest</a> <a href="https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017">models</a> released since the draft Catalogue was published).</p><p><strong>When the Catalogue is finalized and comes into effect, the process will become more complex. </strong>Not everything in the Catalogue would be &#8220;banned&#8221; from export; the majority of items could still be legally exported with a license. (For a full list of the AI-related items in the Catalogue, see the Appendix at the end of this post.) There are two tiers:</p><ul><li><p><strong>6 items with J codes (&#31105;, e.g. 266402J) &#8212; prohibited. </strong>You simply cannot export them.</p></li><li><p><strong>17 items with X codes (&#38480;, e.g. 266404X) &#8212; restricted. </strong>You need a license to export them.</p></li></ul><p>Our diagram breaks it down:</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a into the prohibited or restricted tier.</strong></p><p>Even for &#8220;restricted&#8221; items, this does give MOFCOM a significant say. You only need to remember the TikTok story to see that obtaining a license is not always straightforward. In 2020, the catalogue <a href="http://images.mofcom.gov.cn/fms/202008/20200828200911003.pdf">was revised</a> to add, among other items, &#8220;recommendation of personalized information services based on data analysis,&#8221; which gave Beijing an effective veto over any sale of TikTok&#8217;s US business. ByteDance <a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/cms_files/filemanager/xwfbhzhibo/zhibo_1350.html">applied for an export license</a> for its recommendation algorithms that year but never obtained it. It was only in early 2026, more than five years later, that ByteDance licensed the algorithm <a href="https://newsroom.tiktok.com/announcement-from-the-new-tiktok-usds-joint-venture-llc?lang=en">to a US joint venture</a>.</p><p><strong>But most cases of exporting &#8220;restricted&#8221; AI technologies will likely be far smoother than the TikTok case.</strong></p><h4>The elephant in the room: open models</h4><p><strong>MOFCOM <a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfb/xwfyrth/art/2026/art_d7661dbf62794345a221404f82a25959.html">has been explicit</a> that the revision is not meant to change China&#8217;s supportive stance on open-source:</strong></p><blockquote><p>&#8220;The Chinese government is committed to making AI an important source of momentum for promoting common prosperity and safeguarding common security. In order to support the building of an open-source innovation ecosystem for AI, facilitation measures such as automatic licensing will apply to technology exports in circumstances such as the open-sourcing of AI-category technologies. &#8230; Relevant technologies that have already entered the public domain through open source, the publication of academic papers and similar means are proposed not to be brought within the scope of export management, so as to create a favorable environment for international exchange and cooperation on AI technology.&#8221;</p></blockquote><p>There are essentially two separate types of favorable treatment for open-source here:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Full exemption for </strong><em><strong>already open-sourced</strong></em><strong> AI technologies: </strong>The rules would not retroactively apply to technologies that have already been open-sourced.</p></li><li><p><strong>&#8220;Automatic licenses&#8221; for </strong><em><strong>yet-to-be open-sourced </strong></em><strong>AI technologies</strong><em><strong> in the &#8220;restricted&#8221; category:</strong> </em>These are not &#8220;exempt&#8221; from the regulation, so they will need to get a license. But practically speaking, getting a license to open-source AI in the &#8220;restricted&#8221; category should be very simple, with what MOFCOM calls &#8220;file and go&#8221; (&#21363;&#25253;&#21363;&#20986;) licensing. However, items in the &#8220;prohibited&#8221; category cannot be exported by definition.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Is open-sourcing even considered an export?</strong> The actual rules are somewhat ambiguous,<a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-3" id="footnote-anchor-3" target="_self">3</a> but Chinese lawyers seem relatively confident that this is indeed the case. As one <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/UsuQHDO5xiHAS_1p_RSynA">put it</a>:</p><blockquote><p>&#8220;In the past, the compliance boundary around open-sourcing technology was fairly blurred. This revision explicitly identifies &#8216;open-sourcing&#8217; as a form of technology export &#8212; that is, open-sourcing controlled technology to overseas parties is equivalent to a technology export.&#8221;</p></blockquote><p><strong>This suggests open-sourcing models is considered an &#8220;export.&#8221; The real-world impact then hinges on whether frontier open models would fall within the &#8220;restricted&#8221; or &#8220;prohibited&#8221; tier. As a trade lawyer summarized to <a href="https://companies.caixin.com/2026-10-16/102494131.html">Caixin</a>:</strong></p><blockquote><p>&#8220;Where a technology falls into the restricted category, the technology export operator must still complete declaration formalities, and may then carry out open-sourcing in accordance with laws and regulations after obtaining a licence; where it touches the red line of the prohibited category, outward export in the form of open-sourcing is strictly prohibited.&#8221;</p></blockquote><p>The promise of &#8220;automatic licenses&#8221; only makes sense as long as frontier open models fall in the &#8220;restricted&#8221; tier. As another lawyer told <a href="https://companies.caixin.com/2026-10-16/102494131.html">Caixin</a>:</p><blockquote><p>&#8220;If frontier open models were handled as prohibited for export, then the automatic-licensing path that encourages open-source cooperation would hit a dead end.&#8221;</p></blockquote><h4><strong>Which raises the question: would frontier models fall in the &#8220;restricted&#8221; or &#8220;prohibited&#8221; tier?</strong></h4><p><strong>This is where things get complicated, and ultimately we will have to wait until the rules are in effect to get a clearer picture. Our initial assessment is that some Chinese frontier models and agents would almost certainly fall in the &#8220;restricted&#8221; category, but it is much less clear whether they could be considered &#8220;prohibited&#8221;.</strong><a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-4" id="footnote-anchor-4" target="_self">4</a></p><p>There are four different paths by which frontier models and agents could qualify for the different tiers:</p><p><strong>1) Several Chinese frontier models will almost certainly be considered &#8220;restricted&#8221; based on simple quantitative indicators. </strong>But there are no analogous, higher-value thresholds within the &#8220;prohibited&#8221; tier, meaning models cannot be considered &#8220;prohibited&#8221; based on size or general capability But since general-purpose models are trained mostly on public internet data, they are rather unlikely to include &#8220;core data,&#8221; though this cannot be fully ruled out.</p><p>From an AI safety perspective, an interesting case is that of domain-specific models such as biological foundation models or biological design tools.
