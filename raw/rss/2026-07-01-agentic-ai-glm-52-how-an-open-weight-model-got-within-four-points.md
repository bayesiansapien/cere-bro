# GLM-5.2: How an Open-Weight Model Got Within Four Points of Claude on Agentic Coding

<p>I cloned <a href="https://github.com/zai-org/GLM-5">zai-org/GLM-5</a> expecting a model card. What I found is closer to a thesis statement. The repo is small on purpose: a README, an Apache-2.0 license, one deployment note for Huawei's Ascend chips, and a "skills" folder that is pure documentation. The actual product ships somewhere else, on Hugging Face and behind the z.ai API. But the README tells you exactly what Z.ai (the team formerly branded Zhipu AI, out of Tsinghua) wants you to take away, and the title of their technical report says it plainly: "from Vibe Coding to Agentic Engineering."</p><p>That phrase is the whole pitch. Vibe coding is asking a model for a snippet and pasting it. Agentic engineering is handing a model a repository and a terminal and walking away for an hour. GLM-5 is built for the second job, and the numbers say it is the strongest open-weight model in the world at it right now. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, the GLM-5.2 release scores 81.0 against Claude Opus 4.8's 85.0. For a model you can download and run on your own hardware, four points behind the closed frontier is a different category of result than we had a year ago.</p><p>This post is in two parts. The free half is the analysis: what Z.ai actually built, the three architecture ideas that matter (DSA, IndexShare, and the slime RL stack), and how to read the benchmark numbers without getting sold. The paid half is the part you can act on: a deployment and cost runbook with the real per-token math, when GLM-5.2 beats paying for a frontier API and when it quietly loses, and the <code>reasoning_effort</code> settings that actually move the needle.</p><h2>One repo, three models, one base</h2><p>The first thing to get straight is that "GLM-5" is a family, not a model. The README documents three releases that all share the same 744-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts base with 40 billion parameters active per token.</p><div Total parameters went from 355B to 744B, active parameters from 32B to 40B, and pre-training data from 23 trillion tokens to 28.5 trillion. That is the brute-force half of the story, and it is the half everyone expects: make it bigger, feed it more. The interesting part is that GLM-5.1 and GLM-5.2 ship on the same base. No new pre-training run. The gains there come entirely from post-training and one architecture trick, which tells you where Z.ai thinks the cheap intelligence now lives. It is not in another trillion tokens. It is in how you train the model to use its head over a long task, and how cheaply you can serve it when the context gets huge.</p><p>Forty billion active parameters is the number to hold onto. A dense 744B model would be ruinous to serve. Because GLM-5 is MoE, each token only lights up 40B of weights, so inference cost tracks the active count, not the total. That is why a model this large can be priced the way it is, which is the thread the whole back half of this post pulls on.</p><h2>DSA: making long context affordable</h2><p>Standard attention compares every token to every other token. Cost grows with the square of the sequence length. At a few thousand tokens nobody cares. At a million tokens, which is where GLM-5.2 lives, that quadratic term is the entire bill.</p><p>GLM-5 inherits DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) to break that curve. The idea behind sparse attention is that a token does not actually need to look at every prior token, only at the ones that matter. DSA adds a lightweight "indexer" that scores prior tokens and keeps the top-k most relevant for each query, so attention runs over a short list instead of the full history. You trade a small amount of recall for a large amount of compute, and on long-context work the trade is heavily in your favor. The README is blunt about why they did it: DSA "largely reduces deployment cost while preserving long-context capacity." Cost, not capability, is the headline. This is a model designed to be cheap to run at length, and that design choice is load-bearing for everything downstream.</p><h2>IndexShare: the GLM-5.2 trick worth understanding</h2><p>Here is where GLM-5.2 earns its decimal. DSA still has a cost the README is honest about: that indexer is not free. Running the full top-k selection in every single layer, at a million tokens, adds up fast. So GLM-5.2 introduces <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12201">IndexShare</a>, and the idea is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a reuse the same selected indices. One indexer per four sparse-attention layers. The bet is that the set of "relevant tokens" does not change much from one layer to the next, so recomputing it four times is mostly wasted work. Skip three of the four computations and you cut the per-token FLOPs of the sparse-attention path by 2.9 times at a 1M context length. The model still adapts which tokens it looks at as the sequence evolves, it just stops paying to re-decide that on every layer.</p><p>This is the kind of optimization I respect, because it is not a new architecture, it is noticing that an existing one repeats work. Sebastian Raschka called it "a cross-layer reuse trick for DSA," which is exactly right and not a knock. The cheapest performance win is the one where you delete computation that was never buying you anything. IndexShare is why Z.ai can claim a "solid 1M-token context" rather than a 1M context that technically loads but costs too much to use. The word "solid" is doing real work in that sentence.</p><p>There is a second, quieter improvement riding along: GLM-5.2 reworked its Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) layer, the component that powers speculative decoding, and raised the acceptance length by up to 20%. Speculative decoding lets the model draft several tokens ahead and verify them in one pass; a longer acceptance length means more of those drafts survive, which means faster generation. None of this changes what the model knows. All of it changes what it costs you per hour. That is the GLM-5.2 release in one sentence: same brain, cheaper to think with.</p><h2>slime: the part that is actually hard to copy</h2><p>If IndexShare is the clever trick, the reinforcement-learning stack is the moat. The README points at <a href="https://github.com/THUDM/slime">slime</a>, an asynchronous RL infrastructure the team built specifically because, in their words, "deploying it at scale for LLMs is a In a naive RL loop, you generate a batch of rollouts, wait for all of them to finish, then run a training step, then generate again. For long-horizon agentic tasks, those rollouts have wildly different lengths. One trajectory wraps in twenty tool calls, another grinds through two hundred. The synchronous loop moves at the speed of the slowest rollout in the batch, and your expensive training hardware sits idle waiting for it. slime decouples the two. Generation actors stream finished trajectories into an async buffer, the trainer pulls batches and updates the policy whenever it has enough, and fresh weights stream back to the actors without anyone blocking. Nobody waits.</p><p>Why this matters more than the architecture: the technical report's real claim is not that GLM-5.2 is smart, it is that GLM-5.1 and beyond stay productive over hundreds of rounds and thousands of tool calls, where earlier models "exhaust their repertoire early." A model that plateaus after the first few quick wins is useless for agentic engineering, where the back half of a task is the hard half. Training that behavior in requires running enormous numbers of long, messy, multi-step rollouts and learning from them. You cannot do that economically on a synchronous loop. slime is the infrastructure that makes the long-horizon training affordable, which is why it is open-sourced as a separate project and why it is the hardest piece for a competitor to clone. Anyone can read the IndexShare paper in an afternoon. Rebuilding an async RL stack that keeps a GPU fleet busy is a year of someone's life.</p><h2>The behavior they trained for</h2><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link The loop above is the one GLM-5.1 was tuned to sustain. Break the problem down, run an experiment or a tool call, read the result, find the blocker, revise the approach, go again. The README's claim is that GLM-5.1 "sustains optimization over hundreds of rounds and thousands of tool calls" and that "the longer it runs, the better the result." That last clause is the opposite of how most models behave. Most degrade as the session drags on and the context fills with their own dead ends.</p><p>Z.ai's evidence for this is a year-long vending-machine simulation. On <a href="https://andonlabs.com/evals/vending-bench-2">Vending Bench 2</a>, where the model runs a simulated business across a one-year horizon, GLM-5 finishes with a $4,432 balance, first among open-weight models and within range of Claude Opus 4.5. A vending machine sounds like a toy until you notice what it tests: planning, inventory, pricing, and not panicking over hundreds of sequential decisions where a small early mistake compounds. It is a horizon test wearing a business-sim costume, and it is a smart one.</p><h2>Reading the benchmarks without getting sold</h2><p>Now the numbers, and the caveat that has to come with them. For a team deciding whether to build on this family, that trajectory matters more than any single score.</p><h2>What this means for open weights</h2><p>Step back and the GLM-5 release is a marker for where the open-weight frontier sits in mid-2026. A downloadable, Apache-licensed model is now within four points of Claude Opus on a serious agentic-coding benchmark, ships a usable million-token context, and is engineered from the silicon up to be cheap to serve. Two years ago "open model" meant "good enough for a demo, not for production." GLM-5 is aimed straight at production agentic work, and the architecture choices, every one of them about cost rather than raw capability, tell you Z.ai knows that is the fight that matters. The model that wins the open-weight race will not be the one with the highest benchmark. It will be the one teams can actually afford to run flat-out for hours a day.</p><p>Which raises the only question that pays the bills: should you run this instead of paying a frontier API, and if so, how?
