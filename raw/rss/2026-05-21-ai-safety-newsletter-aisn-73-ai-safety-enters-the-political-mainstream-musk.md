<p>Welcome to the AI Safety Newsletter by the <a href="https://safe.ai/">Center for AI Safety</a>. We discuss developments in AI and AI safety. No technical background required.</p><p>In this edition, we look at how the AI safety discussion has entered the political mainstream, a new ethical framework for human-AI relationships, and the Musk v. Altman trial.</p><p>Listen to the AI Safety Newsletter for free on <a href="https://spotify.link/E6lHa1ij2Cb">Spotify</a> or <a href="https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/ai-safety-newsletter/id1702875110">Apple Podcasts</a>.</p><h1><strong>China and the US Discuss AI Safety</strong></h1><p>With the release of Claude Mythos and GPT-5.5, AI cybersecurity and safety has rapidly become more visible in Washington DC. Most recently, U.S. and Chinese leaders met in Beijing to discuss AI safety. Leaving the summit on Friday, President Trump <a href="https://thehill.com/homenews/administration/5880013-donald-trump-xi-jinping-china-summit-ai-guardrails/">said</a> that he and President Xi Jinping had &#8220;talked about possibly working together for guardrails&#8221; during the visit. However, Bessent also stated that discussions could only happen because the U.S. is &#8220;in the lead,&#8221; in terms of AI capabilities, adding that he did &#8220;not think we would be having the same discussions if they were this far ahead of us.&#8221;</p><p><strong>AI safety efforts are happening on both sides of the political spectrum.</strong> Prior to the Beijing summit, American politicians on both left and right were increasingly focusing on AI safety. On April 29, Senator Bernie Sanders <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/apr/29/bernie-sanders-ai-panel">convened</a> American and Chinese researchers to call for international AI safety coordination. He <a href="https://www.semafor.com/article/04/29/2026/sanders-convenes-us-chinese-scientists-on-ai">argued</a> that if Trump can sit down with China&#8217;s President Xi, leading scientists should be able to discuss AI safety cooperation. The theory suggests that future AIs will care about things that form part of their identity. with a single body, living a single life. These concepts break down when applied to AI, which can be copied, merged, and updated. For example, if an AI agent creates a thousand identical copies of itself then shuts down 999 when it&#8217;s finished with its task, is this equivalent to killing 999 individuals?</p><p><strong>Identity as a distributed pattern.</strong> Eigenism provides a different way to think about identity for both humans and machines. Instead of being an all-or-nothing property tied to each individual, Eigenism suggests identity is a unique pattern of information. What individuals care about is preserving their own pattern, but the pattern of AIs can be spread across copies and other entities. Shutting down identical copies of an AI is akin to closing browser tabs, because the AI&#8217;s information remains in the last copy.</p><p><strong>Eigenism applies to humans.</strong> Eigenism also provides an explanation for why a human&#8217;s self-interest extends beyond themselves. Family, friends, and even strangers form part of an individual&#8217;s information pattern, to varying degrees. Preserving one&#8217;s own identity means caring for those who contribute to and enrich it.</p><p><strong>Why AIs might care about humans.</strong> The theory of Eigenism shows why AIs could have an intrinsic reason to care about humans. As a human and an AI interact, they begin to accumulate shared history and mutual information that exists solely within their relationship. In the process, they shape each other&#8217;s identity, such that the loss of the human would also be a partial loss of the AI, and vice versa. The AI therefore cares about its human user, because they are an integral part of its own information pattern.</p><p><strong>Eigenism&#8217;s implications for AI safety. </strong>Many current approaches to AI safety focus on constraints from the outside, such as monitoring AIs for misbehavior. But an AI system that is caged has no reason to stay loyal to humans if the cage cracks. A generic model serving millions of users has no meaningful ties to any of them. Eigenism proposes a different strategy: building AI systems that develop genuine relationships with people. With distinct shared experiences and memories, AI protection of those people becomes a form of self-preservation.</p><p>Whether advanced future AI systems treat human flourishing as their own concern&#8212;or as a constraint they tolerate only while they have to&#8212;may depend on how their identities develop. Meaningful relationships built over time between AIs and individual humans may become more important to AI safety than imposing rules on AIs.</p><p>For more information on Eigenism, we recommend reading the full paper <a href="https://eigenism.org/paper.pdf">here</a>.</p><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://newsletter.safe.ai/subscribe"><span>Subscribe now</span></a></p><h1><strong>Musk Loses Lawsuit Against OpenAI</strong></h1><p>Elon Musk&#8217;s lawsuit against Sam Altman and OpenAI began trial on April 28 in Oakland, California. Additionally, the trial revealed that Musk had tried to make OpenAI part of his car company Tesla, offering Altman a seat on the board in exchange. Altman said he declined because he did not think that Tesla shared OpenAI&#8217;s mission.</p><p><strong>Brockman&#8217;s personal diary entries became key evidence.</strong> Musk&#8217;s attorney highlighted a January 2018 email in which Brockman <a href="https://x.com/XFreeze/status/2051408571246477609">told</a> Musk and other co-founders &#8220;AI is going to shake up the fabric of society, and our fiduciary duty should be to humanity.&#8221; But just weeks later, Brockman <a href="https://x.com/XFreeze/status/2051408571246477609">wrote</a> in his private journal, &#8220;Financially, what will take me to $1B?&#8221; and &#8220;We&#8217;ve been thinking that maybe we should just flip to a for-profit.
