# China Shipped a National Standard for Agent Identity. We Had Proposed the Same Thing a Year Earlier.

<p>In May 2026 China's SAC/TC 28 committee published GB/Z 185, a seven-part national guidance standard for "AI Agent Interconnection." It covers agent identity codes, identity management, description, discovery, interaction, and tool invocation. In May 2025, my co-authors and I published two papers on the same problem: the Agent Name Service (ANS, arXiv 2505.10609) and a Zero-Trust Identity Framework for Agentic AI (arXiv 2505.19301).</p><p>I read all seven parts of GB/Z 185 against both papers. The convergence is hard to ignore. PKI for trust, capability-aware discovery, a delegation chain from a human owner to the agent, a full credential lifecycle. We reached for the same primitives a year apart, on different continents, without coordination. The divergence is the more interesting story, and it is not about engineering. It is about who holds the root.</p><p>Here is the split for this post. The free half is the comparison and the argument: what China shipped, where it matches ANS and the zero-trust framework, and where the three answers part ways. The paid half is the actionable payload, a field-by-field conformance crosswalk and the seven concrete changes that make an ANS or DID deployment pass GB/Z 185, with an implementer checklist.</p><h2>What China actually shipped</h2><p>GB/Z 185 is a "GB/Z," a guidance technical document, not a mandatory "GB" standard. That word choice matters and I will come back to it. The agent domain holds the agent's own identity, description, authentication, interaction, and tool access. Three service domains sit underneath: a management service domain for identity and credentials, an interconnection service domain for description, discovery, and message dispatch, and a resource access domain for tools. The introduction to every part names MCP, A2A, and ANP by name, then says none of them has industry consensus, so China will write its own unified framework. That is the thesis of the whole series, stated in the first paragraph.</p><p>A second standard came in the same batch, GB/Z 177, "Intelligence grading of AI terminals." It is a different topic. It grades consumer devices on a capability ladder from L1 to L4 (response, tool, assistant, collaboration) across perception, cognition, execution, memory, and learning. GB/Z 177 measures how smart a phone or a car cockpit is. GB/Z 185 governs how agents find and trust each other. The pair tells you China is standardizing both the device layer and the interconnection layer at once. For the rest of this post I focus on GB/Z 185, because that is where my two papers live.</p><h2>Naming: numeric sovereignty, DNS readability, or no root at all</h2><p>The first real fork is how you name an agent. Look at the structure.</p><pre><code>1.2.156.3088.1.1.34C2.478BDF.3GF546
1.2.156   ISO / country member = China
3088      national agent root node
1         identity-code version
1         registrar (registration service provider)
34C2      requester (the org behind the agent)
478BDF    ontology serial (the agent program)
3GF546    instance serial (a running copy)</code></pre><p>This is a numeric code with a fixed national root at <code>1.2.156.3088</code>. A central authority hands out the registrar and requester arcs. The last two arcs encode a distinction I did not have in ANS: the agent ontology, the program, versus the agent instance, a running copy of it. One program spawns many instances, and each gets its own serial under the parent. The code is unique, traceable for its whole life, and the root is sovereign. Appendix B of 185.2 even spells out how a foreign agent gets a code: register under the 3088 node, or stand up your own system and sign a mutual-recognition treaty with it.</p><p>Our ANS paper went the other way. The ANSName is a DNS-style string, readable by a human, with the capability baked into the name.</p><pre><code>ANSName = Protocol "://" AgentID "." Capability "." Provider ".v" Version "." Extension
a2a://translator.DocTranslation.Acme.v2.1.hipaa</code></pre><p>You can read <code>a2a://translator.DocTranslation.Acme.v2.1.hipaa</code> and know the protocol, the capability, the vendor, the semantic version, and a deployment tag, all before you resolve it. I proposed governance "similar to ICANN for DNS," a global registry, not a national one. The zero-trust paper went further still and used Decentralized Identifiers, where trust comes from a verifiable credential rather than from whoever assigned the name. There is no root to capture, and no root to lean on either.</p><p>So the same need produced three philosophies. China optimized for accountability and jurisdiction. ANS optimized for human legibility and capability-first lookup. DIDs optimized for removing the central point of control. None is wrong. They encode different politics.</p><h2>Trust: everyone reached for PKI</h2><p>Here is where I sat up. y2="14"></line></svg></button></div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>GB/Z 185.3 builds identity on public-key certificates under GB/T 20518, China's certificate format standard, with root certificates from nationally-recognized certification authorities. Private keys live in an HSM or a TEE. Authentication is challenge-response: the relying party issues a nonce, the agent signs it with its private key into a "process credential package," and a verifier checks the signature, walks the certificate chain to a trusted root, and validates the delegation chain from the human delegator down to the agent. The verifier then issues a signed assertion, and the relying party decides.</p><p>Swap "GB/T 20518" for "X.509" and that is the ANS trust model almost line for line. ANS issues each agent an X.509 certificate through a Registration Authority and a Certificate Authority, verifies signatures on resolution, walks the chain to a trusted CA, and revokes through CRL or OCSP. Both designs put a human or an organization at the top of the chain and make the agent prove it acts on that party's authority. The zero-trust paper frames the same delegation idea with verifiable credentials instead of X.509, but the shape is identical: no agent is trusted on its own say-so, and every action traces back to an accountable owner.</p><p>The convergence is not a coincidence. When you need machine identity at scale with revocation and accountability, PKI is the tool that already exists and already works. We did not need a new cryptographic idea. We needed to point an old one at a new kind of principal.</p><h2>Discovery: capability-aware on both sides, different keys</h2><p>Both frameworks treat discovery as a first-class problem, and both make it capability-aware. One is a discovery service that matches published agents and stays in sync with a description manager. The other is preset discovery from local cache or a <code>.well-known</code> address. The required search key is a natural-language description of what you need. You ask in plain language, optionally narrowed by name or identity code, and the service returns zero or more matches. The agent description in 185.4 reads like an A2A Agent Card: name, provider, access address, access method, serving area, authentication, input and output types, and a list of skills with tags and examples.</p><p>ANS resolution is more like DNS with a type system. You pass an ANSName plus a version range. The resolver parses it, queries the registry by capability, runs semantic-version negotiation when several agents match, verifies the certificate chain, and returns a signed endpoint with a TTL (I set the default at 300 seconds). The capability is in the name, so resolution is structured, not interpretive.</p><p>The contrast is sharp and it reflects the year between the two. China bet on the language model as the matcher: describe the need in words and let the discovery service reason about fit. ANS bet on structured capability strings and version math. China's choice is friendlier to an LLM-driven agent that thinks in prose. Mine is friendlier to a deterministic resolver you can cache and test. The honest answer is you want both, a structured key for the machine path and a natural-language path for the agent that does not know the exact name.</p><h2>The crosswalk, and the real PKI and certificates. Capability-aware discovery. A register, update, revoke lifecycle. A delegation chain to a human owner. We can argue about OID versus DNS-style names, or about whether a TTL belongs in the spec, but those are taste and tuning. An engineer who built one could build the others.</p><p>The bottom three rows are where the daylight is. On privacy, my two papers go hard at privacy-preserving discovery: zero-knowledge proofs for capability attestation, private information retrieval so the registry cannot profile who is asking, differential privacy on query patterns. GB/Z 185 spends its privacy budget on the other end, vetting the agent at registration through risk assessment and evidence collection, with little on hiding the query. On threat modeling, ANS runs a formal MAESTRO pass across seven layers; GB/Z 185 does risk assessment but ships no adversarial model I could find. On governance, the split is total. China's root is national and sovereign, with treaties for foreign agents. ANS is global and ICANN-like. DIDs have no root to govern at all.</p><p>That governance row is the whole point. The hard problem in agent identity was never the cryptography. We all solved that the same way. The hard problem is who runs the registry, who can revoke your agent, and whose courts decide a dispute. China answered "the state, by default." I answered "a neutral global body." The DID camp answered "no one." Three teams, one threat model, three constitutions.</p><h2>What surprised me, and what I would change</h2><p>Two things stood out. First, the ontology-versus-instance split in 185.2 is sharper than what I shipped. ANS gives an agent one identity. GB/Z 185 gives the program one identity and each running copy another, nested underneath. For a world of ephemeral, autoscaled agents, that is the right call, and I would fold it into ANS: an ontology ANSName with per-instance serials. Second, China made <code>.well-known</code> a first-class discovery path. I treated the central resolver as the main road. A <code>.well-known</code> descriptor costs almost nothing and gives you discovery with no registry online. I would add it.</p><p>What I would not change is the governance stance. A single national root is efficient and accountable inside one jurisdiction, and it is a choke point across jurisdictions. The day two agents under two different sovereign roots need to transact, you are back to treaties and mutual recognition, which is exactly where 185.2 Appendix B already points. That friction is the price of sovereignty, and it is the reason a neutral global directory still has a job. The standard that wins will be the one that resolves a name across roots without asking permission, and none of the three nails that yet.</p><p>If you are building agent infrastructure that has to work in China and outside it, you do not get to pick one. You will run an ANS-style or DID-style identity for the open world and present a GB/Z 185 identity at the border. The rest of this post is how to make one system do both without a fork.</p><p><strong>For paid subscribers &#8212; a hands-on deep dive continues below.</strong></p><p>Everything up to this point is free.
