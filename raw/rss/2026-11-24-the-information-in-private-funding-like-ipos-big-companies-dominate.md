# In Private Funding, Like IPOs, Big Companies Dominate

<p>Shares of Anthropic fell below its $83 IPO price on Tuesday morning, summing up what has been a tough year for IPOs. While a couple of monster IPOs got done—Anthropic and SpaceX—only a handful of others made it through including offerings from SK Hynix, Cerebras and Lime. And as of Tuesday, all of those were trading below their IPO price.&nbsp;</p><p>That makes it the fifth year in a row when the IPO market has been at least <i>a bit </i>disappointing. A bunch of companies are lined up for next year—including OpenAI—but slumping tech valuations fueled by skepticism about the AI boom clouds prospects for 2027.&nbsp;</p><p>In that context, it’s a decent time to take the pulse of startup dealmaking. One thing is clear: as in the IPO market this year, in the private fundraising market, the biggest companies are dominating.</p><p>The top 10 private companies have more than doubled their combined valuation from $1.1 trillion to $2.6 trillion in just <i>one</i> year, Goldman’s top tech banker Jane Dunlevie said on stage last week at Goldman Sachs’ annual private tech conference in Las Vegas.&nbsp;</p><p>She was talking about OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, Stripe, Waymo, Revolut, Anduril, Ramp, Cognition and Jeff Bezos's Prometheus. And more than half of those—OpenAI, Databricks, Anduril, Stripe, Ramp and Cognition—are now trying to raise billions more.</p><p>There’s still plenty of smaller startups emerging. Goldman managed to play matchmaker for more than 400 investors and 400-plus startups in the gilded corridors at the Encore Wynn Hotel. The mood was still electric, though investors are starting to think: as public market valuations fall, how should we value these private companies?&nbsp;</p><p>One reason why private valuations can stay elevated even as public stocks drop is that venture investors tend to zoom in on growth. Many of these AI businesses’ revenues have taken off, even as they bleed money thanks to expensive AI models they run and the chips they need to buy or rent.&nbsp;</p><p>At a fireside chat at last week’s confab, Winston Weinberg, CEO of legal AI startup Harvey, acknowledged that even with fast revenue growth, its gross margins fell to about negative 55% around June. They improved the figure by about 95% in roughly three months. Weinberg said annualized revenue grew from $190 million at the end of last year and expected to end this year with at least $675 million.</p><p>“We’re still early in the build out of AI, which means many of the defining companies of this era are not yet public and many investors are just getting to see the potential of those companies, both on growth and margin.” Goldman’s Jane Dunlevie said in an interview on the sidelines.&nbsp;</p><p>She said each tech era has its own tests with investors: for internet companies, it was a path to profit; for subscription software firms, it was predictable revenue and net revenue retention.&nbsp;</p><p>Meanwhile, attendees at the conference were staying hopeful about next year’s IPO market. Some predicted that older venture-backed startups, such as Vercel, Ramp and Plaid, and even the older vertical software companies with some AI makeover, such as <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/articles/alphasense-tops-700-million-annual-recurring-revenue-takes-ipo-steps">AlphaSense</a> and Clio, will finally go public.</p><p>In the two days of investors’ schmoozing over fancy desserts and cocktails, a lineup of popular AI, chip and cybersecurity startups, like Legora, Unconventional AI and Abnormal AI fielded dozens of investors’ meeting requests. Many older software startups were trying to remind investors they still exist. They are not getting too much love, say bankers and investors.&nbsp;</p><p>One barometer of who’s important in Las Vegas this year was whom Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon invited to his private dinner at the fancy restaurant Delilah.
