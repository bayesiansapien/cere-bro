# DSpark: How DeepSeek Made Its Own Serving Stack 60-85% Faster Without Touching Model Quality

<p>A large language model writes one token at a time. To produce token number 500, it runs a full forward pass over all 499 tokens before it, samples one more, and does it again. The math is brutal: your wait time scales with the length of the answer, and a GPU that could multiply enormous matrices sits mostly idle, shuffling one skinny vector through at a time. For a chatbot that streams a paragraph, you feel this as the pause before the words appear. For an agent that fires off twenty tool calls in a loop, you pay it twenty times. </p><p>Speculative decoding is the standard escape hatch, and it is genuinely clever. But the moment you try to push it hard in a real serving system with hundreds of concurrent users, it turns on you. The technique that speeds up one request slows down the fleet. DeepSeek hit exactly this wall shipping DeepSeek-V4, and their answer is a system called DSpark. It is now the production drafter for V4-Flash and V4-Pro, and it beat the previous production setup by 60 to 85 percent per user, at the same total throughput, with zero change to what the model actually says. </p><p>This post is a walk through the DSpark paper (a joint DeepSeek-AI and Peking University effort). The free half is the part everyone should understand: what speculative decoding is, the two specific ways it breaks at scale, and the two ideas DSpark uses to fix them. The paid half is for people who actually run this stuff: the exact math of the sequential head and the scheduler, the calibration trick that makes it safe, the production hacks that let it survive CUDA graphs and asynchronous scheduling, and a concrete deployment checklist I would hand a team standing up speculative decoding today. </p><h2>The trick: guess a lot, check in parallel</h2><p>Here is the whole idea in one breath. Keep a small, fast <strong>draft model</strong> next to your big, slow <strong>target model</strong>. Let the draft model spit out a block of, say, seven guessed tokens. Then hand all seven to the target model at once and let it verify them in a single forward pass. </p><p>That single pass is the magic. Verifying seven tokens costs the target model almost the same as generating one, because the expensive part of a forward pass is loading the weights, not the arithmetic on a few extra positions. So you check seven guesses for the price of one. The target model walks the block left to right, accepts every token that matches what it would have produced, and stops at the first mismatch. Whatever prefix survived, you keep, plus one free "bonus" token that the target model generates for free at the rejection point. </p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link y2="14"></line></svg></button></div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>The loop above is the beating heart of every speculative decoding system. The draft model proposes, the target verifies in one pass, you accept the good prefix plus a bonus token, and that bonus token becomes the anchor that seeds the next round. The subtle, beautiful part is the acceptance rule. It uses rejection sampling, and it is provably distribution-preserving: the tokens that come out the far end are drawn from exactly the same distribution the target model would have produced on its own. You are not approximating the big model with the small one. You are using the small one to guess and the big one to audit, and the audit is exact. No quality loss, ever. That property is why anyone trusts this in production at all. </p><p>So where does the speed come from? The paper frames it as one clean equation. The average latency per generated token is the total time of a round divided by the number of tokens that round accepted: </p><p><code>L = (T_draft + T_verify) / tau</code> </p><p><code>T_draft</code> is how long the draft model took, <code>T_verify</code> is the target's verification pass, and <code>tau</code> is how many tokens you got to keep. Three levers, and only three: draft faster, draft better so <code>tau</code> goes up, or verify smarter so <code>T_verify</code> stops being wasted. Every speculative decoding paper ever written is pulling on one of these three levers. DSpark's whole contribution is that it pulls on the second and third at the same time, and the two pulls reinforce each other. </p><h2>The drafter dilemma: two ways to lose</h2><p>The draft model is where the design tension lives, and for years you got to pick your poison. </p><p>The first family is <strong>autoregressive drafters</strong>. The draft model is itself a tiny language model that generates its guesses one at a time, each token conditioned on the ones before it. This gives it real modeling power: because token three gets to see tokens one and two, it makes coherent guesses. The problem is right there in the name. To draft seven tokens it runs seven sequential little passes, so <code>T_draft</code> grows with the block size. To keep drafting cheap, you are forced to use tiny blocks and shallow networks. You draft well, but slowly, and only a little at a time. </p><p>The second family is <strong>parallel drafters</strong>. These produce the entire block of seven tokens in a single forward pass. Draft cost becomes nearly independent of block size, so you can afford deep networks and long blocks. DFlash, the state-of-the-art parallel drafter DSpark builds on, works this way: it feeds an anchor token plus a row of "mask" tokens through the draft network and reads out logits for every masked position at once. Fast, cheap, long blocks. But there is a catch, and it is a deep one. </p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img" Autoregressive drafters pay for their coherence with latency that scales with the block. Parallel drafters get the block for free but lose the coherence, and the reason they lose it is worth sitting with, because it is the whole motivation for DSpark's first idea. </p><h2>Why parallel drafts fall apart at the tail</h2><p>When a parallel drafter predicts all seven positions in one shot, position four has no idea what position three actually chose. Each position independently picks the most likely token given only the original context, marginalizing over every possible predecessor instead of the one that got sampled. </p><p>The paper's example is perfect. Say the context could plausibly continue as "of course" or as "no problem." A parallel drafter looks at position one and sees "of" and "no" are both good. It looks at position two and sees "course" and "problem" are both good. So it might confidently emit "of problem" or "no course", a fluent-looking phrase that is complete nonsense, stitched from two different valid continuations. This is called multi-modal collision, and it gets worse the deeper into the block you go, because more and more of the sequence has to stay mutually consistent by luck alone. </p><p>The consequence is <strong>suffix decay</strong>. Early tokens in the block get accepted at high rates; late tokens get rejected because they contradict the ones before them. And because the acceptance rule stops at the first rejection, a bad token at position four throws away positions five, six, and seven no matter how good they were. Your long, cheap block collapses into a short accepted prefix. </p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img" Keep the heavy, expensive draft computation fully parallel, that is where the speed lives, and you do not want to give it up. Then bolt on a tiny sequential module whose only job is to inject local dependency: let each position see what the position before it actually sampled, and nudge its logits accordingly. Once position one commits to "of," the sequential head boosts "course" and suppresses "problem" at position two. The collision is gone. You paid for it with a whisper of extra latency, because the sequential part is deliberately microscopic next to the parallel backbone. </p><p>The name earns itself. It is not fully parallel and not fully autoregressive. It is a parallel drafter with just enough sequential memory to stop lying to itself. </p><h2>The counterintuitive result: parallel starts ahead</h2><p>Before the fix, the paper drops a genuinely surprising finding, and it reframes how you should think about drafters. You would assume the autoregressive drafter, with its careful step-by-step reasoning, produces higher-quality drafts than the reckless parallel one. It does not. Across every model and domain they tested, the parallel DFlash accepts <em>longer</em> sequences than the autoregressive Eagle3. The step-by-step model loses to the one-shot model. </p><p>The reason is a leverage argument, and it hinges on position one. </p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link The first token carries the highest leverage of any token in the block, and the deep parallel model wins it decisively. </p><p>Then the curves diverge exactly as you would expect from the collision story. The autoregressive Eagle3 actually <em>climbs</em> deeper into the block, from 0.53 up to 0.74 on chat, because once a semantic path is locked in, the next tokens get more predictable and its sequential conditioning cashes in on that certainty. An anchor token comes in from the last round. The parallel backbone produces base logits for the whole block in one pass. The sequential head refines them into coherent tokens and, crucially, also emits a confidence score for each position. A scheduler reads those scores and decides how much of the block is even worth sending to the target model, dropping the low-confidence tail. The target verifies what survived, corrects the first rejection, and that correction becomes the next anchor. The first idea made the draft good. The second idea, that scheduler, is about not wasting the target model's time, and that is where the real production win hides. </p><h2>The second failure: verifying too much is a tax on everyone else</h2><p>Here is the trap that catches teams the first time they push speculative decoding into a busy serving system. Making the draft block longer feels free, the draft is cheap, so why not propose sixteen tokens and grab as many as you can? </p><p>Because verification is not free when the system is busy. Every token you send the target model to check occupies a slot in its batch. Under light load, with spare capacity everywhere, checking a few extra low-odds tokens costs nothing; if they get rejected, so what. Under heavy load, that batch capacity is the single most contended resource in the building. Every speculative token you verify with a high chance of rejection is a slot you stole from another user's request that would have used it productively. You sped up one user by making a hundred others wait. At high enough concurrency, aggressive speculation makes aggregate throughput <em>worse</em> than not speculating at all. This is precisely why DeepSeek's previous production baseline was MTP-1, a timid single-token drafter: the safe, boring choice, because anything more aggressive fell off a cliff under load. </p><p>The right verification length is not a constant. It moves along two axes. On the data axis, structured text like code sustains high acceptance and deserves long verification, while open-ended chat rejects more and deserves less. On the system axis, the same token is worth verifying under light load and worth dropping under heavy load. A static block length cannot possibly be right for both a quiet Tuesday and a traffic spike, for both a coding request and a casual chat. It is guaranteed to be wrong somewhere. </p><p>It helps to name the two numbers a serving team is actually fighting over, because they pull in opposite directions. One is aggregate throughput: total tokens per second across the whole GPU, which sets how many users you can serve at once and how much the service costs to run. The other is per-user generation speed, tokens per second for a single request, which is what a person feels as responsiveness and what an agent's loop latency multiplies. Classic speculative decoding trades the first for the second: it burns extra system compute on verification to make one user's stream faster. Under the light-to-moderate concurrency DeepSeek actually runs, though, the two objectives stop competing and start correlating, because the batch rarely saturates the GPU. In that regime the smart move is to route the idle compute toward the most promising draft tokens, which is exactly what the scheduler does. The trap is that "idle compute" is a moving target, and a static block length cannot track it. </p><p>DSpark's second idea is to stop guessing the verification length and start computing it, per request, per moment, from two signals: how likely each drafted token is to survive, and how loaded the engine is right now. That is the confidence-scheduled verification, and it is the part I find genuinely novel. It treats the whole thing as a throughput optimization problem rather than a heuristic threshold, and it solves that problem exactly, live, on every step. </p><p>Everything above is the free read: the two failure modes, and the shape of the two fixes.
