instructions each one takes. Anthropic's <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude/fable">Fable page</a> presents Fable 5.2 as the strongest model in its line for technical work, and Google's <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-carbon-now-available/">Carbon post</a> presents Carbon as the strongest model in its line. The independent composite puts one ahead on raw capability and the other ahead on cost per completed task. The prompt guides differ in kind: Anthropic published a <a href="https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-2">dedicated guide</a> for Fable with a measured effect for each rule, while Google published no Carbon-specific guide, and Carbon prompting uses a <a href="https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies">general guide</a> plus a migration checklist.</p><p>The score pattern is a split. Fable takes the agentic coding tests and the science tests on Anthropic's table, and Carbon takes business workflow automation and the long-context test on Google's table. The independent composite places Fable ahead, and the cost per completed task places Carbon ahead. Each company's table favors its own model, and the shared tests give the cleaner read: the same test, run on both models, shows where the lead sits and how wide the lead is.</p><p>The guide asymmetry matters more than the score split for daily work. Anthropic's guide reads as a list of observed failures, and each failure carries a measured number from Anthropic's own tests. The guide names what breaks when an old prompt meets the new model, and it names the fix and the cost of the fix in tokens. Google's material for Carbon reads differently: a general prompt-design guide written for an earlier model, thinking docs that describe the dial, and a migration checklist that changes settings rather than prompt text. One guide teaches prompt text. The other teaches settings.</p><p>The price pattern differs from the score pattern. Carbon's per-token price is lower than Fable's at launch, and the introductory rate ends before the higher rate starts. The per-token price misleads on its own: the cost of a completed task depends on how many tokens the model spends thinking, and the thinking dial determines that spend. Fable at its highest effort spends far more per task than Carbon at its high level on the same composite, and Carbon at its middle level posts the lower cost on the automation test.</p><p>Both models move thinking out of the prompt and into a setting. Fable sets thinking with an effort dial, and Carbon sets it with a thinking level. The dial changes tokens and time far more than it changes reply length, and both companies put the dial in config rather than in prompt text. Both companies also name a cheaper model as the start point for everyday work, and both treat the top model as the escalation for hard, long, verification-heavy jobs.</p><p>Figure 1 is the independent
