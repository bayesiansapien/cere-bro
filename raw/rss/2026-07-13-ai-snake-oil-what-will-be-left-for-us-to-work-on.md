# What will be left for us to work on?

<p><span>I had the honor of giving a </span><a href="https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/invited-talk/67274"><span>keynote</span></a><span> at the International Conference on Machine Learning in Seoul last week titled &#8220;What will be left for us to work on?&#8221; I addressed the widespread anxiety about how we should adapt as AI capabilities increase. I was thrilled by the talk&#8217;s reception, so I have made my slides available </span><strong><a href="https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/talks/icml-2026-annotated-slides/"><span>here</span></a></strong><span>, annotated with a lightly edited transcript. You can also view them below right here on this page, but the </span><strong><a href="https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/talks/icml-2026-annotated-slides/"><span>online version</span></a></strong><span> has animations, clickable links, and a much nicer experience overall.</span></p><p><span>I made three arguments. First, the </span><em><span>AI as Normal Technology</span></em><span> framework is a correct and useful as a way to think about AI&#8217;s impacts, unless and until there is some future discontinuity such as through recursive self-improvement. Second, even though we should take recursive self-improvement seriously, there is no milestone that companies might achieve in the lab that will suddenly put us all out of work. Third and finally, jobs of the future will be radically different, and a lot of adaptation will be needed. I think everyone should choose their own path, but hopefully there are some interesting ideas here for you to consider.</p><p>By floor I mean what AI can do on its own. By ceiling I mean what AI allows us to do by augmenting our capabilities &#8212; the ability to take on new and ambitious projects that were not possible before AI. But the ceiling is not going up automatically. It is only if we work on pushing the ceiling up.</p><p>I find that I&#8217;m spending something like 10 hours per week just learning and experimenting with new workflows, as well as learning new topics. So one way to think about it is that AI enables big productivity improvements, and I&#8217;ve been trying to take that time saved and &#8220;reinvest&#8221; it into long-term growth and picking up new and complementary skills.</p><p>I&#8217;ve learned that if I don&#8217;t feel exhausted at the end of the day, I&#8217;ve done something wrong. I&#8217;ve offloaded too much to AI. I&#8217;ve sacrificed too much of my long-term growth in the pursuit of short-term productivity.</p><p>Growth and productivity are two legs of a three-legged stool that we need to learn how to balance. And the third leg of that stool is staying in control.</p><p>Here are two heuristics that I&#8217;ve tried to use in order to do that. The first is resisting the black-box temptation. Companies want us to use agents as black boxes &#8212; just prompt it and it will go off and do its thing. I think that&#8217;s a <a href="https://substack.com/@aisnakeoil/note/c-259452894">trap</a>. I think that&#8217;s very dangerous, and it will lead us to gradually give up control over time.</p><p>Second, and related, is what I call the <a href="https://substack.com/@aisnakeoil/note/c-274974582">dependence spiral</a>. It&#8217;s very tempting to use AI for tasks that I am myself not yet an expert at, because learning new things is of course very hard.
