What happens when AI writes all the code?</h2><p>At the end of 2025, many of us in tech saw AI&#8217;s coding capability dramatically improve with Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2, and the models suddenly become good enough to reliably write most of the code &#8211; and soon, almost all of it. In January, I asked: <a href="https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/when-ai-writes-almost-all-code-what">when AI writes almost all the code, what happens to software engineering?</a></p><p>All the entries in this category covered the new challenges of AI generating the code, and how practices at many tech companies have adapted. Many writers compare a &#8220;before&#8221; and &#8220;after&#8221; with data on PR rates, revert rates, build times, and more.</p><h3><em>Winner: </em>&#8216;An engineering manager&#8217;s crazy year&#8217;</h3><p><em>Edited excerpt from <a href="https://www.manager.dev/newsletter/an-engineering-manager-s-crazy-year">the essay</a> by </em><span class="mention-wrap"></span> <em>author of <a href="https://manager.dev/">manager.dev</a>, Engineering Manager at HoneyBook:</em></p><p>&#8220;It&#8217;s June 2026; a nice, sunny day outside when I sit at my computer, looking at a 10k-line PR opened by my team&#8217;s great designer, and I want to die inside.</p><p>It&#8217;s not a mock or a prototype; it&#8217;s code he wants to merge into production. Not only is it full of hacky solutions (not even talking about criminal useEffect abuses), it also has dummy data where backend calls were supposed to be. How the hell did I get here? Let&#8217;s back up a bit.</p><p>I joined HoneyBook as an Engineering Manager in October 2025. It&#8217;s a 13-year-old startup (raised ~$500M), a management platform for business owners (think CRM+contracts+admin, etc.). I joined to lead an existing team of seven experienced engineers responsible for the financial products like managing business expenses, loans, etc.</p><p>This past year has been a crazy ride, going in just a few months from classic engineering to pure vibe coding, ending with the launch of a real zero-to-one initiative at an established company.</p><h4>Code review collapse</h4><p>We split our work into three repositories: the shared ai-workflows codebase, our own backend repo, and a thin frontend layer called atlas-vibe. Each had a different code review approach:</p><ul><li><p>In AI-workflows, every PR was thoroughly reviewed by at least two people including the engineer who wrote the code.</p></li><li><p>In atlas-vibe, there were barely any PRs. We vibe code on the frontend, slowly improving the skills in our repo, and reducing the amount of substandard code a bit.</p></li><li><p>In the backend repo, we had optional code reviews. That&#8217;s an interesting one.</p></li></ul><p>Slowly, it became more and more tempting to just not ask for reviews. It&#8217;s addicting to just develop a feature and immediately have it in production. There&#8217;s no need to have an open end in your brain.</p><p>But still, at that stage I didn&#8217;t accept an &#8216;I don&#8217;t know, Claude wrote it&#8217; approach.
