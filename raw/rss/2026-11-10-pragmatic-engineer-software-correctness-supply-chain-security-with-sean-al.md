What did that teach?</strong></p><p><strong>Sean:</strong> &#8220;When I was learning to program, I decided to pick a language and dedicate myself to it for a while. I ended up settling on Ada and C.</p><p>Ada was driven in a lot of ways by what the US defense department wanted: a language which is very deeply specified and will not let you write <em>incorrect</em> programs. Does anybody care about testing?</h2><p><strong>You&#8217;ve <a href="https://www.seantallen.com/posts/how-do-i-know-its-working/">written</a> &#8220;no one cares about testing. I don&#8217;t value tests,&#8221; but it&#8217;s odd to hear this from you as someone who does care about correctness.</strong></p><p><strong>Sean: </strong>&#8220;I wrote that because people think of Antithesis as a testing company, and I don&#8217;t think of it that way. I think of it as a software verification and correctness company &#8211; which is very different.</p><p>Tests are things that you do to verify that things are <em>working</em>, for things you have no better way to verify. The worst type of testing is manual testing. You can automate it, and get more and more sophisticated with automated tests, but you are still doing something at the <em>late</em> stage of development. You should do as much as possible <em>before</em> you get to testing.</p><p>With ML languages, you can usually represent within the type system what the states are, and what the allowable transitions are. Once you have these in place, you can make sure that things can <em>never</em> transition to a state you don&#8217;t expect. Being able to do so is huge!</p><p>In practice, when you&#8217;re trying to work out whether your system works, you&#8217;re not thinking about the entire thing all the way down. You&#8217;re only thinking about the stuff your type system will <em>not</em> allow you to express. This is the idea behind &#8220;make illegal states unrepresentable.&#8221;</p><p><strong>Playing devil&#8217;s advocate, you write the tests first in test-driven development (TDD), so that&#8217;s doing testing upfront, isn&#8217;t it?</strong></p><p><strong>Sean:</strong> &#8220;I love TDD. The person writing the implementation is supposed to do the simplest possible thing.</p><p>When people did these katas with me, they quickly discovered that I took writing the least amount possible to pass very literally. I would hard-code answers into the implementation to make their example-based tests pass. Workloads and correctness</h2><p><strong>You&#8217;ve previously said that many people don&#8217;t have &#8216;workloads&#8217; in their testing strategy. What are workloads?</strong></p><p><strong>Sean:</strong> &#8220;Most people&#8217;s examples of property-based testing are simple:</p><ul><li><p>Reverse a list</p></li><li><p>&#8594; then reverse it again</p></li><li><p>&#8594; confirm you have the same list you started with.</p></li></ul><p>However, if you want to apply property-based testing to a distributed system or database, then the <em>workload</em> is a combination of:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Inputs</strong>: created by you to drive your code</p></li><li><p><strong>Validation</strong>: whatever you use to validate that the thing is working</p></li></ul><p>For example, let&#8217;s take a key-value store with three operations. The workload is the combination of:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Inputs</strong>: the GET, PUT, and DELETE operations</p></li><li><p><strong>Validation</strong>: deciding how many keys there are, what the range of keys is, and how big the key and their values can be</p></li></ul><p>When reading about property-based testing, it&#8217;s easy to think that randomness is going to do the testing for you. For example, say you pick one of GET, PUT, and DELETE operations at random. By doing so, you won&#8217;t trigger many interesting places in your code because after a PUT, you&#8217;ll probably do a DELETE nearby. The odds of doing 10,000 PUTs in a row &#8211; pushing into areas where your store needs to swap to disk, garbage collect, or reshard &#8211; are tiny. Leaving all of this to chance is like walking when drunk: people don&#8217;t get very far from where they start.&#8221;</p><p><strong>What&#8217;s a specific workload that can help with testing?</strong></p><p><strong>Sean:</strong> &#8220;There&#8217;s one workload commonly used for testing isolation guarantees in ACID databases, and it&#8217;s the single most important thing you want to guarantee in a database. It&#8217;s called the &#8216;Bank Test&#8217;:</p><p>Let&#8217;s take this setup:</p><ul><li><p>There&#8217;s $10,000 in the system</p></li><li><p>You split this $10,000 between 10 accounts. Each account has $1,000.</p></li><li><p>The workload transfers money from one account to another inside a transaction</p></li></ul><p>While running the workload which does transfers between accounts, you have another component constantly querying the total amount of money in the system. If the isolation guarantees hold, the only value it should ever see is 10,000.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img"
