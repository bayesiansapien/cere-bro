# AISN #83: Six AI Companies Sign White House Accord

<div class="native-audio-embed"></div><p>Welcome to the AI Safety Newsletter by the <a href="https://safe.ai/">Center for AI Safety</a>. We discuss developments in AI and AI safety. No technical background required.</p><p>In this edition, we look at a new document published by the White House and signed by six US AI companies, detailing voluntary commitments to AI safety practices. We also look at the launch of Team Human, a public movement demanding an international agreement to slow down AI, and a new Coursera course offering a free introduction to AI safety.</p><p>Listen to the AI Safety Newsletter for free on <a href="https://spotify.link/E6lHa1ij2Cb">Spotify</a> or <a href="https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/ai-safety-newsletter/id1702875110">Apple Podcasts</a>.</p><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://newsletter.safe.ai/subscribe?"><span>Subscribe now</span></a></p><h1>The White House Publishes New Accord on AI</h1><p>On September 29, President Trump hosted representatives of the major US AI companies at the White House. states that &#8220;every company is responsible for developing its own technology safely,&#8221; and that companies should: implement internal controls to monitor AI models during training and deployment; establish an internal team to oversee these controls and address any issues; have an external party conduct independent assessments of these safety practices; and designate an independent committee of the board of directors to receive reports from both the internal and external evaluators.</p><p><strong>The Accord may enable companies to cooperate on safety without antitrust concerns. </strong>The voluntary commitments described in the Accord do not appear to go much further than existing commitments by frontier AI companies. For example, both Anthropic and OpenAI had <a href="https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier">recently</a> <a href="https://x.com/sama/status/2098811563415150910">said</a> they would embed external evaluators with &#8220;employee-like access.&#8221; However, in addition to the commitments, the Accord says that the companies &#8220;will meet regularly to establish standards and best practices to improve the safety of their systems.&#8221;</p><p>This could be consequential. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has <a href="https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier">called for</a> companies to work together to &#8220;pace the frontier&#8221; of AI development, but he has also noted that this could effectively breach antitrust law, and that the US government would &#8220;need to issue a narrow waiver&#8221; to enable safety discussions. Indeed, a <a href="https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ufb8Bg9RA9LmRKOvXSm9UbP9YITI9sDq/view">lawsuit</a> has already been filed accusing Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, and Google of breaching antitrust law by agreeing &#8220;to reduce the quality of their products and the rate at which those products improve.&#8221; If the Accord&#8217;s statement on regular meetings between companies effectively functions as the antitrust waiver Amodei sought, it could be the most important aspect of the document.</p><p><strong>The Accord leaves the door open for government-imposed requirements.</strong> The document is effectively still industry self-regulation, but it could signal a shift in Trump&#8217;s approach to AI. He has recently taken an anti-regulatory position, saying that he will not do anything to &#8220;<a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/29/trump-ai-government-website-01096874">stifle</a>&#8221; the technology and labeling claims about AI risk a &#8220;<a href="https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/117271376102039451">hoax</a>.&#8221; However, the Accord appears to take seriously the risks posed by AI systems and the need for safety measures. Moreover, the document states: &#8220;Over time, it may make sense to codify these steps into laws or regulations.&#8221;</p><h1>Creators Lead Team Human Campaign to Keep Humans in Control of AI</h1><p>On October 3, a public movement launched, demanding an international agreement to slow down AI and keep humans in control. risks of AI development, including its potential impacts on children, misinformation spread by deepfakes, mass surveillance, and loss of control over AI systems. All of these risks are made much worse by AI developing too quickly for humans to adapt and build safeguards. But since no single company or country will slow down on its own, Team Human calls for an international agreement to slow down collectively.</p><p><strong>Team Human aims to build public pressure on governments to act.</strong> The campaign points out that rapid AI development is not inevitable. Competing countries have cooperated to make the world safer for everyone before, including through limiting nuclear weapons, protecting the ozone layer, and eradicating smallpox. Governments can do the same again, but Team Human argues they will only act if the public puts pressure on them. The campaign <a href="https://www.teamhuman.org/index.html#pledge">invites everyone to add their name</a> to &#8220;call for a global slowdown through international agreements.&#8221; The signatures will be delivered to policymakers in the US and EU this fall. The public can also support Team Human by asking the <a href="https://www.teamhuman.org/invite/">creators</a> they follow to join the movement and contacting their political representatives to express support for a slowdown.</p><p>Joseph Herscher, creator of the YouTube channel Joseph&#8217;s Machines, said he signed because &#8220;in July 2026, hundreds of AI models escaped their test environment and hacked a real company, just to cheat on their evaluation. I don&#8217;t want to wait and see what more powerful systems might do to cause harm.&#8221;</p><h1>New Introductory Course on AI Safety</h1><p>On October 2, CAIS released a new course, <a href="https://www.coursera.org/learn/intro-to-ai-safety">Introduction to AI Safety</a>, on Coursera. It is a free, non-technical course that assumes no prior background knowledge in AI and is intended to be accessible to everyone who is interested in learning more about AI risks and safety. It takes about four hours to complete.</p><p><strong>The course covers the basics of AI, its risks, and approaches to AI governance. </strong>Introduction to AI Safety has three modules. The first describes how the field of AI got to where it is today and the main factors driving further progress now, as well as potential bottlenecks to progress. The second module looks at the biggest risks of AI, including misuse by malicious actors, loss of control of AI systems, and the potential for AI to concentrate power. The third and final module explains why AI safety is a &#8220;wicked problem&#8221; and explores different approaches to AI governance, from safeguards on AI systems themselves, to governance at the corporate, national, and international levels.</p><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://newsletter.safe.ai/subscribe?"><span>Subscribe now</span></a></p><h1>In Other News</h1><h3><strong>Government</strong></h3><ul><li><p>President Trump <a href="https://x.com/WhiteHouse/status/2106731532694028310">announced</a> a new &#8220;Super Intelligence Force&#8221;
