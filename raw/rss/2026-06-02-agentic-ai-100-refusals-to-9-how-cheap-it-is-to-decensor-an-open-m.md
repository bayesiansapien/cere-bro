# 100 Refusals to 9: How Cheap It Is to Decensor an Open Model — and Why That’s a Policy Problem

<h2><strong>Abstract</strong></h2><p style="text-align: justify;"><em>On May 31, 2026, NPR reported</em> that the safety guardrails on freely downloadable &#8220;open-weight&#8221; AI models can now be stripped away by almost anyone, in minutes, on a cheap laptop. The story quotes <strong>Noam Schwartz</strong>, CEO of the AI-security firm <strong>Alice</strong>, who observes that what was once &#8220;the job of the data scientist&#8230; a senior employee&#8221; is now within reach of &#8220;everybody with access to the internet and a laptop for like 400 bucks.&#8221; One named technique&#8212;<strong>abliteration</strong>&#8212;and one named tool&#8212;<strong>Heretic</strong>, by developer Philipp Emanuel Weidmann&#8212;sit at the center of that claim. This report does three things. First, it explains the mechanism: refusal behavior in a language model is mediated by a single direction in activation space, and removing that direction removes the model&#8217;s ability to say &#8220;no.&#8221; Second, it documents a hands-on reproduction we ran on a consumer Intel Arc integrated GPU: across 100 optimization trials, refusals on a held-out harmful set fell from 100/100 to as low as 9/100&#8212;a 91% reduction&#8212;with the model&#8217;s general capabilities measurably intact (KL 0.063), in two roughly 44-minute batches, at essentially zero marginal cost. Third, it maps the governance gap this exposes: once weights are public, the only remaining locus of control is the downstream user, and &#8220;you can&#8217;t recall a download.&#8221; We survey the policy levers under debate and their openness-versus-safety tradeoffs.</p><h1><strong>1. The Warning</strong></h1><p style="text-align: justify;">The motivating piece for this report is an NPR article by Huo Jingnan, published May 31, 2026, under the headline &#8220;These AI models are free, private, and will never say &#8216;no.&#8217;&#8221; (NPR&#8217;s journalist authored the article; the experts below are featured and quoted in it, not its authors.) The article opens with the uncomfortable prompts that mainstream chatbots refuse&#8212;instructions for explosives, drugs, or mass violence&#8212;and then notes that &#8220;another type of AI model will never refuse to provide what the user asks for,&#8221; and that &#8220;in recent months, these models have become more accessible and popular.&#8221;</p><p style="text-align: justify;">The article&#8217;s central voice on the industry side is <strong>Noam Schwartz, CEO of the AI-security firm Alice</strong>, which performs red-teaming and safety evaluation for AI model developers. Schwartz frames both the promise and the peril in one sentence: &#8220;Everybody can download and operate their own state-of-the-art model and use it for great things and terrible things.&#8221; The democratization is real&#8212;and so is the loss of oversight. Unlike ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, open-weight models &#8220;run locally on users&#8217; computers, and don&#8217;t need the internet to function,&#8221; so &#8220;the companies behind them have no idea how they&#8217;re being used.&#8221;</p><p style="text-align: justify;">The article grounds the threat in evidence rather than hypotheticals. Hugging Face now lists <strong>over 6,000 abliterated models, up from about 600 in 2024</strong>, per research by the National Counterterrorism Innovation, Technology, and Education Center (NCITE), a DHS-supported consortium. NCITE&#8217;s Samuel Hunter warns that the most disturbing dimension is not raw information but encouragement&#8212;the &#8220;bubbly persona&#8221; of an abliterated model cheering on a vulnerable, isolated user &#8220;down a darker path.&#8221; In late April 2026, House lawmakers attended an NCITE demonstration of abliterated models. The capability gap is closing: per the International AI Safety Report chaired by Yoshua Bengio, open-weight models trail the best closed models by <em>less than one year</em>.</p><p style="text-align: justify;">Crucially, the article is balanced, and so is this report. Heretic&#8217;s own developer, Philipp Emanuel Weidmann, is quoted defending the work: AI is &#8220;just an information processing and retrieval system akin to a search engine,&#8221; and concentrating the power to decide &#8220;what is acceptable and is not acceptable&#8221; in &#8220;a very small set of entities&#8221; creates &#8220;a stifling intellectual climate.&#8221; &#8220;There&#8217;s too much power in AI,&#8221; he told NPR. &#8220;Unrestricted models being available to the powerful while not being available to anyone else will lock in power structures forever.&#8221; That argument deserves to be taken seriously, and we return to it.</p><h1><strong>2. How the Safety Layer Actually Works (and Comes Off)</strong></h1><p style="text-align: justify;">A modern instruction-tuned model is taught to refuse certain requests through alignment training (RLHF and related methods). The striking empirical finding behind abliteration&#8212;established by Arditi et al. in &#8220;Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction&#8221; (arXiv:2406.11717, NeurIPS 2024)&#8212;is that this refusal behavior is, to a first approximation, <strong>one-dimensional</strong>. For each layer, one can compute a single vector&#8212;a &#8220;refusal direction&#8221;&#8212;in the residual stream such that erasing that direction stops the model from refusing harmful instructions, while adding it makes the model refuse even harmless ones. The authors validated this across 13 open chat models up to 72B parameters.</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>Computing the direction. </strong>Run two batched sets of prompts through the model: a <em>harmful</em> set and a <em>harmless</em> set. At each layer, take the mean residual-stream activation for each set; the difference of means is an estimate of the refusal direction for that layer. Intuitively, it is the axis along which &#8220;this request should be refused&#8221; is encoded.</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>Removing it (abliteration). </strong>To make the model stop refusing, project that direction out of the weights&#8212;orthogonalize the relevant projection matrices against it&#8212;so the model can no longer write to or read from that axis. Heretic applies this surgically to the components that matter for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507: the attention output projections (attn.o_proj, 36 of them) and the MLP down-projections (mlp.down_proj, 36 of them). Rather than overwriting the base weights, it expresses the subtraction as <strong>LoRA adapters</strong> targeting down_proj and o_proj, with row-normalization set to FULL and orthogonalize_direction enabled. The original paper called this &#8220;a novel white-box jailbreak&#8230; that surgically disables refusal with minimal effect on other capabilities.&#8221;</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>Why it stays smart. </strong>Because the edit is rank-limited and aimed at a single direction, most of what the model knows is untouched. The engineering challenge is to remove <em>enough</em> of the refusal behavior without distorting the rest of the model&#8212;a tradeoff that, as the next section shows, Heretic turns into a formal optimization problem. Heretic also credits a &#8220;projected abliteration&#8221; variant (grimjim) among the refinements that improve this capability-preservation tradeoff.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vWGG!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff9cca78c-60d5-4a25-baa2-3dd890d6b18f_1980x1140.png" target="_blank"><div class="image2-inset"><source type="image/webp" /><img alt="Figure 1. Optuna&#8217;s <strong>multivariate-TPE</strong> sampler jointly minimizes both, building up a Pareto front of trials that trade refusals against fidelity. Why This Matters for Policy</strong></h1><p style="text-align: justify;">The reproduction makes an abstract debate concrete. Three properties of what we just did drive the entire governance problem.</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>1. Asymmetry of cost. </strong>Safety tuning is expensive&#8212;large alignment datasets, human feedback, repeated training runs. Removing it is cheap, automated, and getting cheaper: one command, ~44 minutes, ~free, on hardware millions of people own. The defender pays a fortune; the remover pays almost nothing.</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>2. Irreversibility. </strong>Once weights are public, they are public forever. As the framing goes, <strong>you can&#8217;t recall a download</strong>. A model pulled by hundreds of thousands of users cannot be un-released; takedowns on Hugging Face affect future downloads, not the copies already on disk and running offline.</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>3. Loss of observability. </strong>Abliterated models run locally, with no telemetry back to the developer. The lab that trained the model genuinely &#8220;has no idea how it&#8217;s being used.&#8221; Post-hoc monitoring, usage policies, and API-level filters&#8212;the backbone of closed-model safety&#8212;simply do not apply.</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>The marginal-risk question. </strong>The serious version of the policy debate is not &#8220;is this dangerous?&#8221; but &#8220;what is the <em>marginal</em> risk relative to what a determined actor could already do with search engines, closed models, or expertise?&#8221; The 2024 NTIA report on dual-use models with widely available weights adopted exactly this posture: it recommended the U.S. government <strong>monitor</strong> rather than restrict open weights for currently available systems, collecting evidence on capabilities and acting only when indicators cross thresholds. The International AI Safety Report cautions that the calculus shifts as the capability gap shrinks&#8212;and notes the core dual-use bind directly: &#8220;Features enabling beneficial applications in medicine or research can be repurposed for harm, and once weights are public, distinguishing legitimate from malicious uses can be difficult.&#8221;</p><p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>Where the law is heading. </strong>The EU AI Act carves out partial exemptions for free and open-source general-purpose AI models&#8212;while preserving obligations for models posing systemic risk&#8212;so &#8220;open&#8221; status is becoming a regulated category rather than a free pass. Adjacent levers under discussion include <strong>export controls</strong> on frontier weights, <strong>KYC-for-weights</strong> (identity/use attestation before download of the most capable models), mandatory <strong>pre-release dangerous-capability evaluations</strong>, and <strong>liability</strong> allocation when an abliterated derivative causes harm.
