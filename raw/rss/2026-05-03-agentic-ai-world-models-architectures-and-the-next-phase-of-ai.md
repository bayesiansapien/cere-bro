# World Models, Architectures, and the Next Phase of AI

<h2>A debate, not a duel</h2><p>At the Spring School AI For Impact in March 2026, two of the most consequential thinkers in machine learning sat for what was billed as a debate but functioned more like a forensic disagreement about what intelligence is for (<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8LKgvrNYZz0">YouTube recording</a>). On one side, Yann LeCun, Meta&#8217;s former chief AI scientist, argued that any system that models the world by trying to reconstruct pixels is doomed because most of what happens in a video - leaves trembling, light flickering, individual molecules of air - is intrinsically unpredictable, and forcing a network to predict it wastes capacity and corrupts representations (<a href="https://ai.meta.com/blog/v-jepa-yann-lecun-ai-model-video-joint-embedding-predictive-architecture/">Meta AI on V-JEPA</a>). On the other side, Eric Xing, president of MBZUAI and longtime CMU professor, argued that abstract latent prediction without a generative validator is essentially meditation in a closed room: elegant, internally consistent, and prone to losing contact with reality (<a href="https://mbzuai.ac.ae/news/how-mbzuai-built-pan-an-interactive-general-world-model-capable-of-long-horizon-simulation/">MBZUAI LeCun&#8217;s Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) trains an encoder to map observations into representations, then trains a predictor to map a representation of the past into a representation of the future, and stops there. There is no decoder, no reconstruction, no insistence that the system explain how the world looks (<a href="https://openreview.net/pdf?id=BZ5a1r-kVsf">LeCun&#8217;s &#8220;A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence&#8221;, 2022</a>). Xing&#8217;s Generative Latent Prediction (GLP), which underlies the PAN world model his Institute of Foundation Models built, keeps the latent prediction but reattaches a decoder so the model has to render the next observation as a check on what the latents claim is happening (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05169">Xing et al., &#8220;Critiques of World Models&#8221;, arXiv 2507.05169</a>). One is faith in abstraction. The other is insistence that abstraction be validated against the messy world that produced it.</p><p>Stripped of its technical detail, this is the oldest fight in the philosophy of science: rationalism versus empiricism, played out in tensors. Should the model represent the world more compactly than the world itself, throwing away anything it cannot use? Or must it be perpetually re-grounded in observable reconstructions, even at vast computational cost? Almost everything else interesting in AI right now - diffusion versus autoregression, transformers versus state-space models, video generators versus simulators, agents versus oracles, the whole question of whether and when AI starts improving itself - connects, sometimes loosely and sometimes directly, to which side of that argument turns out to be right.</p><p>This essay walks through that argument. It begins with what a world model actually is, traces the architectural lineage from Sutton and Schmidhuber to Dreamer and Sora, sets the JEPA and GLP positions against the broader landscape of transformers, diffusion, and state-space models, then connects all of it to the rapidly maturing world of agentic AI and recursive self-improvement. The thesis throughout is that neither LeCun nor Xing is fully right, and that the next decade will be defined by hybrid architectures that learn when to trust abstraction and when to demand reconstruction. The harder question is whether we will figure that out before the systems we build are powerful enough to start figuring it out for themselves.</p><h2>What a world model is, and why it matters</h2><p>A world model is a learned function that takes a state of the world and an action, and predicts what comes next. That is the technical definition. The cognitive intuition is older: a system with a world model can rehearse possible futures inside its own head, evaluate them, and choose actions without paying the cost of trying everything in reality. Richard Sutton named this idea in his 1990 Dyna architecture, where an agent maintained a learned model of the environment and used it to generate &#8220;simulated experience&#8221; that supplemented real interaction (<a href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/122344.122377">Sutton, &#8220;Integrated Architectures for Learning, Planning, and Reacting&#8221;, 1990</a>). J&#252;rgen Schmidhuber&#8217;s parallel work in 1990 and 1991 made the same proposal for recurrent networks, framing curiosity and planning around models that compress and predict (<a href="https://people.idsia.ch/~juergen/world-models-planning-curiosity-fki-1990.html">Schmidhuber&#8217;s historical overview</a>).</p><p>The 2018 Ha and Schmidhuber paper &#8220;World Models&#8221; is where the modern era starts. They built an agent for the CarRacing and VizDoom environments using three pieces: a convolutional variational autoencoder that compressed each frame into a 32- or 64-dimensional vector, a mixture density recurrent network that predicted the next latent given an action, and a tiny linear controller with under a thousand parameters (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122">Ha and Schmidhuber, &#8220;World Models&#8221;, arXiv 1803.10122</a>; <a href="https://worldmodels.github.io">interactive paper</a>). The controller was trained with evolutionary search, but the striking result was that the agent could be trained entirely inside its own dream - the recurrent network running as a virtual environment - and then deployed in the real one. The world model achieved 906 &#177; 21 on CarRacing-v0, clearing the threshold considered to solve the task, and 1092 &#177; 556 on VizDoom when trained purely in dream and tested in reality.</p><p>The reason this matters is that learning by interaction with the real world is dangerous, slow, and expensive. A robot that learns to grasp by trying every possible grasp on a real arm will break either itself or whatever it is grasping. A self-driving car that learns by collision is unacceptable. The promise of world models is that an agent can practice in imagination, and that imagination can be cheaper, faster, and safer than reality. The Moerland et al. survey on model-based reinforcement learning lays out the canonical version of this argument and the canonical risk: a model-based agent can also learn to exploit inaccuracies in its model rather than the true environment, finding policies that look perfect in dream and fail catastrophically in deployment (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16712">Moerland et al., 2020</a>).</p><p>The Dreamer line, developed by Danijar Hafner and collaborators across three papers, is the cleanest demonstration that this works at scale. PlaNet introduced the Recurrent State-Space Model, which split the latent state into a deterministic GRU branch and a stochastic Gaussian branch, and used it to plan via cross-entropy method search; it achieved fifty times the data efficiency of the model-free D4PG baseline on the DeepMind Control Suite (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04551">Hafner et al., &#8220;Learning Latent Dynamics for Planning from Pixels&#8221;, ICML 2019</a>). Dreamer V1 replaced explicit search with an actor-critic that trained inside imagination, backpropagating policy gradients through the world model itself (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01603">Hafner et al., &#8220;Dream to Control&#8221;, ICLR 2020</a>). Dreamer V2 introduced discrete categorical latents and was the first world-model agent to reach human-level performance on the 55-task Atari benchmark (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02193">Hafner et al., &#8220;Mastering Atari with Discrete World Models&#8221;, 2020</a>). Dreamer V3, in 2023, used a single hyperparameter configuration to outperform specialized methods on more than 150 tasks across DMControl, Atari, BSuite, Crafter, and Minecraft, and was the first agent to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch with no human demonstrations or curricula (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04104">Hafner et al., &#8220;Mastering Diverse Domains through World Models&#8221;, 2023</a>).</p><p>DeepMind&#8217;s MuZero is the planning-centric counterpart. Rather than try to model the world, MuZero learns only an abstract latent dynamics that captures the three things needed for planning: value, policy, and reward. It then uses Monte Carlo tree search inside that latent space. In Nature in 2020 it matched AlphaZero on Go, chess, and shogi while also reaching new state-of-the-art on Atari, despite never being told the rules of any of those games (<a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03051-4">Schrittwieser et al., &#8220;Mastering Atari, Go, Chess and Shogi by Planning with a Learned Model&#8221;, Nature 2020</a>; <a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/muzero-mastering-go-chess-shogi-and-atari-without-rules/">DeepMind blog</a>). EfficientZero pushed MuZero further, reaching 194.3% mean human performance on Atari 100k with only about two hours of game experience by adding self-supervised consistency losses (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00210">Ye et al., &#8220;Mastering Atari Games with Limited Data&#8221;, 2021</a>).</p><p>These early systems share a structure that defines the field. There is some way to encode observations into a state, some way to roll the state forward in time given actions, and some way to extract decisions or rewards. Everything that has happened since - JEPA, Sora, Genie, Cosmos, Marble, PAN - is a variation on those three pieces, with sharply different choices about what each piece should look like and what should be done with the rolled-forward state.</p><h2>The architectures, and what each one is good for</h2><p>The dominant architectural debate in machine learning has converged around four families of sequence models - transformers, diffusion models, state-space models, and recurrent networks - and around the hybrids built from them. Each family makes a different bet about how information should be stored, accessed, and propagated in time, and those bets translate directly into different strengths and weaknesses for world modeling.</p><p>Transformers replace recurrence with global attention. Each token attends to every other token, and the resulting representation is a learned, content-addressable summary of the entire context. This makes them outstanding at long-range dependency capture but pays a quadratic cost in memory and compute. The TransDreamer paper, which swapped Dreamer&#8217;s GRU-based recurrent state-space model for a transformer state-space model, showed that on a hidden-order discovery task with four balls the transformer achieved a 23% success rate compared to 7% for DreamerV2, and that it produced sharper imagined frames and more accurate reward predictions when given more context (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2202.09481v2">Chen et al., &#8220;TransDreamer&#8221;, arXiv 2202.09481</a>). The price was memory: TransDreamer could imagine from only three states per replay sample, against DreamerV2&#8217;s full replay, because the attention mechanism&#8217;s KV-cache made longer rollouts infeasible. A formal paper on next-latent prediction transformers proved that such systems can converge to belief states - compressed representations of history sufficient for future prediction - establishing a theoretical link between transformer language modeling and world modeling (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05963v1">Next-Latent Prediction Transformers, arXiv 2511.05963</a>).</p><p>Diffusion models take a fundamentally different approach. They learn to invert a noise-adding process, modeling the entire distribution over outputs through iterative denoising. This makes them exceptional at generating diverse, high-fidelity samples, including video, and gives them an explicit mechanism for representing uncertainty about multimodal futures. A recent ICLR 2026 result proves that DDPM&#8217;s denoising score matching is asymptotically efficient - the estimator achieves the Cram&#233;r-Rao lower bound - establishing strong statistical grounding for the family (<a href="https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/35338">DDPM Score Matching Is Asymptotically Efficient, ICLR 2026</a>). Lilian Weng&#8217;s diffusion video survey traces the rapid maturation of both U-Net-based and transformer-based diffusion video models, including Imagen Video, VDM, and Sora (<a href="https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2024-04-12-diffusion-video/">Lilian Weng, &#8220;Diffusion Models for Video Generation&#8221;</a>). The DOME paper showed that domain-specific diffusion world models can predict future 3D occupancy frames for autonomous driving with high fidelity (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10429">Gu et al., &#8220;DOME&#8221;, arXiv 2410.10429</a>). The cost of all this expressivity is inference: each sample requires dozens to hundreds of sequential forward passes through the denoising network, which puts pure diffusion outside the regime of fast rollouts that reinforcement learning needs.</p><p>Diffusion transformers (DiTs), introduced by William Peebles and Saining Xie in 2023, replace the U-Net backbone of a diffusion model with a transformer operating on patch tokens. OpenAI&#8217;s Sora is the most prominent application: a video compression network produces spatiotemporal latent codes, those codes are split into spacetime patches that act as transformer tokens, and the diffusion objective trains the model to denoise them (<a href="https://openai.com/index/video-generation-models-as-world-simulators/">OpenAI, &#8220;Video generation models as world simulators&#8221;, 2024</a>). OpenAI&#8217;s claim was that Sora was a path toward general-purpose physical simulators, with emergent properties - 3D consistency under camera motion, long-range object permanence, persistent painter strokes, bite marks left on a burger - arising from scale alone. The same report admitted, in plain language, that Sora &#8220;does not accurately model the physics of many basic interactions, like glass shattering,&#8221; that long-duration samples become incoherent, and that objects appear spontaneously. A subsequent academic survey concluded that Sora shows &#8220;the beginnings of a world model&#8221; but does not yet constitute one (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2405.03520v1">Liu et al., &#8220;Is Sora a World Simulator?&#8221;, arXiv 2405.03520</a>).</p><p>Autoregressive video models, by contrast, generate frame by frame in causal order, like a language model generating tokens. DeepMind&#8217;s Genie 2 used an autoregressive latent diffusion architecture: a causal transformer predicts the next latent, and a diffusion head renders the actual visual content for each predicted latent frame (<a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-2-a-large-scale-foundation-world-model/">DeepMind, &#8220;Genie 2&#8221;, 2024</a>). The result was a system that could be prompted with a single image and then driven interactively with keyboard and mouse to generate consistent virtual worlds for up to a minute, with object permanence for off-screen regions and emergent physics like water and smoke. Genie 3, in August 2025, achieved real-time interactive generation at 24 frames per second and 720p resolution, with consistency maintained for several minutes (<a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/">DeepMind, &#8220;Genie 3&#8221;, 2025</a>). It uses no explicit 3D representation - no neural radiance fields, no Gaussian splatting - and is purely autoregressive, with the trajectory growing as the model plays. The iVideoGPT line scaled the autoregressive video transformer to integrate visual observations, actions, and rewards into a unified sequence, making it directly applicable as a world model for RL agents (<a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/96668">iVideoGPT, NeurIPS 2024</a>).</p><p>State-space models occupy the most interesting niche in this taxonomy. The structured state-space model (S4), introduced by Albert Gu and colleagues, parameterized the system matrix as a diagonal-plus-low-rank construction that allows efficient convolutional training and recurrent inference (<a href="https://srush.github.io/annotated-s4/">The Annotated S4</a>). Mamba added selective state spaces, where the matrices A, B, and C are functions of the input, allowing the model to selectively propagate or forget information based on content rather than only position; combined with a hardware-aware parallel scan it achieves five times the throughput of a transformer at inference and linear scaling in sequence length (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752">Gu and Dao, &#8220;Mamba&#8221;, arXiv 2312.00752</a>). A unified theoretical framework expresses attention, SSMs, and RNNs all as linear structured time-varying dynamical systems, clarifying the family resemblance (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15731v2">Gu et al., &#8220;Attention, State Space Models, and Recurrent Neural Networks&#8221;, arXiv 2405.15731</a>).</p><p>For world modeling, state-space models have been quietly excellent. The 2023 NeurIPS paper &#8220;Facing Off World Model Backbones&#8221; benchmarked S4 against transformers and RNNs and found that S4WM outperformed both on long-term memory tasks and was more efficient during training and imagination (<a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2023/poster/72223">Deng et al., NeurIPS 2023</a>). DRAMA, a Mamba-2-based world model, achieved 105% normalized human performance on Atari100k with only 7 million parameters, against DreamerV3&#8217;s 200 million (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08893v1">Wang et al., &#8220;DRAMA&#8221;, arXiv 2410.08893</a>). GLAM introduced global-local variation awareness on top of a Mamba backbone, mimicking how humans infer trends from temporal variation (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11949v1">GLAM, arXiv 2501.11949</a>). The catch is that Mamba&#8217;s selective compression into a fixed-size hidden state means it loses information that was deemed irrelevant at the time of compression, which can hurt content-based retrieval tasks where precise recall matters.</p><p>Recurrent networks, the original world-model substrate, are not gone. The Recurrent State-Space Model is still the heart of every Dreamer variant. xLSTM, introduced by Sepp Hochreiter&#8217;s group, revises classical LSTM with exponential gating and matrix-valued memory, addressing the degradation classical LSTMs exhibit at long sequences and large scales (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13427">xLSTM 7B, arXiv 2503.13427</a>; <a href="https://www.nx-ai.com/en/technology">NX-AI on xLSTM</a>). The Large Recurrent Action Model trained with xLSTM on 432 tasks across six domains and compared favorably to transformers in both performance and inference speed (<a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/100902">LRAM, NeurIPS 2024</a>). The R2I paper proposed a Dreamer-style agent with improved memory, demonstrating superior performance on tasks where long-term dependencies between actions and outcomes are separated by many steps (<a href="https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/0a0e436e8830f746300f592377730fca-Paper-Conference.pdf">R2I, ICLR 2024</a>).</p><p>Hybrids are now the empirical default. AI21&#8217;s Jamba was the first large-scale hybrid, interleaving attention and Mamba layers at a 1:7 ratio with mixture-of-experts and supporting 256K-token contexts (<a href="https://www.ai21.com/blog/rise-of-hybrid-llms/">AI21 on hybrid LLMs</a>). NVIDIA&#8217;s Nemotron-H replaced 92% of attention layers with Mamba2 blocks, achieving three times the throughput of LLaMA-3.1 while matching its accuracy on MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, and MATH. For world models specifically, the dominant pattern is autoregressive temporal structure with diffusion spatial generation: Genie 2 and Genie 3 instantiate this, and the Astra paper formalizes it as autoregressive denoising, where a pretrained video diffusion model is augmented with action-aware adapters and generation proceeds chunk-by-chunk in autoregressive fashion (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08931v1">Astra, arXiv 2512.08931</a>). Astra outperforms pure diffusion and earlier hybrids on instruction following and consistency. Diffusion Mamba replaces self-attention in DiTs with Mamba SSM layers to retain quality at linear complexity (<a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/95642">Diffusion Mamba, NeurIPS 2024</a>).</p><p>The honest summary across all of these is that no single architecture currently solves the joint problem of long-horizon consistency, physical plausibility, real-time generation, and sample efficiency under partial observability. Transformers and DiTs win on visual fidelity and open-ended generation. Mamba and xLSTM win on inference speed and rollout efficiency. RNNs, even simple GRUs, remain strong inside Dreamer-style architectures because the bottleneck has often turned out to be regularization and loss design rather than the recurrence itself. The Algorithma analysis argues that the scaling law, once a reliable guide, now predicts diminishing returns, and that the next leap in AI requires architectural innovation rather than additional parameters and data (<a href="https://www.algorithma.ai/articles/beyond-the-scaling-laws-why-the-next-leap-in-ai-requires-an-architectural-revolution">Algorithma, &#8220;Beyond the Scaling Laws&#8221;</a>). The fundamental-limits paper catalogues five structural weaknesses of LLMs at scale: hallucination, context compression, reasoning degradation, retrieval fragility, and multimodal misalignment (<a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2511.12869v1">Fundamental Limits of LLMs at Scale, arXiv 2511.12869</a>).</p><h2>The schools of world modeling, and the territory they claim</h2><p>Architectures are tools. What people actually do with them is what produces the schools that now shape the field. A clean way to read the current landscape is as five overlapping camps, each making different claims about what a world model is and what it is for.</p><p>The first is the agent-centric camp, descended directly from Ha and Schmidhuber. Its representative systems are PlaNet, Dreamer V1 through V3, MuZero, and EfficientZero. The defining commitment is that a world model is valuable insofar as it improves an agent&#8217;s policy. Everything else - visual fidelity, physical realism, interactivity for humans - is a means to that end. The Dreamer V3 Minecraft diamond result is the most legible single demonstration that a learned latent dynamics, trained with a relatively standard reconstruction-plus-KL objective, can let an agent practice in imagination well enough to solve a long-horizon, sparse-reward task that had defeated every previous reinforcement learning system (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04104">Hafner et al., 2023</a>). MuZero&#8217;s Nature paper showed that you do not need to model the whole observation; you need to model only what matters for value, policy, and reward (<a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03051-4">Nature 2020</a>). The agent-centric camp tends to be quietly skeptical of the rest of the field, on the grounds that beautiful videos are not actually evidence that anything has been learned about the world.</p><p>The second is the foundation-world-model camp, anchored by DeepMind&#8217;s Genie line and NVIDIA&#8217;s Cosmos. Its claim is that world models should be trained at the scale of foundation models, on enormous quantities of internet video, and treated as substrates for downstream agent training. Genie 1, an 11-billion-parameter system, was the first model widely described as a foundation world model: it learned a latent action space from unlabeled video and could generate playable 2D environments (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15391">Bruce et al., &#8220;Genie&#8221;, arXiv 2402.15391</a>). Genie 2 extended this to 3D environments generated from a single image, and Genie 3 made the system real-time and explicitly framed it as &#8220;a key stepping stone on the path to AGI&#8221; (<a href="https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/">DeepMind blog, 2025</a>). NVIDIA&#8217;s Cosmos platform took a different commercial position: a 9,000-trillion-token training corpus drawn from autonomous driving and robotics video, with autoregressive and diffusion variants in nano, super, and ultra sizes, all open-weight under permissive license (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03575">Cosmos World Foundation Model Platform, arXiv 2501.03575</a>; <a href="https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/advancing-physical-ai-with-nvidia-cosmos-world-foundation-model-platform/">NVIDIA developer blog</a>). On a static-scene 3D consistency benchmark, Cosmos 1.0 Diffusion Text2World 7B achieved a Sampson error of 0.355 and 62.6% pose-estimation success against a VideoLDM baseline of 0.841 and 4.4%. The Cosmos report acknowledges that even at this scale, the generated videos still suffer from object impermanence and gravity violations.</p><p>The third is the spatial-intelligence camp, defined by Fei-Fei Li&#8217;s World Labs and its Marble product. Its argument is that language models trained on text cannot develop true 3D understanding, and that the next foundation for AI is spatial intelligence - systems that reconstruct, generate, and simulate three-dimensional space (<a href="https://www.worldlabs.ai/blog/marble-world-model">World Labs blog</a>). Marble, launched in November 2025, generates full 3D worlds from text, single images, multi-image sets, short videos, or coarse 3D layouts, and exports Gaussian splats, triangle meshes, and pixel-accurate camera-controlled videos. World Labs raised over a billion dollars by early 2026 (<a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmarkman/2026/03/06/world-labs-1-billion-bet-to-advance-spatial-intelligence/">Forbes coverage</a>). The Xing critique of this camp is that static 3D environments without dynamic agents, physics, or interactivity are insufficient for physical causality, multi-agent behavior, or goal-driven planning, and therefore are not world models in the demanding sense (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05169">Critiques of World Models</a>). The counter-response from World Labs is that spatial reconstruction is a foundation; physics and interaction can be added on top.</p><p>The fourth is the video-as-simulation camp, instantiated by Sora and the academic literature that grew up around it. Its claim, in OpenAI&#8217;s words, was that &#8220;scaling video generation models is a promising path towards building general purpose simulators of the physical world&#8221; (<a href="https://openai.com/index/video-generation-models-as-world-simulators/">OpenAI 2024</a>). The evidence cited was emergent: 3D consistency, object permanence, world interaction with painted strokes and bite marks, and the zero-shot Minecraft demonstration where Sora simultaneously controlled a player with a basic policy while rendering the surrounding world. The counter-evidence was equally direct: glass-shattering failures, eating without state changes, spontaneous object appearance, and incoherence at long durations. The practical constraint is that video generation at world-model fidelity remains expensive, slow, and difficult to turn into reliable action-conditioned simulators. The Xing critique was sharper: Sora and its peers are &#8220;pixel-level video synthesis tools&#8221; that &#8220;fall outside world model definition for reasoning/planning&#8221; because they produce fixed trajectories, not state-action-conditioned simulators (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05169">arXiv 2507.05169</a>).</p><p>The fifth is the abstraction-and-validation camp, where LeCun&#8217;s JEPA line and Xing&#8217;s PAN line both live, despite their disagreement. The JEPA position, articulated in LeCun&#8217;s 2022 white paper, is that generative reconstruction of pixels is wasteful: most details cannot be predicted, and forcing a model to predict them causes blurry outputs and wasted capacity (<a href="https://openreview.net/pdf?id=BZ5a1r-kVsf">LeCun 2022</a>). The architectural choice is to encode both observations and predict in abstract representation space, regularizing against representation collapse with methods like VICReg. I-JEPA, the image variant, predicts the representations of multiple target blocks from a single context block in the same image, using non-generative semantic-level objectives, and a ViT-Huge/14 trained on ImageNet in under 72 hours on 16 A100s (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08243">Assran et al., I-JEPA, CVPR 2023</a>). V-JEPA extended this to video, with masking strategies that force scene-level understanding and 1.5 to 6 times improvement in training and sample efficiency over generative video approaches (<a href="https://ai.meta.com/blog/v-jepa-yann-lecun-ai-model-video-joint-embedding-predictive-architecture/">Meta AI, V-JEPA blog</a>). V-JEPA 2, in 2025, pre-trained on more than a million hours of internet video at the 8-billion-parameter scale and achieved state-of-the-art on Something-Something v2 (77.3 top-1 accuracy), Epic-Kitchens-100 (39.7 recall-at-5), and PerceptionTest (84.0 after LLM alignment). Its action-conditioned variant, V-JEPA 2-AC, was post-trained from less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot videos and deployed zero-shot on Franka robotic arms in two different labs to perform pick-and-place with image goals, with no robot-specific data and no task-specific training (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09985">V-JEPA 2, arXiv 2506.09985</a>). This is the single strongest empirical case for the JEPA thesis: web-scale self-supervised video pretraining, plus minimal action-labeled data, transfers to physical robots.</p><p>The PAN position from Xing&#8217;s group at MBZUAI accepts the JEPA argument that prediction must happen in abstract space but rejects the conclusion that reconstruction should be discarded. Their architecture, called Generative Latent Prediction, has a vision encoder that turns observations into latents, an autoregressive LLM-based backbone that evolves the latent state forward in time conditioned on natural-language actions, and a video diffusion decoder that renders the next observation as a check (<a href="https://mbzuai.ac.ae/news/how-mbzuai-built-pan-an-interactive-general-world-model-capable-of-long-horizon-simulation/">MBZUAI on PAN</a>; <a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/137008">NeurIPS 2025 talk</a>). The decoder uses what Xing&#8217;s group calls a Causal Swin-DPM mechanism, a sliding-window denoising process that keeps transitions smooth and prevents drift in long simulations. PAN can be prompted with natural-language actions like &#8220;drive through a snowy forest&#8221; or &#8220;grasp the yellow can from the middle tray&#8221; and produces stateful, action-conditioned rollouts. The defining mathematical property they report is that the generative loss is a strict upper bound on the latent loss, so minimizing the generative loss also minimizes the latent loss, while minimizing the latent loss alone does not necessarily minimize the generative loss. The interpretation is that reconstruction acts as a guardrail: it prevents the encoder and predictor from quietly degenerating into representations that are internally consistent but disconnected from observable reality.</p><p>These five camps are genuinely incompatible on some technical questions and genuinely compatible on others. They all agree that prediction in abstract space is necessary; they disagree on whether prediction is sufficient. They all agree that scale matters; they disagree on whether scale of pixels or scale of structure is the limiting factor. They all agree that real-world deployment matters; they disagree on whether the bottleneck is data, architecture, or evaluation. The PAN team&#8217;s &#8220;Critiques of World Models&#8221; paper is unusually clean as a structuring document because it lays out the taxonomy explicitly and forces each camp to specify what it is claiming.</p><h2>The deeper dispute</h2><p>The technical surface of the LeCun-Xing debate is whether to attach a decoder. The deeper dispute is what counts as predictive success.</p><p>LeCun&#8217;s argument, which he illustrated in the debate with the example of an ideal-gas law and entropy, is that meaningful prediction requires throwing away unpredictable information. Consider a sealed box of gas. No physicist tries to simulate the trajectories of individual molecules. The pressure-volume-temperature relationship - PV = nRT - is the abstraction physicists use precisely because it ignores the molecular details. The Navier-Stokes equations do the same thing for fluid dynamics, working at the scale of small cubes of air rather than individual particles. Biochemistry, psychology, sociology - every level of scientific description is defined by which details it discards. LeCun&#8217;s position is that machine learning systems should do the same thing automatically: the encoder should learn what to ignore. If you point a camera at a room and ask the model to predict what the room will look like in five seconds, the system has no business predicting where every leaf on every plant will be, because that information is not predictable. Forcing it to make those predictions, in his words, &#8220;kills it.&#8221;</p><p>Xing&#8217;s response is that &#8220;useless&#8221; and &#8220;unpredictable&#8221; are not the same thing. Doctors take tissue samples even when they do not know what nuances will turn out to matter. A self-driving car may not need to predict each leaf, but it does need to know that there are leaves rather than fog, that there is wind rather than calm, that the lighting indicates evening rather than morning. A world model trained to discard whatever it cannot predict will be hyper-specialized to whatever distribution it saw; on a downstream task that uses information it threw away, the model will have to relearn features from scratch. The generative reconstruction objective in GLP forces the system to keep that information available. The decoder may not need to render every leaf accurately, but it has to render something that is consistent with the whole observation, which means the latents have to remain rich enough to support reconstruction.</p><p>The strongest version of the Xing argument is the identifiability claim. A pure latent predictor can collapse: the easiest way to make the predicted representation match the encoded next representation is to force both encoder outputs to be a constant. LeCun acknowledged this in the debate and described it as &#8220;the system happily wants to collapse.&#8221; The standard JEPA fix is a regularizer like VICReg or SIGRAG that maximizes the information content coming out of the encoder, but this is mathematically delicate to characterize and easy to misconfigure. By contrast, a generative reconstruction loss naturally prevents collapse: a constant encoder output makes the decoder unable to reconstruct anything, so the gradient from the decoder pushes the encoder away from collapse without needing a hand-designed regularizer.</p><p>The strongest version of the LeCun argument is the impossibility claim. Most of the information in a high-dimensional sensory stream is intrinsically unpredictable. If you train a model to predict it anyway, the model&#8217;s capacity is consumed modeling the unpredictable parts, and the predictable parts get worse. LeCun spent fifteen years trying to make pixel-level video prediction work and concluded, after a decade of failure, that the blurriness in his predictions was not a numerical artifact but a structural property: when you minimize a pixel-level reconstruction loss against an inherently stochastic future, the optimal solution is the average of all possible futures, which looks blurry. Latent variable models, including diffusion, can express the multimodality but at a cost that scales with the dimension of what you are trying to reconstruct. JEPA&#8217;s response is to make the prediction at a representation level where the multimodality has been compressed away by the encoder.</p><p>There is no clean empirical resolution yet. V-JEPA 2&#8217;s zero-shot robot control is the strongest evidence for the JEPA approach: a system that never learned to render pixels was nevertheless able to plan and act on a real Franka arm using image goals. PAN&#8217;s own results - best open-source fidelity in agentic scenarios, strongest transition smoothness, and improved task success in tabletop manipulation - are the strongest evidence for GLP, though the PAN team has not yet published peer-reviewed quantitative benchmarks against V-JEPA 2 directly. The most honest read is that V-JEPA 2 wins on perception and short-horizon control where the encoder&#8217;s discarded information genuinely was not needed, and that GLP-style architectures should win on long-horizon, language-conditioned, multi-agent tasks where the discarded information turns out to matter. Whether either approach generalizes to the open-ended physical world is unknown.</p><p>A deeper observation: the architectural debate may be less important than it looks because a generative model with a sufficiently weak reconstruction loss is approximately a JEPA, and a JEPA with a sufficiently strong information-content regularizer is approximately a generative model. PAN&#8217;s authors note that GLP &#8220;strictly subsumes&#8221; JEPA - turn off the generative function, and you have a JEPA. LeCun, for his part, acknowledged that a JEPA could in principle be augmented with a decoder for evaluation, though he insisted such a decoder should not be on the training path. The terrain is genuinely contested, but the contest has been moving toward a hybrid view in which the question is not whether to generate but when, and against what, and at what cost.</p><h2>From world models to agents</h2><p></p>
