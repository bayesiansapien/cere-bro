# How Bankers Are Turning Tech Stock Volatility Into Cash

<p>Tech firms spending heavily on AI buildouts have flooded the market with new stock sales this year. And with investments in AI infrastructure soaking up so much investor cash, raising money for other capital-hungry areas of tech like quantum computing and robotics is requiring especially creative dealmaking.&nbsp;</p><p>For companies that are already public but need more cash for ambitious projects, sharp swings in their share prices are making it tough to sell more stock at an attractive price. Bankers say one workaround is turning that volatility into a selling point by bundling shares with warrants, which give investors the right to buy more stock later at a locked-in price. If investors are willing to pay up for a chance at a future payoff, companies can sell stock at a premium rather than the usual discount for new share sales.&nbsp;</p><p>One recent example is quantum computing company IonQ, which raised $2 billion in mid-October at a 15% premium to its prior closing price. In the IonQ deal, investors got stock plus warrants to buy more shares at double the stock’s pre-offering price within the next seven years, an extra way for them to profit if IonQ’s stock soars. IonQ has raised $5 billion in total through three such offerings since July 2025, helping pay for an acquisition spree that expanded its business into chip manufacturing and cybersecurity. The company’s stock has been volatile over that period, falling sharply from an October 2025 high of $82, rebounding to about $70 this May and then tumbling again. The shares currently trade around $21.&nbsp;</p><p>With tech investors particularly skittish right now, there’s more reason for cash-hungry companies to pounce on any opportunities to raise money. That’s also boosting the appeal of at-the-market stock sales, which allow companies to sell shares gradually into the open market, giving them the flexibility to take advantage of rallies. And while less conventional ways of raising equity were once viewed on Wall Street as signs of desperation, bankers say that stigma has faded in recent years as companies have embraced the benefits of having more options for raising cash.&nbsp;</p><p>“Historically, ATMs and some of these more creative financing tools have been a bit of a scarlet letter...it was hard to get a management team or board around them,” said Connor Leahey, managing director at Cohen &amp; Co. Capital Markets. “And now it's totally shifted.”</p><p>But getting to the public market in the first place has also become much tougher. Investors have had to sop up a <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/articles/ai-stock-can-market-absorb">surge in new stock </a>issued by already-public tech giants this year alongside mega listings from SpaceX and last week’s debut by Anthropic, with most of those shares performing poorly after their IPOs. The broader market for tech debuts has all but dried up, and Anthropic’s rapid drop below its offering price doesn’t bode well for other IPO hopefuls.&nbsp;</p><p>That helps explain a comparative burst of activity in mergers with special purpose acquisition companies. Five companies went public via a SPAC merger in October, including two space technology firms and a semiconductor tech company, according to data and analytics company <a href="https://www.boardroomalpha.com/spac/despacs">Boardroom Alpha</a>. Four more companies have gone public via SPAC so far this month, including robotics company Agility Robotics and spacecraft developer Quantum Space.&nbsp;</p><p>Public debuts via SPAC boomed in 2020 and 2021 before falling sharply in recent years after a string of poorly performing deals and other market headwinds. But companies and bankers are taking a renewed look at SPACs as an IPO alternative. In a SPAC deal, a cash-poor private tech firm is acquired by a cashed-up public shell of a company. (SPAC stands for special purpose acquisition company).</p><p>“SPACs are still alive,” said Steven Halperin, co-head of capital solutions and head of public equity markets at Moelis &amp; Co. “And as the IPO market gets more fickle and transaction sizes become larger and larger, will more companies think about going this route?”</p><p>One reason why the SPAC route may be more appealing right now is a proposed regulatory change that could make it easier for newly public companies to raise new equity after their debut. One drawback of SPACs is that they don’t guarantee a big infusion of cash, since investors in the SPAC can typically cash out their shares before the merger closes, which they often do if they don’t like the business getting acquired.</p><p>The Securities and Exchange Commission in May proposed easing restrictions on stock sales by newly public companies, including ones that go public via a SPAC. Among other changes, the proposal would eliminate a one-year waiting period for newly public companies to register for fast-tracked stock offerings. That could make it easier for those companies to take advantage of rallies in their share prices, including through ATMs and other deals already gaining traction with their public peers. </p><article
