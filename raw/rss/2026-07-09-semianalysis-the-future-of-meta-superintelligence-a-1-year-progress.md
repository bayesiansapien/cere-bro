# The Future of Meta Superintelligence: A 1 Year Progress Update

<p><span>It&#8217;s been a little over 1 year since the disastrous Llama 4 release spurred Zuck to rebuild his entire AI org. Highlights include the shocking $14.3B Scale AI &#8220;investment&#8221; just to poach Alexandr Wang and the best people from his Safety, Evaluations, and Alignment Labs (SEAL) team, the multi-hundred million dollar (sometimes $1B+) pay packages offered to top AI researchers/engineers, and the expedited compute ramp enabled by their new &#8220;Tent&#8221; datacenter design. For more details, see our </span><a href="https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/meta-superintelligence-leadership-compute-talent-and-data"><span>original post</span></a><span> on MSL.</span></p><p><span>Since then, frontier AI has increasingly felt like a two horse race between OpenAI vs Anthropic. Google had a brief moment in the spotlight with Gemini 3 Pro and Nano Banana, but they&#8217;ve since faded dramatically. Microsoft has completely blown their early lead with GitHub copilot and failed to effectively leverage their access to OpenAI IP. SpaceXAI is selling $26B a year worth of GPUs to Anthropic/Google, and the Chinese labs are simply too compute poor to truly reach the frontier.</span></p><p><span>Meanwhile, MSL made their public debut this April with the launch of Muse Spark. most important scaling law for improving AI capabilities today. The general idea is that instead of just predicting the next token, you teach the model how to complete entire tasks (e.g. fixing a bug in a codebase). Besides the </span><em><span>tasks</span></em><span> themselves, RL requires you to provide an </span><em><span>environment</span></em><span> for the model to complete the task in, </span><em><span>tools</span></em><span> the model can use to interact with the environment, and </span><em><span>verifiers</span></em><span> to check if the model&#8217;s answer is correct or not. For more background, see </span><a href="https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/rl-systems-mind-the-gap-matching"><span>our</span></a><span> </span><a href="https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/scaling-reinforcement-learning-environments-reward-hacking-agents-scaling-data"><span>previous</span></a><span> </span><a href="https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/rl-environments-and-rl-for-science"><span>posts</span></a><span>.</span></p><p><span>It is worth emphasizing that many AI insiders believe more RL environments/tasks are all we need to automate virtually all white-collar work. Here&#8217;s a quote from my roommate Sholto Douglas, a prominent Anthropic researcher, on the </span><a href="https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/sholto-trenton-2"><span>Dwarkesh podcast</span></a><span> last year:</span></p><blockquote><p><span>Even if algorithmic progress stalls out, and we just never figure out how to keep progress going&#8212;which I don&#8217;t think is the case, that hasn&#8217;t stalled out yet, it seems to be going great&#8212;</span><strong><span>the current suite of algorithms are sufficient to automate white collar work provided you have enough of the right kinds of data.</span></strong><span> Compared to the TAM of salaries for all of those kinds of work, it is so trivially worthwhile.</span></p></blockquote><p><span>Generally, all of these data companies create new RL tasks by hiring expert contractors from the relevant industries (hence why it&#8217;s called &#8220;human data&#8221;). However, there&#8217;s another thing every data company is desperately searching for today: real recordings of white-collar work.</span></p><h4><strong><span>Screen recordings are extremely valuable for making RL tasks</span></strong></h4><p><span>Naively, you might think screen recordings are primarily useful for older training paradigms like supervised fine-tuning (SFT) which teach the AI to mimic humans. After all, the whole point of RL is that you let the AI figure out the steps itself and assign rewards based solely on outcomes.</span></p><p><span>In reality, however, screen recordings can still be extremely useful for making RL tasks as long as you&#8217;re willing to do a little extra work.</span></p><p><span>The first benefit is realism. RL data is only good if it&#8217;s 1) representative of real economically valuable work and 2) at the right difficulty level for the AI. Too easy and there&#8217;s nothing for the AI to learn, too hard and it&#8217;ll never achieve any reward.</span></p><p><span>The only way to calibrate task difficulty is to just have the AI try solving the problem a bunch of times and then iterating accordingly. It&#8217;s actually quite hard to make an RL task that&#8217;s sufficiently difficult for frontier models today, so your expert contractors normally spend their time making tasks more challenging. Mechanize, for example, only </span><a href="https://www.mechanize.work/what-working-here-is-like/"><span>expects</span></a><span> the software engineers they pay $400k+ per year to make 1 good task per week.</span></p><p><span>Under these constraints, if you ask the average PE analyst to come up with a new financial modeling task from scratch because you want to RL your model to be better at Excel, you often end up with a contrived task that&#8217;s optimized for difficulty over realism. This is the main problem with benchmarks like OpenAI&#8217;s </span><a href="https://openai.com/index/gdpval/"><span>GDPval</span></a><span> and Mercor&#8217;s </span><a href="https://www.mercor.com/apex/"><span>Apex</span></a><span> suite (a good benchmark and a good RL env are roughly equivalent). If you read the tasks, you&#8217;ll see that most are unnaturally over-specified and don&#8217;t sound like something a human would actually ask an AI to do.</span></p><p><span>For example, here&#8217;s a GDPval task that asks the AI to make an itinerary. For example, a good coding RL task today would involve orchestrating subagents, and this simply didn&#8217;t exist just 7 months ago.</span></p><p><span>Screen recordings are also extremely useful for making verifiers. These days, most verifiers are </span><em><span>rubrics</span></em><span>. A sufficiently detailed recording gives you this info for free.</span></p><h4><strong><span>Meta just created a top tier RL environment startup</span></strong></h4><p><span>With this context, we can better appreciate the recent news about Meta starting to track their employees&#8217; screens, keyboards, and mouse movements. This is quite literally some of the most valuable data in the world today! Of course, it&#8217;s also poetically apt that the Scale AI man is the one spearheading the transformation.</span></p><p><span>Whereas all the data companies are desperately trying to partner with investment banks, law firms, and advertising agencies to record their workflows, Meta is one of the few companies in the world that has a sufficiently large workforce dedicated to each of these industries in-house.</span></p><p><span>The fact that Meta is still nimble and aggressive enough to do this despite the PR hit and initial employee backlash is already quite impressive. Yes, they&#8217;ve since walked it back </span><a href="https://www.theinformation.com/articles/meta-rolls-back-parts-employee-tracking-tool-staff-backlash"><span>slightly</span></a><span> by strengthening privacy protections and giving employees the option to pause the tracker for 30min, but we think these are very minor concessions.</span></p><p><span>Furthermore, they took their data efforts to </span><em><span>another level</span></em><span> in late May by announcing a new &#8220;applied AI engineering org&#8221; as part of their most recent round of layoffs/restructuring. </span><strong><span>~3000 engineers</span></strong><span>, which includes 70% of their new grads and a significant number of seniors, will now be </span><strong><span>making RL tasks/environments full-time</span></strong><span>.</span></p><p><span>We think this is an extremely underappreciated advantage for MSL. Anthropic has been the most aggressive lab by far when it comes to buying coding data from RL environment startups, and it&#8217;s one important reason why their models are so good at coding today.</span></p><p><span>Mercor recently </span><a href="https://x.com/EverettRandle/status/2074527860510085498"><span>disclosed</span></a><span> that they logged 2,517,000 expert hours on their platform in 2Q26, which is equivalent to ~4800 people working 40 hours a week.
