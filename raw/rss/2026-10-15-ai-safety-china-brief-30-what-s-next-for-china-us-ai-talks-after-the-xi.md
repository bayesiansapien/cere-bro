# Brief #30: What’s next for China–US AI talks after the Xi–Trump summit

<h3>Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li><p>Last month, Xi and Trump agreed to continued China&#8211;US AI dialogue, with the two sides meeting again in November, plus a bilateral channel for AI incidents.</p></li><li><p>Chinese state media suggest the coming round of talks in November should flesh out the incident channel, align risk definitions, and start with worst-case risks.</p></li><li><p>Two new unofficial AI Law drafts join a crowded field of expert proposals, but a national AI Law remains uncertain.</p></li><li><p>ByteDance upgraded its Responsible AI team to a new Alignment &amp; Preparedness Department focused on frontier risks.</p></li><li><p>Also: we are hiring! More details at the very end.</p></li></ul><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/subscribe?"><span>Subscribe now</span></a></p><h3>AI safety outcomes from Xi-Trump summit exceeded our expectation</h3><p><strong>What happened: </strong>One day after our <a href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/brief-29-what-chinas-new-ai-safety">last newsletter</a>, President Xi met President Trump in the US on September 24-25.</p><p>The meeting produced two concrete outcomes on AI safety, confirmed in both the <a href="https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/xw/zyxw/202609/t20260926_12031663.html">Chinese</a> and <a href="https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/09/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-advances-a-fair-and-reciprocal-relationship-with-china-while-hosting-historic-state-visit/">US</a> readouts:<a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-anchor-1" target="_self">1</a></p><ul><li><p><strong>A China&#8211;US AI Dialogue</strong> to &#8220;exchange views on risks and benefits related to AI,&#8221; with the next meeting scheduled in November.</p></li><li><p><strong>A bilateral communication channel for AI incidents.</strong></p></li></ul><blockquote><p>&#8220;President Xi pointed out that China and the United States have strong AI capabilities. They may have competition, but they may cooperate even more. The right thing to do is to draw on each other&#8217;s strengths, not guard against each other. The two sides can continue their dialogue on AI, exchange views on its risks and benefits, and jointly prevent the misuse and abuse of AI. AI must be kept under human control. A people-centered approach should be upheld, and AI should be developed for the positive and for good, so that AI will serve the progress of humanity.&#8221;</p><p><em>Official Chinese readout from the leaders&#8217; summit (<a href="https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/xw/zyxw/202609/t20260925_12031181.html">EN</a>, <a href="https://paper.people.com.cn/rmrb/pc/content/202609/25/content_30183032.html">CN</a>).</em></p></blockquote><p><strong>Why we are positively surprised: </strong>These outcomes exceeded our expectations, especially given the low baseline: the two governments had held no formal bilateral AI talks in two years. That the two sides committed to meeting again on a short timeline suggests the dialogue is meant to be ongoing rather than a one-off, which is a promising signal.</p><p><strong>What to watch next:</strong> According to <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/wpKCPMP1mVn9tZvQmfT6Bg">Yuyuan Tantian (&#29577;&#28170;&#35885;&#22825;)</a>, a WeChat account under China&#8217;s state broadcaster (CCTV), Chinese participants in the talks said that time was limited in Washington, so the two sides mainly laid out their respective positions and concerns, and plan to move forward on specific issues at the November dialogue. This naturally raises the question of what will be on the agenda next time.</p><p>While neither side has confirmed an agenda, the Yuyuan Tantian article argues for a narrow, practical focus. It is not an official Chinese government statement, but, being state-affiliated, it may reflect official expectations. The article suggests the November talks could:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Flesh out the incident channel: </strong>The channel &#8220;cannot remain a verbal agreement.&#8221; The two sides need to specify which incidents must be reported, how quickly, what information must be shared, and how failures to notify will be handled.</p></li><li><p><strong>Work on shared definitions and risk classification: </strong>The two sides need to settle basic questions like &#8220;What counts as a major risk? Which model behaviors constitute a serious loss of control?&#8221; The article highlights that this is non-trivial, because countries classify risks differently. For example, China&#8217;s <a href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/i/216085368/key-chinese-safety-framework-expands-to-agents-cyber-deception-embodied-ai-and-more">TC260 AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0</a> classifies risks into &#8220;inherent&#8221;, &#8220;application&#8221;, and &#8220;derivative&#8221; risks, a taxonomy that doesn&#8217;t map neatly onto Western AI safety frameworks.</p></li><li><p><strong>Start with the worst-case risks: </strong>These are the &#8220;low-hanging fruits&#8221; where Track 2 dialogues have already built expert consensus. One example is &#8220;preventing the most dangerous capabilities from being misused,&#8221; including by terrorists.</p></li></ul><p><strong>The wider discourse:</strong> Recent discussions in China&#8217;s expert community mirror this focus on <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/QsX1OG_io9kNSH2NigWmKw">fleshing out the incident mechanism</a>, <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/peWkw4Pm7UIafKqrB7sI0w">definitions</a>, and <a href="https://fddi.fudan.edu.cn/1b/20/c21253a793376/page.htm">major shared threats</a>. Some also suggest discussing <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/pKyrwn6G9TW6M0gCG9uTWA">risk reduction for military AI</a>; others go further, proposing a <a href="https://www.pekingnology.com/p/henry-huiyao-wang-ai-has-accelerated">reciprocal no-first-use commitment</a> on AI systems capable of autonomously initiating harm, modeled on China&#8217;s nuclear no-first-use policy. Chinese experts also <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/QsX1OG_io9kNSH2NigWmKw">stress</a> separating shared safety threats from structural competition. They <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/pKyrwn6G9TW6M0gCG9uTWA">warn</a> against treating China&#8217;s AI development itself as a risk, and <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/peWkw4Pm7UIafKqrB7sI0w">suggest</a> managing issues like export controls on a separate &#8220;list of differences&#8221; so they don&#8217;t block cooperation on common risks.<a class="footnote-anchor" href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-anchor-2" target="_self">2</a></p><h3>Two new unofficial expert drafts for a potential Chinese AI Law</h3><p><strong>What happened: </strong>Two new expert drafts of a potential national AI Law were published, one by a Zhejiang University team, the other by LIN Qianduo (&#26519;&#21315;&#22810;) from Wenzhou University of Technology. They add to an increasingly crowded space of similar unofficial proposals, even as the prospects for an actual national AI Law remain uncertain.</p><p><strong>The new drafts: </strong>The Zhejiang University draft is built around a distinctive idea of &#8220;fit-based&#8221; governance. It asks whether an AI system is fit for a given task by weighing three factors: the system&#8217;s execution capability (how autonomously it can act), its cognitive maturity (how reliable, robust and controllable it is), and the nature and impact of the task (what is at stake). The more autonomous the system and the higher the stakes, the more maturity it must demonstrate. Obligations, oversight and liability all scale in intensity accordingly.</p><p>Lin Qianduo&#8217;s draft aims to synthesize ideas from the various other model laws that have come before, as well as from the EU AI Act and the TC260 AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0. What stands out is a relatively strong focus on AI-enabled cybersecurity threats, including traceability requirements for cyberattacks carried out autonomously by AI (Art. 60) and controls on open-source models with offensive cyber capabilities (Art. 22).</p><p><strong>The necessary context: </strong>China already governs AI through <a href="https://www.cac.gov.cn/2023-07/13/c_1690898327029107.htm">several</a> <a href="https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/14/c_1743654684782215.htm">binding</a> <a href="https://www.cac.gov.cn/2026-04/10/c_1777558395078289.htm">regulations</a>, which require public-facing AI services to register with regulators and pass security assessments. Ministry-level agencies can issue these directly, making them a relatively quick and flexible governance tool.</p><p><strong>What China does not yet have:</strong> A comprehensive, horizontal AI Law, like the EU AI Act. That would have to pass through the National People&#8217;s Congress (NPC), a much slower and more complex process. The debate over whether China needs such a law gained new momentum in May, when the <a href="https://www.moj.gov.cn/pub/sfbgw/zwgkztzl/xxxcgcxjpfzsx/fzsxyw/202605/t20260511_534688.html">State Council&#8217;s 2026 legislative plan</a> called for &#8220;accelerating comprehensive legislation on the healthy development of AI.&#8221;</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a of proposals is becoming hard to keep track of.</p><p>But prospects for an actual national AI Law remain uncertain, and the legal community is sending mixed signals:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Some suggest work is already well underway.</strong> In his keynote at the Zhejiang University launch, senior jurist ZHANG Wenxian (&#24352;&#25991;&#26174;) <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/QjFHoy-Mz2QzH88oZeV_kQ">said</a> the relevant authorities have formally begun research and drafting work on an &#8220;AI Law,&#8221; and that drafting is &#8220;advancing on all fronts and accelerating.&#8221; He suggested the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC) is the appropriate department to lead the effort (implying that the NDRC is already leading this work). He added that several legal scholars have joined an &#8220;AI legislation expert team.&#8221; SHI Jianzhong (&#26102;&#24314;&#20013;) of China University of Political Science and Law similarly recently <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/eUTJcJ4o_gzYZvY-AZCAeg">said</a> that he sits on an &#8220;AI legislation expert group&#8221; and that the process is &#8220;moving very fast.&#8221; It is unclear whether the two are referring to the same group.</p></li><li><p><strong>Others are more skeptical. </strong>For example, SHOU Bu (&#23551;&#27493;) of Shanghai Jiao Tong University Law School <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/FW-JSNNkv-EXfsQzGKeXHg">has explicitly criticized the expert drafts</a> because he reads the official legislative plan as abandoning a standalone &#8220;AI Law&#8221; in favor of undefined &#8220;comprehensive AI legislation&#8221;, whose name, legal rank, and content all remain open.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Whatever the outcome, the debate shows how competitive policymaking in China can be. </strong>Multiple universities, think tanks, law firms and individual scholars are all vying to shape what a potential law would look like, and even whether there should be one at all. The range of proposals is clear evidence that China&#8217;s AI governance debate is far from monolithic.</p><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/brief-30-whats-next-for-chinaus-ai?utm_source=substack&amp;utm_medium=email&amp;utm_content=share&amp;action=share"><span>Share</span></a></p><h3>Also on our radar</h3><ul><li><p><strong>ByteDance has set up a <a href="https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/YauSE1KRBIkCu045SISQKw">new Alignment &amp; Preparedness department</a> and kickstarted a hiring round for safety talent.</strong> The company says it will &#8220;invest in this direction firmly and for the long term, and give the team resources and support on par with frontier model research.&#8221; The department will work on safety frameworks, safety evaluations for frontier LLMs and agents, interpretability, alignment, &#8220;preparedness for future model risks,&#8221; and coordinate with third-party testers. According to <a href="https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34241236">Chinese media</a>, the team will work on frontier risks like CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear) misuse, and will be headed by YAN Lin (&#20005;&#26519;), who previously led the Responsible AI team, and report directly to ByteDance&#8217;s head of LLM development, WU Yonghui (&#21556;&#27704;&#36745;).   </p></li><li><p><strong>A DeepSeek <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22978">technical report</a> describes how it tackles reward hacking in its agentic reinforcement learning sandbox platform.</strong> The researchers observed agents obtaining test answers through unintended channels, such as forging internal system requests, searching platform logs for leaked answers, and probing the network for mirrors and existing code. DeepSeek says it has therefore decided to &#8220;strengthen observability to identify emerging problems and continuously harden&#8221; the platform. The report acknowledges that no single mechanism can prevent all agent misbehavior and system failures.</p></li><li><p><strong>The National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC) <a href="https://www.nsfc.gov.cn/p1/3381/2824/142423.html">announced an application guide</a> for special funding on technical AI safety. </strong>The NSFC is China&#8217;s main funder of basic research. A dedicated call on AI safety indicates the topic is becoming a funding priority in its own right, though the funding amount and specific research directions have not been disclosed.</p></li><li><p><strong>The UN Security Council <a href="https://webtv.un.org/en/asset/k1c/k1cdceg9sg">discussed AI risks</a>. </strong>China&#8217;s UN Ambassador <a href="https://un.china-mission.gov.cn/eng/hyyfy/202609/t20260924_12030908.htm">highlighted</a> the need for continuous improvement of regulatory frameworks, emergency response, and cross-border cooperation to address AI risks.</p></li><li><p><strong>ZENG Yi (&#26366;&#27589;), Professor at Renmin University, <a href="https://www.studytimes.cn/kjqy/202609/t20260930_92476.html">argues</a> in the Central Party School&#8217;s </strong><em><strong>Study Times</strong></em><strong> that superintelligence should not be developed until it is scientifically confirmed to be safe and controllable. </strong>Recursive self-improvement should require human supervision, and red lines should be coordinated through an international institution with enforcement power.</p></li><li><p><strong>The Standardization Administration of China gave TC260 (China&#8217;s main standardization body for AI) the formal green light to start drafting <a href="https://www.tc260.org.cn/tc260/tzgg/202610/2ac025d624a046199d8895fd6eeee5aa.shtml">25 new national standards.</a></strong> They cover security testing methods for foundation models, capability requirements for AI safety testing and evaluation institutions, security guidelines for open-sourcing AI models, security specifications for large models used in cybersecurity, and more.
