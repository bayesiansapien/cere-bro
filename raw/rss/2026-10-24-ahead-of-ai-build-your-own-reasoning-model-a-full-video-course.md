# Build Your Own Reasoning Model: A Full Video Course

<p>I have written a lot about reasoning models here over the last two years on Ahead of AI, including a recent article on mechanisms to <a href="https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/controlling-reasoning-effort-in-llms">control reasoning effort levels in contemporary LLMs</a>. Reasoning models are still as relevant as ever. In fact, almost all current-generation LLMs today are reasoning models or offer a reasoning mode.</p><p>For those who are interested in how reasoning models work, including inference scaling, reinforcement learning, and distillation, I wrote my recent <a href="https://mng.bz/Nwr7">Build Reasoning Model (From Scratch),</a> which implements these from the ground up in PyTorch, which, in my experience, is still the best way to understand and learn how things really work.</p><p>Now, in the last few months, I also sat down and recorded accompanying lectures I wanted to share with you. So, below, you will find eight reasoning model-from-scratch coding videos (11 hours of content).</p><p>We start with a pre-trained LLM and implement methods to evaluate and improve it in Python and PyTorch. So, if you enjoyed my <a href="https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/coding-llms-from-the-ground-up">Coding LLMs from the Ground Up</a> course last year, this is essentially the sequel (but you can also watch it without the prior course as a prerequisite).</p><p>I&#8217;m currently taking a short break and will be back soon with more exciting LLM research roundups next month. In the meantime, I hope you enjoy coding along!</p><h2>How LLMs, reasoning models, and agents relate and fit together</h2><p>As a small side note, and since I often get asked how reasoning models relate to today&#8217;s AI agents, here&#8217;s a short section on this before getting started with the videos.</p><p>A reasoning model is essentially an upgrade from a conventional LLM, that is, adding more post-training via reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (and optionally inference scaling techniques) to make it more capable of solving more challenging problems. Classically, reasoning models are developed for math and code, but the capabilities can generalize to other problem domains as well.</p><p>A reasoning model then functions as the engine inside an agent harness, where the agent harness is a software or tooling framework around the LLM/reasoning model to tackle even more challenging problems (bigger coding tasks, travel booking, and all other sorts of personal assistant tasks). Motivation and code setup</h3><p>The first video starts with an explanation of the relationship between conventional LLMs, reasoning models, and agents. Next, the main topic of the video is setting up the coding environment. This includes installing the Python and PyTorch dependencies (with uv), among others. I also discuss hardware choices and common setup issues.</p><p><strong>Topics covered</strong></p><ul><li><p>The relationship between conventional LLMs, reasoning models, and agent harnesses</p></li><li><p>On the benefits of learning through code implementations</p></li><li><p>The scope of this video series</p></li><li><p>Installing Python dependencies</p></li><li><p>Choosing hardware and checking CUDA or Apple Silicon support</p></li><li><p>Using a remote GPU with VS Code and troubleshooting the environment</p></li></ul><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-Kh9mqTzjuEQ"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kh9mqTzjuEQ">Watch video 1</a> &#183; <a href="https://github.com/rasbt/reasoning-from-scratch/tree/main/ch02/02_setup-tips">Setup instructions</a></p><h3>2. Loading a base model, text generation, and KV caching</h3><p>Before adding reasoning methods in the upcoming videos, we need a working base model. Instead of training it from scratch, since that would be a whole other book (see <a href="https://amzn.to/4fqvn0D">Build A Large Language Model (From Scratch)</a>), we start with a pre-trained model.</p><p>Here, we load the pre-trained weights of a Qwen3 0.6B model. Building a verifier for evaluation and reinforcement learning</h3><p>In this lecture, we build a math-answer verifier step by step, from answer extraction to normalization and checking mathematical equivalence. We then use it to evaluate the model on MATH-500.</p><p>We use this verifier to evaluate the base model&#8217;s performance on the MATH-500 evaluation dataset.</p><p>However, the verifier also has a second job later in the series: providing the reward signal for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR).</p><p><strong>Topics covered</strong></p><ul><li><p>Intro to verifiers and how they fit into LLM evaluation methods (other methods are covered in the book&#8217;s appendix)</p></li><li><p>Reusing answer verification as a reward signal for RLVR (more on that in chapter 6)</p></li><li><p>The verifier flow, including extracting final answers, normalizing answers and checking mathematical equivalence</p></li><li><p>Building an answer grader and an evaluation loop for MATH-500</p></li><li><p>Comparing base and reasoning models on MATH-500</p></li></ul><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-JQJ_8_jSAoY"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JQJ_8_jSAoY">Watch video 3</a> &#183; <a href="https://github.com/rasbt/reasoning-from-scratch/tree/main/ch03">Chapter 3 code</a></p><h3>4. Inference-time scaling: sampling and self-consistency</h3><p>Now we explore ways to improve answers while keeping the model weights fixed. But before implementing specific inference-scaling techniques, we first implement methods to generate diverse answers from the same prompt (this is standard practice in proprietary LLM chatbots, such as ChatGPT).</p><p>Concretely, we implement temperature scaling, multinomial sampling, and top-p filtering to generate diverse responses. Then we combine multiple responses through self-consistency, using majority voting over their final answers.</p><p>We also examine the tradeoff between answer accuracy and the extra computation needed to generate more candidates.</p><p><strong>Topics covered</strong></p><ul><li><p>Comparing how additional compute benefits model performance during training and during inference</p></li><li><p>A gentle intro to inference-scaling through chain-of-thought prompts</p></li><li><p>Implementing temperature scaling and multinomial sampling to generate diverse answers</p></li><li><p>Adding top-p filtering to control the quality of the diverse answers</p></li><li><p>Combining candidate answers through self-consistency, one of the most popular inference scaling techniques</p></li><li><p>Evaluating MATH-500 accuracy against the cost of additional inference compute</p></li></ul><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-t5y-kS9nNxU"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t5y-kS9nNxU">Watch video 4</a> &#183; <a href="https://github.com/rasbt/reasoning-from-scratch/tree/main/ch04">Chapter 4 code</a></p><h3>5. Inference-time scaling: scoring and self-refinement</h3><p>Building on the previous video, we implement scoring functions to compare multiple answers generated from the same prompt. We can also use these scoring methods to break ties in self-consistency (covered in the previous lecture).</p><p>The scoring methods covered here include computing token and sequence log probabilities (this will also be relevant in the later reinforcement learning chapters). We then implement a self-refinement loop in which the model critiques an answer and attempts a revision.</p><p>The evaluation lets us check how useful those additional steps are for our model.</p><p><strong>Topics covered</strong></p><ul><li><p>Using scoring functions for comparing multiple answers and breaking ties</p></li><li><p>Implementing a simple heuristic scorer</p></li><li><p>Computing token probabilities and sequence likelihoods (also relevant for the following lectures)</p></li><li><p>Using log probabilities for improved numerical stability</p></li><li><p>Building a self-refinement loop for generating critiques and revised answers</p></li><li><p>Evaluating self-refinement on MATH-500</p></li></ul><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-TVMyOJ_3Gxo"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TVMyOJ_3Gxo">Watch video 5</a> &#183; <a href="https://github.com/rasbt/reasoning-from-scratch/tree/main/ch05">Chapter 5 code</a></p><h3>6. Reinforcement learning: implementing GRPO for RLVR</h3><p>In this lecture, we move on to implementing a training technique called Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Reinforcement learning: tracking, clipped policy ratios, and KL terms</h3><p>Now that we implemented the training loop in the previous lecture, we can revisit it and analyze the training runs in more detail.</p><p>Concretely, this video adds more training metrics for inspection and analysis, including entropy and advantage statistics, clipped policy ratios, KL loss term values, and formatted rewards.</p><p>We then review the training logs and evaluation results to understand what these additions do and whether additions such as clipped policy ratios and KL loss terms are helpful.</p><p><strong>Topics covered</strong></p><ul><li><p>Running longer training experiments and interpreting their logs</p></li><li><p>Tracking advantages and entropy to understand training behavior</p></li><li><p>Implementing clipping in the policy loss and analyzing its effects</p></li><li><p>Adding a KL loss term and discussing reward hacking</p></li><li><p>Understanding limitations of the simplified KL formulation</p></li><li><p>Implementing format rewards and conditioning them on answer correctness</p></li><li><p>Comparing checkpoints on MATH-500 and exploring further GRPO research</p></li></ul><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-VxfUrUrrczs"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VxfUrUrrczs">Watch video 7</a> &#183; <a href="https://github.com/rasbt/reasoning-from-scratch/tree/main/ch07">Chapter 7 code</a></p><h3>8. Distilling a reasoning model</h3><p>Finally, we explore another way to train a small reasoning model, that is, learning from answers generated by a stronger (and bigger) teacher model.
