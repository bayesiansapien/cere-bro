# How software engineering is changing: a tale of two eras

Regular scheduling returns next week!</em></p><div><hr /></div><p>In September, when The Pragmatic Engineer <a href="https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pragmatic-engineer-five-years">turned five</a>, I <a href="https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/how-software-engineering-is-changing-an-essay-challenge/">announced</a> an essay challenge, inviting readers to write about how things have changed in tech workplaces and in our projects, for the chance to win a grand prize of $10,000 awarded to our favorite article.</p><p>We received 125 submissions, covering a wide range of viewpoints and experiences. <em>Many thanks to everyone who took part.</em> In today&#8217;s issue, we publish the winning piece in full.</p><p>Congratulations to software engineer <a href="https://neilkakkar.com/">Neil Kakkar</a>! We appreciate the sheer volume of data that Neil has collected about how his own output has changed over time. Neil is a dev based in London, with nine years of professional experience; he has interned at Google, and been a software engineer at Bloomberg, Posthog, and Tano. His previous workplace, PostHog, is an open source project, which must&#8217;ve been useful for gathering historic data.</p><p>In a follow-up issue, we&#8217;ll highlight runner-up essays, and link to the ones that impressed us for the effort on show and interesting thoughts.</p><p><em>With that, it&#8217;s over to Neil. He covers:</em></p><ol><li><p><strong>Two eras of shipping</strong>. Until last year, he was merging between eight and 20 pull requests (PRs) per month. But since joining an AI-native startup, Neil now ships 200+ PRs per month, approximately a tenfold increase.</p></li><li><p><strong>Old constraints that disappeared. </strong>No more communications overhead or code reviews. The tradeoff: moving faster and breaking things.</p></li><li><p><strong>New constraints that appeared. </strong>Too much context switching (thrashing), losing shared context, pacing yourself is harder, less focus on design, and worries about AI plan limits.</p></li><li><p><strong>How AI is changing software engineering. </strong>Everyone needs to be onboard with AI to get the benefits at work, encouraging experimentation makes a difference, and many existing technologies and processes crack under the change.</p></li></ol><div><hr /></div><p>I&#8217;ve been working at an AI native startup for the past eight months. Before joining <a href="https://www.tano.ai/pragmatic">Tano</a>, I was at <a href="https://posthog.com/">PostHog</a> for four years, a fast moving pre-AI startup, and started my career at <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/uk">Bloomberg</a>, a big fintech private company, where I spent three years. All three workplaces are different: Bloomberg is huge, with 20,000 people and very defined processes. PostHog is smaller, at around 10 people when I joined, and now at 200. At Tano, I was the first engineer and we&#8217;re now around 10 people in total.</p><p>In each job, I&#8217;ve seen what a difference speed and culture make, and more recently, how culture needs to change to keep up with AI.</p><p>To back things up, I use in-depth stats about my work at PostHog and Tano. I also have some very rough numbers from Bloomberg. By far, I&#8217;ve written more code and made a lot more PRs at Tano than I did at PostHog, and a major part of this is down to AI. Facilitating the shift is engineering culture.</p><p><em>One disclaimer before we get into it: the PostHog stats are from pre-AI. I&#8217;ve been following their journey, and PostHog is as &#8220;AI-pilled&#8221; as it gets now, and output is increasing significantly. In this article, I treat it as a &#8216;pre-AI baseline&#8217; for excellent engineering work. Tano is a fast-moving AI-native startup, founded after AI agents emerged.</em></p><h1><strong>1. Two eras of shipping</strong></h1><p>Let&#8217;s start with some raw numbers. I&#8217;m shipping 11x more PRs at Tano than at PostHog, where I shipped 2.4x as many as I did at Bloomberg.</p><p>At Bloomberg, I was just starting my career, so I wasn&#8217;t the fastest, and moving to PostHog opened my eyes to what a fast-moving startup culture really looks like: we were encouraged to take responsibility, ship things quickly, listen to customers, and solve their issues ASAP.</p><p>At Bloomberg and PostHog there was a high bar for code: everything had to be peer reviewed, and as I became senior, I spent a big chunk of my time reviewing code.</p><div It makes sense for them, but not so much at our scale &#8211; just yet.</p><h1><strong>2. Old constraints that disappeared</strong></h1><p>Eleven times more PRs per month at Tano wouldn&#8217;t be possible without AI; I couldn&#8217;t write 100k+ lines of code a month by hand, let alone think through that many things. Furthermore, distractions during a normal work day &#8211; like new bugs, small feature requests, emergency incidents &#8211; break flow and decrease productivity. With AI, these can be passed onto a parallel work thread and not take up focus, front and centre.<br /><br /><strong>How much does the 11x multiplier stack because of everything else, apart from AI? </strong>Imagine visiting a pre-AI PostHog where reviews are mandatory as a time traveller from 2026, and you&#8217;re the only engineer there with secret access to AI tech from the future. At best, I think you&#8217;ll hit a 1-2x productivity gain because the bottleneck shifts from writing to PR approvals &#8211; but <em>only for you</em>. Others still aren&#8217;t free to review your completed PRs, and your PR output being much faster makes the review queue longer.</p><p>At Tano where we&#8217;re shipping 10x more, bottlenecks like this and others appear, like communicating with colleagues about what you&#8217;re doing, reviewing code, and figuring out what to build next. Let&#8217;s talk about some of these.</p><h2><strong>Communication overhead: How engineering works together</strong></h2><p>Engineers at Tano work as independently as possible. We come together and talk through big architecture decisions that affect the whole product, but for most things, there&#8217;s enough trust at the company that I can go do what I think is best. They also trust that if I&#8217;m not sure about what to do, or if there&#8217;s risks involved, that I&#8217;ll flag the risks and account for trade-offs.</p><p><strong>A big part of moving fast is having just one person responsible for things.</strong> Of course, this assumes that they can figure out how to do it themselves, and go ship it.</p><p>At PostHog, when building out surveys, two engineers and one designer worked together to ship V1 in a month. All three of us iterated on the design, backend, and frontend. When I discovered something that needed to change on the backend, it often meant the other engineer needed to change something on the frontend as well. This was the communication overhead. Even so, engineers who worked together went faster than I would, figuring out the backend, <em>then</em> building out the frontend, <em>and then</em> iterating over any new constraints.</p><p><strong>Now that writing code is faster, a communication overhead makes little sense. </strong>If I imagine that we&#8217;d be building the same survey functionality today, it&#8217;s now a lot more efficient if one person owns the entire stack end-to-end; design, frontend, backend. With Claude Code, this is doable by one engineer.</p><p>There&#8217;s still value in talking about unknown unknowns and things I&#8217;m not sure about with other engineers, but where the output is mostly known and it&#8217;s a straightforward feature or product, then I think one person working solo is more effective.</p><h2><strong>Code reviews</strong></h2><p><strong>Code reviews are optional at Tano. </strong>That is, they are optional once you have a handle on the codebase. The time to confidence is variable, depending on how quickly you can get onboarded and how quickly human PR review comments go down to zero. I felt comfortable enough three weeks after I started, but depending on experience it varies, going up to eight weeks.</p><p>I built a code review agent via Claude Code Github Actions that runs on every PR, which is now standard across the codebase for everyone and runs in continuous integration (CI). There&#8217;s also a deeper, stronger agent we can run on demand if there&#8217;s a risky change.</p><p>In most cases, this automated reviewer is the only review on PRs. who helped us prioritise what to work on. Having a clear-cut distinction between engineers and product managers is great when working at huge companies, but these roles merge as workplaces get smaller. At Bloomberg, every team of engineers had a dedicated product manager. At PostHog, there was one product manager for several teams. At Tano, there are none.</p><p>The product managers at PostHog helped with lots of research and setting of the product direction, but engineers were also expected to dive in, talk to customers, do our own research, and form our own opinions on product direction as and when needed. I thrived in that environment.</p><p><strong>In my current role, I am the product manager, too. </strong>We have a roadmapping session every Monday where everyone comes up with issues they&#8217;ve faced, things we need to fix, things that will become a problem soon, such as the number of support tickets per customer increasing &#8211; which means that as we get more customers, humans could not keep up with the number of tickets to solve.</p><p>The CEO and CTO provide the most context on things to be done, and usually have a better idea of prioritisation. They give engineers context on things like customer churn risk, features needed for a new sale to close, etc. Together, we decide what makes sense to tackle, then divide the work among engineers. Usually, every engineer picks up a separate thing to work on.</p><p><strong>What used to take a month now often takes a week.</strong> This is because agents build things much faster, and I&#8217;m typically working on multiple projects at the same time, and no longer have to wait on a product manager to feed me items to build. Hence, we just share context and priorities, and go build things to achieve our weekly goals.</p><h2><strong>Move fast and break things</strong></h2><p>Over my career, I&#8217;ve been part of five big migrations; one at Bloomberg, one at PostHog, and I&#8217;ve led three at Tano.</p><p><strong>At PostHog, the big migration took a year. </strong>It was about <a href="https://posthog.com/blog/persons-on-events">person-on-events</a>. We have two huge tables at PostHog: the events table, with 20 billion+ events of what some anonymous id did; the persons table, which stores persons and their properties. When building funnels or unique user queries, we needed to join these two tables, then aggregate by the person. These queries were very slow, and downright unusable for larger customers. The person-on-events migration moved the person identifier and properties onto events, alongside the anonymous id. It was a massive company-wide effort to update every query to not use joins.</p><p>This person-on-events migration was very painful, and all of the code updates were written by hand. We took the time to make sure we future-proofed it because doing another similar migration on events was unfeasible.</p><p><strong>At Tano, we finished two complex migrations in just a few weeks. </strong>Tano is an influencer marketing agency that builds AI agents to talk to creators, negotiate with them, go through content review with them, then pass it on to brands for final approval so that content can go live. The first iteration of this agent was a free-form checklist of what we wanted the agent to do, and lots of tools for it to accomplish those things. We didn&#8217;t have good observability in this system, and the agent would get pretty confused from time to time, using the wrong tool. The solution was to move to a workflow where each task is well defined with its own limited set of tools.</p><p>The migration at Tano moved every creator over to the new workflow, mapping existing checklist items into workflow tasks, and building new agent tools for each workflow. As we scaled this system over time, we realised the agent often lacked context on multiple threads with the same creator. The next migration addressed these deficiencies. Both these migrations took weeks, instead of a year.</p><p><strong>How fast we want to move is a conscious decision now</strong>. In the second migration, I missed some guardrails, which led to creators getting paid twice for the same work. Duplicate payments are not great, but moving fast and breaking things was a trade-off we were very willing to make because getting the new architecture in place meant we could scale faster. At PostHog, we didn&#8217;t have the option to migrate quickly.</p><p>Our product architecture went from a mostly irreversible decision to a reversible one, which changes the calculus of what kind of experiments you can run, what kind of products you can build, and how specialised they can be for specific clients. We don&#8217;t need to get every detail right at the beginning, and can optimize for more information in production, then quickly iterate based on that information.</p><h1><strong>3. New constraints that appeared</strong></h1><p>Of course, not everything is ideal with an AI-native work culture. I&#8217;ve gone into what&#8217;s possible with it, and now I&#8217;m going to focus on its costs. The bottlenecks aren&#8217;t gone, they just move on to me. Fast-paced work comes at the real cost of exhausting myself quickly, keeping a lot more things in my head, how easily new things distract me, and how little I know about what everyone else is working on.</p><h2><strong>Building in parallel</strong></h2><p>I&#8217;m almost always working on multiple projects at once. Working on more than one at a time involves a fair amount of context switching, and I find it can get hard to keep up with all the PRs generated by my agents. I limit the number of things I work on at the same time, but it still feels a lot more mentally draining and exhausting than it was before AI agents.</p><div A new issue comes in, and immediately I fire a Claude session up to fix it because I know that it won&#8217;t take more than an hour end to end, with no real active input from me.</p><p><strong>I used to be much more careful about choosing to work on something new. </strong>For example, at PostHog, I&#8217;d make a measured decision based on the criticality of an issue, and how it competed on priority with my sprint goals. And at Bloomberg, I wouldn&#8217;t hear about new work to do at all until a sprint planning session.</p><p>In computer science, thrashing occurs when a system spends most of its time switching between tasks, rather than doing them. The fix is to limit concurrency and get the highest priority tasks done first.</p><p>Thanks to working on a lot more things, I get pulled in so many different directions that I&#8217;m reacting to everything and fixing new bugs that come up day-to-day, instead of what I should be doing. Like a computer, I&#8217;m spending more time switching between tasks and getting bugs fixed, than focusing on my own sprint goals.</p><p>Sometimes, when bugs are critical, changing priorities is valid and useful. But when I do it just because each individual task doesn&#8217;t take long, then it&#8217;s not great at moving the company forward.</p><p>It&#8217;s no wonder that the thrashing problem makes context-maxxing worse. I end up more mentally exhausted because I picked up more work in fixing quick bugs, when I should&#8217;ve been working on sprint goals.</p><h2><strong>Losing shared context</strong></h2><p>Since there are no more code reviews, I&#8217;ve stopped reviewing most code as well. Of course, we review our own code and new team members&#8217;, but seldom for experienced colleagues.</p><p>Code reviews used to be one of my favourite things at PostHog. I had a feed of every PR coming in and I read them all. Reading helped me keep context of what everyone was working on, and I could jump in to help where needed, i.e., by suggesting things that already might exist in another part of the codebase. As PostHog grew from 20 to ~100 people, the number of new PRs per working day also grew from 15 to 30. It was impossible to keep reading every PR.</p><p>At Tano, we are merging about 30 PRs a day as well, and I&#8217;m not a big outlier. Another engineer at Tano has very similar output, and newer engineers + CTO are at roughly a third of that because of being new or having other responsibilities.</p><p><strong>Having no time to read all PRs means missing context across the business.</strong> Doing code reviews was one way I used to keep up with what other engineers were doing, and how the product was growing. Also, isolated contexts affect team culture, as we end up working in silos and this discourages collaboration. On the upside, one person owns big patches of the product and can move fast. On the downside, it also means only one person has that context.</p><p>In software engineering, the &#8220;bus factor&#8221; is the number of people who need to disappear from a project in order for it to stall. If only one person has the context for the project, the bus factor is one. Having a low bus factor is an open problem for every team I&#8217;ve worked on. With AI, I do alone what my whole team used to do, which makes the bus factor problem more acute. I think AI provides the solution: if I&#8217;m good at writing down context in <a href="http://agents.md">AGENTS.md</a> files in my product areas, anyone can hit the ground running, learn with AI, and I don&#8217;t know the answer yet, but it&#8217;s something I would love to experiment further on.</p><p>Overall, it seems like the world is changing very quickly, and I&#8217;m super excited to be in the middle of it all.</p><h1><strong>4. How AI is changing software engineering</strong></h1><p>So, how much of the change is due to AI, and how much is from everything else around it?</p><p><strong>AI is a speed multiplier.</strong> Speed is a defining feature of start ups, and how they outcompete incumbents by experimenting and adapting to market demand quickly, and catching up to incumbents in terms of features. PostHog was not the first product analytics platform &#8211; in fact, it was ten years late to the party. But people at PostHog executed quickly, caught up with the competition, and the product has now expanded into several different categories, like experimentation, data warehouse, error tracking, and session recordings.</p><p>We built these products before AI. Today, a startup with AI can out-execute another without AI, and since speed is a defining feature, the startup without AI will have a very difficult time.</p><p><strong>Your productivity gains are limited if the rest of the company is not onboard with AI. </strong>It&#8217;s clear that AI is necessary, but it&#8217;s not sufficient. For example, if you were a time-traveller visiting PostHog, pre-AI, you&#8217;d still not be all that productive with all of today&#8217;s AI tools. This is because with mandatory code reviews from fellow engineers, the review queue keeps increasing endlessly since we produce faster than we can review.</p><p><strong>Encouraging experimentation in ways of working can pay off.</strong> I&#8217;ve seen teams with this approach end up better off. A team like Tano encourages experimentation by not frowning upon me for leaving my laptop at work and letting agents run overnight.</p><p>Further, a company that is flexible, recognises new constraints when they show up, and works towards removing them so it can move even faster, seems to do better than ones that adopt one way of working and stick to it. For example, Anthropic also automated code reviews away, but then ended up <a href="https://claude.com/resources/articles/agentic-coding-is-straining-ci-heres-how-we-scaled-test-impact-analysis-at-anthropic">constrained by continuous integration (CI) pipelines</a>. They unblocked CI as the new constraint and then found out their source control couldn&#8217;t keep up. In order to keep moving faster, they <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/redesigning-source-control">redesigned source control</a>.</p><p><strong>As you adopt AI to move faster, a lot of working technologies and team processes will crack.</strong> At the very least, they will strain under the load of getting more work done. A lot of startups will probably not end up at the scale where they have to redesign source control, but the general principle stands: you can restrict AI for what I believe suboptimal results, or you can lean into it, find out where new constraints end up &#8211; like in code reviews &#8211; and relax those constraints by automating those reviews so you can continue moving faster.</p><p>I value a team culture where we experiment, are open to being proven wrong, humble, curious, and try to make the most of the tools we have. At Tano, this culture is a defining factor in our successful AI adoption, and I believe culture is a necessary &#8211; and sufficient &#8211; condition of success at every AI-era startup.</p><h1><strong>Takeaways</strong></h1><p><em>Gergely, again.</em></p><p>Thanks to Neil for this data-driven essay on how he sees things changing in the industry, and how quickly it&#8217;s happening.</p><p>Neil&#8217;s story about how his PR output increased tenfold from the beginning of the year is an interesting one.
