# We Visited China’s Stargate in Inner Mongolia

<p>Stargate Abilene has become the symbol of America&#8217;s AI buildout. It is a gigawatt-scale campus on the Texas plains built for OpenAI. China has campuses on a similar scale, though few people outside the country have had a look inside. In September, we visited one, and we found the speed, scale, ultra low cost, and deep vertical integration astonishing.</p><p>During the trip, we toured Inner Mongolia, China&#8217;s fastest-growing AI datacenter region. It's the &#8220;West Texas&#8221; of China, but while the Lone Star State faces its highest ever anti-datacenter sentiment, Inner Mongolia is accelerating and faces no such backlash.</p><p>Our main stop was Galaxy, a campus in Ulanqab planned for up to 800MW of IT capacity. </p><p>Galaxy is about the size of Abilene&#8217;s core campus. Its first 120MW phase was delivered within a year at a fifth of US costs. Galaxy drops the central chiller and keeps much of that flexibility, a design that has been running at scale in northern China for years.</p><p>In this report, we use what we saw on the site visit to show how China builds hyperscale datacenters today, with a deep dive on each of the topics above. Crusoe began building Abilene in mid-2024 and leased it to Oracle, which runs it for OpenAI. Crusoe describes the eight-building campus as 1.2GW of power, which comes to about 820MW of critical IT capacity, on par with the 800MW planned at Galaxy. That makes Galaxy one of the largest single campuses in China. Our <a href="https://semianalysis.com/datacenter-industry-model/">SemiAnalysis Datacenter Model</a> also tracks an expansion phase that Crusoe plans to add, powered behind the meter, which takes the whole Abilene site to 1.5GW of IT capacity. Galaxy also has room to grow. If the wind turbine factory next to the campus is converted, a later expansion phase could take the campus to ~1GW.</p><p>In addition to scale, the two share a starting point. Both are developed by companies with roots in energy, and both were planned around their own power. Abilene draws on the grid and on gas turbines on site, while Galaxy relies largely on wind. </p><p>What sets Galaxy apart from Abilene, and what draws our attention, is its vertical integration. </p><h2><strong>Own wind for its own load </strong></h2><p>Galaxy&#8217;s power comes through a model China calls source-grid-load-storage (&#8221;SGLS&#8221;). Under this model, a project builds its own wind and/or solar farm, battery storage and datacenter, and links them through a dedicated line instead of the public grid. </p><p>For hyperscalers, the appeal of an SGLS project is long-term power at a largely fixed cost. Power from wind and solar also counts toward green targets.</p><p>Inner Mongolia holds about 57% of China&#8217;s usable wind resources. A wind farm here runs on average about 2,800 full-load hours a year, a capacity factor of roughly 32%. Ulanqab is windier, with a capacity factor of 35&#8211;40%. That sets the starting point for how much of an SGLS project&#8217;s power can come from wind.</p><p>For Galaxy, Envision aims to supply over 60% of the first two phases&#8217; power use with its own wind farms. Three factors make this possible:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Siting.</strong> Envision chose windy sites, even though they sit about two hours&#8217; drive from the campus. The two wind farms are about 80km apart, which cuts the hours in a year when neither has wind.</p></li></ol><ol start="2"><li><p><strong>Scale.</strong> The wind farms have a combined 420MW of installed capacity, about 1.75 times the 240MW datacenter load. With this oversizing, even light-wind hours can still cover much of the load.</p></li></ol><ol start="3"><li><p><strong>Storage.</strong> A 4-hour battery storage system shifts power from windy hours into calm ones. Most SGLS projects add storage too, but Envision makes its own storage systems and runs them on its own software, which we detail in the next section.</p></li></ol><p>The chart below shows how each Envision designs its own turbines and develops the converters and control software. The battery energy storage systems (BESS) at both the datacenter campus and the wind farm come from its affiliate AESC, which builds every major part in-house, from cells to power conversion and software, so the whole system can be tuned and tested together at the factory before shipping. Most vendors cover only one part of the system (high voltage, low voltage or generators), so getting the parts to work together falls to on-site commissioning, which can take 3&#8211;6 months. Envision&#8217;s factory pre-configuration has become its core selling point. </p><p>The second role goes beyond Envision&#8217;s traditional business into the datacenter space. For Galaxy, Envision designed and integrated the low-voltage power modules and the 800V SST system. </p><p>800V DC is the architecture that Nvidia is pushing for its next generation of high-density racks, as Kyber racks at around 600kW cannot be served efficiently at today&#8217;s voltages. In the US, the industry is still early on this path. SSTs that convert medium-voltage AC straight into 800V DC are the final step. Galaxy&#8217;s SST pilot is only 5MW, but it already runs 800V DC all the way to the rack at a live hyperscaler site. We discuss this in detail shortly. </p><p>Lastly, Envision co-designed the prefabricated substation sitting inside the campus. The substation is built and tested as modules in the factory while civil work goes on at the site, then shipped and connected on site. This takes less than 3 months, versus about 6 months for a conventional indoor substation.</p><p>Today, vertical integration on its own does not lead to hyperscaler orders, but two trends suggest it could matter more over time.</p><p>First, access to power is becoming central to datacenter development. In the US, developers bridge the power gap with on-site gas turbines, fuel cells and batteries. In Southeast Asia, governments have been pushing developers to bring their own power this year. Malaysia now requires datacenters to source at least 30% of their power from renewables and encourages on-site generation. In China, approvals increasingly favor projects that pair compute with green power, under the national push for &#8220;Compute Power Coordination.&#8221; Across different markets, access to energy is becoming part of what a datacenter developer sells.</p><p>Second, the power architecture from the grid connection to the rack is being redesigned. In the US, the industry is moving toward higher-voltage DC power, while battery backup units (BBUs) and BESS are starting to take over the role of UPS. The technology roadmap is not settled yet, but the shift looks set to continue, giving integrated players room to compete differently from traditional developers. </p><p>These trends have drawn other players onto a similar path. The closest case is the tie-up between VNET, CATL and Zhongheng Electric. </p><p>VNET is a leading colocation operator, CATL is the world&#8217;s largest EV battery maker, and Zhongheng Electric is China&#8217;s largest supplier of HVDC power systems for datacenters, working with both Alibaba and ByteDance. </p><p>In 2026, CATL became VNET&#8217;s largest shareholder and announced a deal to take a 49% stake in Zhongheng Electric&#8217;s controlling shareholder. It has since agreed with VNET to jointly develop gigawatt-scale datacenter campuses, and separately with Zhongheng to work on datacenter power systems. Between them, VNET covers the datacenter side, CATL leads green power and storage, and Zhongheng brings power conversion inside the hall. </p><p>CATL is also one of DeepSeek&#8217;s largest outside investors, which extends its reach downstream to the model layer. </p><p>Where Envision builds most of the pieces in-house, CATL links them through partnerships and equity stakes.</p><p>China&#8217;s datacenter market is highly competitive. Rents have roughly halved over the past five years, to about $40/kW/month excluding power. Capex has fallen about 25% over the same period, to less than $3mm per MW. Power in Inner Mongolia costs about $0.055/kWh all-in, among the lowest in the world. </p><p>In a market like this, owning more of the value chain is one way to differentiate, a playbook familiar from China&#8217;s EV market, where players like BYD make much of what goes into their cars.</p><p>It is too early to say whether vertical integration will become a meaningful advantage in China&#8217;s datacenter market. For now, it gives integrated players more control over cost and schedule, but hyperscalers still choose vendors mainly on resources and track record. That could change as power becomes harder to secure and the power system inside the datacenter is redesigned.</p><p>Behind the paywall, we walk through how Galaxy was built and designed in detail. </p><ol><li><p>On speed and cost, we follow Galaxy from land auction to customer acceptance and explain why capex keeps falling. </p></li><li><p>On power, we trace how China took a different path from the US, adopting 240V DC in its datacenters years ago, and walk through Galaxy&#8217;s live 5MW pilot.
