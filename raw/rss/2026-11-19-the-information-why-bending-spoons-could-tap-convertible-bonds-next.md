# Why Bending Spoons Could Tap Convertible Bonds Next

<p>Since going public in July, Bending Spoons has tapped the debt and equity markets to raise money mostly to buy more tech firms, and to run its growing portfolio. One financing option the company hasn’t yet used is convertible bonds, but that could make sense soon.&nbsp;</p><p>With interest rates higher now than they have been, the company is considering issuing convertible bonds, as a supplement to its existing financing, a person close to the company said. Convertible bonds generally have lower interest rates than comparable non-convertible debt, because holders get the option to convert into stock, which can dilute existing shareholders.&nbsp;</p><p>Its need for capital is likely to remain intense. Bending Spoons has snapped up a slew of aging but well-known tech brands like AOL, Eventbrite, Airtable and Vimeo, betting it can revive their fortunes through aggressive restructuring. Its most recent acquisition was whiteboarding software company Miro, completed in late October at a valuation of around $1.36 billion, a 90% discount to Miro’s 2021 private valuation.&nbsp;</p><p>The company has typically paid between one and seven times revenue, and is currently targeting buying more companies with at least $500 million in annual revenue with a price tag in the low billions. The firm remains broadly open to acquisitions across different areas of tech, but healthcare technology stands out as an interesting area right now, the person added.</p><p>Bending Spoons has paid for these acquisitions mostly through borrowed money, supplemented by free cash flow and equity. It generated around $250 million in free cash flow in the first six months of 2026, far less than the $2.3 billion it spent on acquisitions.</p><p>Bending Spoons aims to maintain a net leverage ratio—debt minus cash divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization—of two to three times. It was within that range at the end of June, when its net debt of $4.09 billion translated to a leverage ratio at around 2.4 times. (The company is set to report third-quarter earnings on Friday.)&nbsp;</p><p>&nbsp;Moody’s and S&amp;P both rate Bending Spoons below investment grade, so it pays higher rates than blue-chip borrowers. In September, Bending Spoons borrowed roughly $1.7 billion in loans, partly to pay for Miro, and in October it raised $1.8 billion through its first high-yield bond sale.&nbsp;</p><p>Bending Spoons currently pays a blended effective interest rate of around 9% on its debt, the person said, a figure that typically averages borrowing costs across loans and bonds.&nbsp;</p><p>Bending Spoons’ borrowing costs could come down somewhat as the company builds more of a track record as a public company, or if broader interest rates fall. Securing an investment-grade credit rating would allow the company to borrow money more cheaply, but Bending Spoons would need to pare down its leverage or make other trade-offs, which it isn’t considering doing right now, the person said.&nbsp;</p><p>That helps make convertible bonds a potentially attractive option. Bending Spoons views convertible bonds as a potential supplement rather than a core funding source, because they wouldn’t provide funding at a massive scale, the person said. That may partly reflect the company's existing debt or a desire to minimize that potential dilution of existing shareholders.</p><p>Meanwhile, Bending Spoons has raised equity to run and grow the businesses it already owns, and also to help fund deals. Last month it sold $330 million in stock to Berkshire Hathaway, a private placement that followed Berkshire’s $600 million investment in Bending Spoons’ IPO. Issuing equity instead of debt could help the company keep its leverage ratio below the top of its target range.&nbsp;</p><p>Once it completes an acquisition, Bending Spoons lays off workers and brings in around 50 to 60 of its own employees, putting its own staff in leadership roles.
