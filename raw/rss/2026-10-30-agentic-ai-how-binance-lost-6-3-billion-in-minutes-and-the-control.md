# How Binance Lost $6.3 Billion in Minutes — and the Controls We Published Years Ago That Could Have Prevented It

<h2>How it happened</h2><p>Preparation started at least 36 hours before the theft. On the morning of 28 October, the operator wallet received three small withdrawals from Tornado Cash, which obscures the source of funds. Early on 29 October, the operator deployed attack code and tested the delegate-and-sweep sequence on wallets it controlled, with amounts of 2 to 6 dollars each, then rehearsed the final version on a third wallet and routed test funds back to itself. At 15:59 UTC on 29 October, the operator deployed the sweeper contract.</p><p>The theft opened on the XRP Ledger. Five Binance wallets sent 233.46 million XRP to a single attacker address between 20:05 and 20:09 UTC, each wallet keeping only its minimum balance. Solana and Optimism wallets followed shortly after 20:10 UTC. Bitcoin followed at 20:20 UTC, when one Binance Bitcoin hot wallet sent just over 18,270 BTC to an attacker address in 11 transactions, all in block 973,732, each transfer looking like an ordinary Binance withdrawal. Tron followed within about two minutes, with a Binance Tron hot wallet sending 612.7 million USDT plus 46.4 million TRX to the attacker.</p><p>Ethereum then supplied the largest single sweep. At 20:20:59 UTC, one transaction carried 50 EIP-7702 authorisations for Binance 14. At 20:24:23 UTC, the operator called sweep on Binance 14, moving 167,464 ETH, 1.615 billion USDT, and about 60 to 63 other tokens to a collection address with no prior history. About 24 seconds later, Binance 16 sent 28,005.6 ETH, 55.7 million USDT, 25.9 million RLUSD, and 84.5 WBTC to the same collection address by ordinary signed transfer, with no delegation. Within 12 minutes of the first sweep, Optimism, Arbitrum, and Avalanche wallets moved. The delegation kept working after the first sweep: at 20:41:23 UTC, the operator used the still-delegated Binance 14 to move 780 million USDC, then a further 43 million USDC, from a Binance smart account, and at 21:44 UTC swept 200 million USDT plus 13 tokens that had arrived since the first sweep. A second wave hit Avalanche and Polygon after 23:04 UTC, more than two hours after the first sweep.</p><p>Containment arrived in layers, at different speeds. Binance acknowledged a potential security issue near 22:30 UTC and suspended deposits and withdrawals as a precaution. At 00:08 UTC on 30 October, the Arbitrum Security Council used emergency powers to force two transfers out of the attacker Arbitrum wallet, moving 8,426 ETH plus 74.6 million ARB to an intermediary frozen wallet. By the morning of 30 October, Binance said deposits operated normally and withdrawals stayed paused while Binance overhauled the withdrawal service. Issuers froze about 3 billion dollars in stablecoins in total; native ETH and BTC have no issuer that can freeze them, so the value converted into those assets stayed beyond reach.</p><p>Figure 1 is working balances in a hot wallet. A hot wallet that signs continuously can drain in minutes, as Binance wallets did in about 20 minutes. Larger balances belong in warm or cold storage behind slower approval paths, sized so a loss inside the detection-to-containment window stays survivable.</p></li><li><p>Separate wallets by tier and by chain. Hot, warm, and cold wallets must not share one signing service, one credential set, or one approval path, and no single human, laptop, or service container may authorise transfers across all chains. <a href="https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/artifacts/csa-crypto-asset-exchange-security-guidelines-abstract/">Our CSA Crypto-Asset Exchange Security Guidelines</a> set out hot-wallet and cold-wallet separation as exchange-operator best practice.</p></li><li><p>Require multisig and a second approval for material transfers. Cold-wallet transfers need multisig, and any large transfer, new destination, or cross-chain transfer needs approval on a separate device from the request. A single laptop or service container must never be enough to move exchange funds.</p></li><li><p>Harden engineer laptops that touch signing. Privileged DevOps laptops need endpoint controls, separate credentials, and no direct path to request signatures without that second approval, because Binance said a compromised DevOps laptop gave access to a service container that submitted unauthorised withdrawals.</p></li><li><p>Pre-sign delegation revocation and monitor hot addresses for code changes every block. A delegation persists after a sweep, so a tested revocation ready for submission matters more than detection alone.</p></li><li><p>Pre-agree issuer coordination. Pre-label hot addresses with Tether, Circle, and every other issuer, and agree the freeze-request format in advance, because the gap before issuers acted allowed hundreds of millions to convert into assets beyond issuer reach.</p></li><li><p>Hold a reserve buffer above 100 percent for hot-wallet coins, larger for ETH and BTC. <a href="https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-39288-7">Our book, A Comprehensive Guide for Web3 Security</a>, treats wallets, smart contracts, and DevSecOps processes as foundational Web3 security building blocks.</p></li></ul><p>These controls are not new, and they are not borrowed. We published them with fellow researchers years before this incident: the CSA Crypto-Asset Exchange Security Guidelines in 2021 and A Comprehensive Guide for Web3 Security in 2023. Those controls still apply to this incident, and to the future security of crypto and blockchain systems.</p>
