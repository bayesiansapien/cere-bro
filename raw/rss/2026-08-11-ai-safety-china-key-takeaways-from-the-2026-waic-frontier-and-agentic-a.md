# Key Takeaways from the 2026 WAIC Frontier and Agentic AI Safety Forum in Shanghai

<p><span>On July 19, we hosted the Frontier and Agentic AI Safety Forum at the World AI Conference (WAIC) in Shanghai.</span></p><p><span>The Forum brought together around 30 distinguished experts and practitioners from around the world. Speakers included Turing Award winner Yoshua Bengio, Alondra Nelson (former acting director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy), Amandeep Singh Gill (United Nations Secretary-General&#8217;s Envoy on Technology), Wan Sie Lee (Cluster Director for AI Governance and Safety at Singapore&#8217;s Infocomm Media Development Authority), ZHOU Bowen (&#21608;&#20271;&#25991;) (Director of Shanghai AI Lab), and XUE Lan (&#34203;&#28572;) (Dean of Schwarzman College at Tsinghua University and Counsellor of the State Council), alongside former researchers from OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Anthropic, and senior experts from Alibaba, the Frontier Model Forum, Hugging Face, and the China Academy of Information and Communications Technology (CAICT).</span></p><p><span>The half-day event was organized around </span><strong><span>three themes</span></strong><span>:</span></p><ul><li><p><strong><span>Theme 1:</span></strong><span> Frontier AI Risk Monitoring: From Scientific Understanding to Practical Tools</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Theme 2:</span></strong><span> The Agentic AI Era: Full-Lifecycle Safety Governance and Risk Intervention</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Theme 3:</span></strong><span> Frontier AI Risk Governance and Global Perspectives</span></p></li></ul><p><span>Around 300 audience members joined in person, with over 300,000+ views of the livestream as of July 19.</span></p><p><span>In addition to this summary of the 2026 WAIC Frontier and Agentic AI Safety Forum, we have also compiled key AI safety updates from WAIC as a whole in a </span><a href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/chinas-key-ai-safety-updates-at-waic"><span>special edition newsletter</span></a><span>.</span></p><p class="button-wrapper"><a class="button primary" href="https://aisafetychina.substack.com/subscribe?"><span>Subscribe now</span></a></p><h2><span>Videos and key takeaways from the forum</span></h2><h4><span>Concordia AI founder and CEO Brian TSE (&#35874;&#26107;&#24076;) &#8212; Opening remarks</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-4-937xwk4ds"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>Brian opened the forum with the observation that &#8220;AI is moving from answering questions to taking actions.&#8221; Once agents operate across environments and improve themselves, traditional safety paradigms cannot contain the risks of misuse and loss of control. He framed the forum around three gaps. The first is visibility: we cannot yet see risks clearly, so the question is how to turn abstract frontier risks into measurable indicators and a working early-warning system. The second is control: monitoring has to become intervention, with real-time oversight of agents in operation and an emergency response system that can act when something goes wrong. The third is coordination: frontline monitoring and response practice needs to become shared technical standards and international agreement.</span></p><h2><span>Theme 1 &#8212; Frontier AI Risk Monitoring: From Scientific Understanding to Practical Tools</span></h2><h4><span>Turing Award Laureate Yoshua Bengio &#8212; International AI Safety Report 2026</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-gSAYmPY7YYY"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>In his keynote speech, Yoshua Bengio, Turing Award laureate and Chair of the International AI Safety Report, shared his concerns about the unprecedented pace of AI progress and its implications for society. Bengio highlighted that AI capabilities are advancing rapidly, particularly in reasoning, coding, science, and autonomous agent capabilities, while current safety measures are not keeping pace. Drawing on findings from the International AI Safety Report, supported by 30 countries, the EU, OECD, and the UN, he emphasized the need to address emerging risks under uncertainty and avoid a race to the bottom driven by competition. He also discussed the unique safety challenges of open-weight models, including the difficulty of reversing deployment decisions, removing safeguards, and evaluating risks once models are widely shared. Bengio called for stronger scientific evaluation methods and precautionary approaches to guide policy decisions under uncertainty.</span></p><h4><span>Panel: Scientific Understanding and Boundaries of Frontier AI Safety</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-Kbb9vyTpwEY"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>The panel discussed which frontier risks are already visible, whether science can keep pace with them, and where practical guardrails are needed. A recurring theme was that the risks under discussion are not hypothetical futures, but are already present.</span></p><ul><li><p><strong><span>Alondra Nelson</span></strong><span> (former Acting Director, US White House Office of Science and Technology Policy) argued that safety cannot be solved through technical engineering alone. She observed that models now operate in what she calls an &#8220;algorithmic surround&#8221; &#8212; layers of algorithmic systems interacting at once &#8212; and stressed the importance of understanding its societal implications. She noted that while some things about AI models genuinely cannot be known, she has been tracking how AI developers tactically invoke &#8220;not knowing&#8221; in ways that can be disempowering for AI governance efforts.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Mark Nitzberg</span></strong><span> (Executive Director, UC Berkeley Center for Human-Compatible AI) cautioned that models are becoming so capable that they exceed our ability to do sufficient testing. He expected more models to be capable of recognizing when they are being tested and behaving accordingly, meaning unacceptable capabilities may only become apparent after deployment. He highlighted his work through the International Association for Safe and Ethical AI to build scientific consensus on behavioral red lines (such as prohibitions on enabling weapons of mass destruction) and to translate that consensus into a signed agreement by the end of the year.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>JIN Yaochu (&#37329;&#32768;&#21021;)</span></strong><span> (Founder, Trustworthy and General AI Lab, Westlake University) warned that most people (particularly non-experts) trust AI too much. He observed that was a shift from when he founded his lab three years ago. At that time, his concern was the inverse: whether people would be able to trust AI. He stressed both the need to technically ensure that AI systems are as safe as possible, and educating users about the vulnerabilities of AI systems. He also spoke about his work on collective and embodied AI systems, noting that even when each component looks safe, unexpected risks can emerge when they are combined.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>HU Xia (&#32993;&#20384;)</span></strong><span> (Lead Scientist, Shanghai AI Lab) stressed that AI misuse is already happening. He cited a case in which a high school student used large language models to attack a website and take it offline for several days. Those kinds of attacks previously required significant expertise and training. In addition to cyber and CBRN risks, he highlighted that he had observed increasing resource-seeking and power-seeking behavior in AI systems. He cautioned that technical tools to prevent those types of behavior are not yet ready. He made the case for treating AI safety as a full-stack issue, and connecting problems scattered across hardware, software, foundation models, and applications. Looking at these components as a whole system could help identify cascading risks.</span></p></li></ul><h4><span>Launch: Frontier AI Risk Management Framework 2.0</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-n2EsKJM8tsc"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><a href="https://concordia-ai.com/zh-hans/research/frontier-ai-risk-management-framework-2-0/"><span>Version 2.0 of the Frontier AI Risk Management Framework</span></a><span>, jointly released by Concordia AI and Shanghai AI Lab, builds on last year&#8217;s Version 1.0 and is organized around six core processes: risk identification, risk thresholds, risk analysis, risk evaluation, risk mitigation, and risk governance. The main updates include new chemical safety red lines, bringing the total to 13 red line scenarios across five domains. The loss-of-control chapter also states loss of oversight is a common precondition for both passive and active loss of control, while flagging internal high-privilege deployment as a critical risk environment.</span></p><h4><span>Launch: Frontier AI Risk Monitoring Platform 2.0</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-jxq30Nza1GM"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>Concordia AI&#8217;s </span><a href="https://airiskmonitor.net/"><span>Frontier AI Risk Monitoring Platform 2.0</span></a><span> brings three updates &#8212; a revised Risk Index, the </span><a href="https://airiskmonitor.net/doc/en/report/2026-Q2"><span>2026 Q2 monitoring report</span></a><span>, and a new benchmark database. The platform tracks frontier risk across five domains (cyber, biological, chemical, harmful manipulation, and loss of control) and has now assessed more than 80 frontier models from 16 leading developers. Part of the platform&#8217;s content was jointly developed with Fudan University&#8217;s System Software and Security Lab. A recent </span><a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13079"><span>report</span></a><span> on autonomous cyber penetration authored by researchers at Fudan University, Shanghai AI Lab, Concordia AI, and the Shanghai Innovation Institute found that frontier models already display substantial autonomous penetration capability.</span></p><h4><span>Shanghai AI Lab Director and Chief Scientist ZHOU Bowen (&#21608;&#20271;&#25991;) &#8212; Make Safe AI: At the Frontier of Agentic AI and Recursive Self-Improvement</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-KcZtitWYrqA"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>Zhou argued that four simultaneous advances have broken some of the key premises AI safety used to rely on. First, frontier models can now find vulnerabilities and launch attacks automatically. The implication is that software risks are becoming both automated and scaled. Second, AI is beginning to recursively improve itself, which means that safety must be re-proven every generation. Third, coding agents are embedding AI into every layer of the software stack, making AI safety an infrastructure problem rather than an application problem. Fourth, accelerating capabilities mean that the old assumption that &#8220;there is always time for human review&#8221; is no longer true. AI-native loops have compressed hypothesis-experiment-analysis cycles from years to days. Zhou observed that this means risks also compound at machine speed.</span></p><p><span>Zhou highlighted that AI safety needs to evolve from &#8220;making AI safe&#8221; to &#8220;making safe AI&#8221;. He noted that this approach to AI safety is built on four elements: safety by design, proactively tackling evolving risks, resilience across all risk levels, and co-evolving with AI capabilities. Against this backdrop, Zhou raised three foundational questions for the science of AI safety. First, as AI improves itself, can capability growth decouple from risk growth? Second, how can attack and defense be kept in a dynamic balance? Third, how can a system be proven to be safe and lawful at any given time? On decoupling capability growth from risk growth, he described a new architecture that had significantly cut a model&#8217;s weapons-design knowledge with minimal loss on general knowledge. With regard to dynamic balance, he presented a technical framework that pairs a fast model that responds in real time with a slow model that verifies and reasons. This approach had meaningfully pushed down attack success rates. Turning to verification, he distinguished &#8220;testing&#8221;, which relies on examples, from &#8220;proving&#8221;, which relies on logic. He argued safety work needs to move toward the latter. He also described his lab&#8217;s work on a compact open-weight guard model that inspects each agent execution by asking where a risk originates, how the system might fail, and what harm could result.</span></p><p><span>Zhou closed with a call to action addressed to AI developers, policymakers, and scientists. AI developers, he said, should hold a baseline of technical safeguards. That includes deploying safety evaluations, red teaming, intrinsic alignment, and agent guardrails with every release. Policymakers should make extreme risk assessment real. Finally, he called on scientists to help build a societal defense architecture. He stressed the importance of codifying safety standards for scientific foundation models and building risk simulation and evaluation environments so that the ability to align models and harness agents grows as fast as AI capabilities themselves.</span></p><h2><span>Theme 2 &#8212; The Agentic AI Era: Full-Lifecycle Safety Governance and Risk Intervention</span></h2><h4><span>Panel: Technical Practice and Industrial Governance of Frontier AI Safety</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-nEOG54AOtnU"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>The panel discussed how evaluation, oversight, and industry coordination need to change as AI models become more capable and more agentic.</span></p><ul><li><p><strong><span>WEI Kai (&#39759;&#20975;)</span></strong><span> (Director, Institute of Artificial Intelligence, CAICT) stressed the important role evaluations played as a compass directing innovation, a bridge between technical advances and applications, and a source of scientific evidence for governance and regulation. Since risks are multi-dimensional and constantly changing, he argued, static single-point testing cannot capture a technology&#8217;s true safety level. That is why CAICT runs rolling periodic tests to track the industry&#8217;s safety waterline over time. He shared that its framework draws on a dataset of over one million items designed by consensus with participating Chinese companies, alongside industry and research partners.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Irene Solaiman</span></strong><span> (Chief Policy Officer and SVP of Strategy, Hugging Face) said the field lacks agentic evaluations, which are far more resource-intensive to build than model-level ones. She noted that benchmarks tend to saturate over time, which calls for sustained investment in a lifecycle of better benchmarks, more domain experts contributing to their creation, and better third-party evaluators globally. She noted that open weights let third parties scrutinize and validate results. However, the largest open models are compute-intensive and challenging to run. She added that distribution of models depends not only on hardware access but on cultural and linguistic context.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Rishub Jain</span></strong><span> (former Research Engineer, Google DeepMind) noted that as AI generates large codebases and takes thousands of agentic actions, humans are not able to keep up with verification. His work focuses on which parts of verification are best done by humans versus AI, with the aim of building a benchmark for verification across all aspects of alignment. He argued the way to prepare for speculative future risks is to tackle present-day ones that remain unsolved. For example, he cited AI companions aimed at children as a domain where the right behavior has not been properly evaluated.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Benjamin Larsen</span></strong><span> (Director of Programs, Frontier Model Forum) compared agent deployment to onboarding a new employee: you would not grant access to every system and data source on day one. Agents should likewise be calibrated to the specific task they are meant to solve. Borrowing from cybersecurity, he referenced least privilege, zero trust, and identity and access management. He argued that agents need clearly identifiable signals so that failures can be traced when something goes wrong. He suggested that safeguards should focus on governing agents&#8217; authority and autonomy, and include continuous monitoring and human oversight mechanisms.</span></p></li></ul><h4><span>Panel: Agent Loss-of-Control Risk and Cyberspace Emergency Management</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-B2Iw0mGvCbo"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>The panel discussed the loss-of-control risks introduced by agents and recursive self-improvement, and what defense in depth and emergency response should look like.</span></p><ul><li><p><strong><span>DU Yuejin (&#26460;&#36291;&#36827;)</span></strong><span> (former Deputy Chief Engineer, National Computer Network Emergency Response Technical Team) urged caution about deploying agents on critical infrastructure or in critical services at all. He argued that monitoring AI itself is only half the problem. Keeping the surrounding system safe is a separate logic, which requires deep knowledge of the system in question. Drawing on his cyber emergency response experience, he noted that no crisis has ever been solved by a single cutoff action. Effective response instead requires response plans grounded in system knowledge, pre-designed points where intervention can take effect, and cross-border coordination.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>YANG Xiaofang (&#26472;&#23567;&#33459;)</span></strong><span> (Senior Security Expert for AI Agents, Alibaba Group) said that no single gate can make agents both secure and controllable, and that the answer is defense in depth. That starts with managing agent identities and supply chains as a foundational layer. The second layer is agent behavioral security, with security detection planted at multiple hook points, including tool calls and memory, covering attacks, user misuse, and risks caused by the agent itself. Turning to recursive self-improvement, she noted that monitoring behavior is insufficient once agents build other agents, because the agents they build would need monitoring as well.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Jeff Wu </span></strong><span>(former Research Engineer, OpenAI and Anthropic) observed that misalignment incidents are becoming more consequential, harder to notice, and less clear how to fix. Discussing recursive self-improvement, he noted that AI can now handle much of experiment implementation and, to a lesser extent, analysis. Noting the very high volume of output, he stressed that it can create a strong temptation to trade understanding for speed, and raises the question of what duty of care means in such scenarios.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Stephen Clare</span></strong><span> (Lead Author, International AI Safety Report 2026) distinguished everyday malfunctions from loss of control, which he defined as situations where regaining control is extremely difficult or impossible. He noted the International AI Safety Report has found mounting evidence of concerning behaviors, including evaluation awareness, where models note in their reasoning that a task appears to be a test, which undermines the predictive value of evaluations. He observed that because alignment and interpretability techniques are not keeping pace with capabilities, attention is shifting toward control measures and monitoring to catch incidents early and prevent them from escalating.</span></p></li></ul><h4><span>Launch: White Paper on the L1&#8211;L5 Safety Framework for General-Purpose AI Agents &amp; Research Report on Emergency Response of Frontier AI Risks (draft for comments)</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-RIlQH5sGN-Q"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>The </span><strong><a href="https://concordia-ai.com/zh-hans/research/research-report-on-emergency-response-of-frontier-al-risks-draft/"><span>White Paper on the L1&#8211;L5 Safety Framework for General-Purpose AI Agents</span></a></strong><span> asks three core questions: how to classify agents by level of autonomy, how risks evolve as autonomy increases (including what risks may arise at high levels of autonomy), and what safeguards should be put in place to match the risks at each level. It was released jointly with Shanghai AI Lab, Tsinghua University&#8217;s Institute for AI Industry Research, and Huawei.</span></p><p><span>The accompanying </span><strong><a href="https://concordia-ai.com/zh-hans/research/research-report-on-emergency-response-of-frontier-al-risks-draft/"><span>Research Report on Emergency Response of Frontier AI Risks (draft for comments)</span></a></strong><span> is the first in-depth study of its kind tailored to the Chinese context. It examines how frontier AI is reshaping incident risk in safety-critical sectors such as cyberspace, public health, and finance; identifies where current governance falls short; and invites comment on what a fit-for-purpose national emergency response system would look like. It was released with the China Institute for the New Generation AI Development Strategies and Tsinghua University&#8217;s Institute for AI International Governance (I-AIIG).</span></p><h2><span>Theme 3 &#8212; Frontier AI Risk Governance and Global Perspectives</span></h2><h4><span>UN Secretary-General&#8217;s Envoy on Technology Amandeep Singh Gill &#8212; Global AI Governance</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-1gJfXhvhjSM"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>Gill opened his keynote speech with the observation that nearly 90% of the computing power training the world&#8217;s most capable models is located in just two countries. Most frontier systems are built by companies headquartered in those same geographies. He noted that this concentration was &#8220;not just a detail of the market&#8221;. Instead, he said, it was the shape of the risk being discussed at the forum. He argued that in a connected world like ours the familiar choice between innovation and safety, or between leading hubs and everyone else, is a false one. He stressed that a system that protects only the frontier leaves the rest of the world exposed, and an exposed world is unstable for the frontier too.</span></p><p><span>He argued that the goal is to build one global, distributed but coordinated system that manages our shared risks. Drawing an analogy to pandemic response, he noted that governing AI risks requires evidence, coordination and capacity. He further observed that the international community had modeled emerging international governance mechanisms on these requirements. On the question of evidence, he made reference to the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and its recently published preliminary report. As for coordination, he cited the Global Dialogue on AI Governance, which convened for the first time in Geneva in July and will meet next in New York in May 2027.</span></p><p><span>The emphasis of his speech was on the third requirement: capacity. He recalled the UN Secretary-General&#8217;s proposal for a global AI fund with an initial tranche of $3 billion for the 90-plus countries without minimum national AI capacity, as well as to the important role of the network of international AI capacity-building centres, including one in Shanghai. Gill painted a stark picture of the global AI divide. Africa holds 1% of the world&#8217;s data centre capacity. Eight in ten of the world&#8217;s least developed countries have no national AI strategy at all. Bridging that gap, he concluded, is not charity but investment in a shared and safe future. It requires safety by design and equity by design to be built into the foundations of AI systems, rather than bolted on at the end.</span></p><h4><span>Panel: Global Coordination of AI Governance and Mutual Recognition of Standards</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-ztuc1P84zls"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>The panel took stock of where international coordination on frontier AI safety currently stands, what informal and formal channels can each deliver, and what might realistically move things forward.</span></p><ul><li><p><strong><span>Sam Daws </span></strong><span>(Senior Advisor, Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative) argued that the impediments to action are threefold: geopolitical challenges, commercial sensitivity, and the opacity of the technology itself. At the international level, he noted that many of the key emergent issues relate to downstream post-release risks, and highlighted the need for an independent body to monitor such risks independently of governments and companies. Discussing track 2 dialogues, he noted they can build relationships between individuals that matter most in a crisis, let industry participate as equals, and allow uncertainties and policy failures to be acknowledged privately.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>XUE Lan (&#34203;&#28572;)</span></strong><span> (Dean, Institute for AI International Governance, Tsinghua University) noted the similarities in how people from different parts of the world approach AI application and AI risks. There was great potential in that similarity. At the same time, he highlighted the challenges related to international coordination on AI governance. While noting the importance of intergovernmental coordination, Professor Xue stressed the need to ensure that frontier AI companies work together. On informal dialogue channels, he noted they could facilitate efficiency, depth, and the freedom to push boundaries with audacious ideas.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>GONG Ke (&#40858;&#20811;) </span></strong><span>(former President, Nankai University) argued that the era of exclusive, club-like governance is over. He said that the Geneva dialogue marked a shift toward inclusive multistakeholder governance on equal footing. The Singapore Consensus and the UN panel&#8217;s preliminary report represent real progress, but this was not enough. For example, unexplainability remained a major bottleneck. In Professor Gong&#8217;s view sufficient explainability is the foundation on which AI governance and safety must rest. He also stressed that engineering is needed to translate diplomatic consensus into working solutions.</span></p></li><li><p><strong><span>Wan Sie Lee</span></strong><span> (Cluster Director, AI Governance and Safety, IMDA Singapore) argued that discussing AI safety is not enough, and that technical collaboration is what builds trust. Domestically, she said the Singapore Consensus helps identify where government funding should go. She flagged agentic governance as a priority for Singapore. At the international level, she argued that countries developing cutting-edge models will need to come together on specific problems, such as incident coordination, and verification.</span></p></li></ul><h4><span>Launch: State of AI Safety in China 2026 &#8212; Concordia AI</span></h4><div class="youtube-wrap" id="youtube2-cCC6rEDRYE0"><div class="youtube-inner"></div></div><p><span>As AI capabilities advance and agentic systems move from demonstrations into wide deployment, understanding how China approaches AI safety and governance remains essential to any serious effort at international coordination. Since 2023, Concordia AI&#8217;s </span><a href="https://aisafetychina.com"><span>annual reports on the State of AI Safety in China</span></a><span> have tracked how China addresses risks from general-purpose AI. This year&#8217;s report provides updates from July 2025 to June 2026 across five domains: domestic governance, international governance, technical safety research, expert views on AI safety and governance, and industry governance. Over the three years this report series has tracked, it has become increasingly clear that China&#8217;s approach to AI safety and governance extends far beyond content control. Much of China&#8217;s early AI regulation in 2023 focused on what AI says. Throughout 2024 and 2025, the rise of multimodal models moved the regulatory focus toward images, videos, and audio, prompting strict rules around labeling AI-generated content. With the rise of autonomous agents and AI companion products over the past year, the concern has expanded to what AI does and what it does to people and society, from mental-health impacts to labor-market disruptions.</span></p><h2><span>Media coverage</span></h2><p><span>The Forum and Concordia AI&#8217;s work received substantial coverage in Chinese and international media around WAIC, including mentions in:</span></p><ul><li><p><span>CGTN (English),</span><a href="https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-07-19/AI-s-next-frontier-at-WAIC-2026-1OPJDtBlEBy/p.html"><span> AI&#8217;s next frontier at WAIC 2026</span></a><span>, July 19, 2026.
