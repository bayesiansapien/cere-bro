# Beyond Mythos: why automated security validation becomes more critical in the AI Era

<p>Thank you Ridge Security for sponsoring this article</p><p><strong>Authors</strong></p><p>Nick Mo, CEO of <a href="https://ridgesecurity.ai/">Ridge Security</a></p><p>Ken Huang, CEO of <a href="http://distributedapps.ai">DistributedApps.ai</a></p><p><em>AI has changed offense. This paper explains why that makes continuous, automated</em></p><p><em>security validation is more important than ever &#8212; not less.</em></p><h1><strong>1.  Executive Summary</strong></h1><p>In April 2026, Anthropic previewed <strong>Claude Mythos</strong> &#8212; an advanced AI system capable of discovering complex vulnerabilities in production-grade code, developing working exploits on the first attempt in over 83% of cases, and uncovering a 27-year-old vulnerability in OpenBSD during pre-release testing. It followed a string of similar milestones: Google Big Sleep intercepted a live zero-day exploit in SQLite before any human defender, DARPA&#8217;s AIxCC demonstrated end-to-end autonomous vulnerability discovery and patching, and AI-orchestrated offensive activity has been observed in the wild at scale.</p><p>The security industry&#8217;s response has been predictable. Some call it the end of traditional defense. Others call it marketing theater. Both miss the point.</p><p>AI has genuinely changed offense. But most of the conversation around Mythos focuses on the wrong question &#8212; <em>can AI find bugs?</em> The answer is yes, and it has been yes for years. The more important questions are:</p><ul><li><p>If AI finds old bugs, why is it simultaneously writing thousands of new bugs into production code every day?</p></li><li><p>When attackers use AI to find one working path into your environment, what does your defense look like?</p></li><li><p>Before worrying about the vulnerabilities Mythos will disclose tomorrow, do you know which of your existing vulnerabilities are reachable and exploitable right now?</p></li><li><p>Will the next generation of AI models replace the dedicated security products organizations have invested in?</p></li></ul><p>This paper argues that the arrival of Mythos-class capabilities does not make traditional security obsolete. Instead, it makes one discipline more critical than ever: <strong>continuous, </strong>autonomous <strong> security validation.</strong> Not because AI changes the defender&#8217;s fundamental asymmetry &#8212; but because it widens it, and only continuous validation closes that gap.</p><p><strong>Before you read on:</strong></p><p>Ridge Security is offering an additional 100 credits to readers of this article to experience Agentic AI-powered website testing. What Mythos Actually Represents</strong></h1><p>Let us be clear about what has changed.</p><p>AI systems can now find real, exploitable vulnerabilities in complex software faster than human researchers and at scale. Anthropic&#8217;s internal testing found Mythos identified <strong>thousands of previously unknown zero-day vulnerabilities</strong> across every major operating system and every major web browser &#8212; bugs that survived decades of human security review and millions of automated tests.</p><p>Google&#8217;s Big Sleep made history in July 2025: it detected and intercepted <strong>CVE-2025-6965</strong> &#8212; a critical integer overflow in SQLite &#8212; <strong>before any public exploit surfaced.</strong> This was the first confirmed case of an AI agent directly foiling a zero-day exploit in the wild. Independent research by Daniel Kang demonstrated that GPT-4 could exploit known software vulnerabilities autonomously with an <strong>87% success rate at a cost of $8.80 per exploit.</strong></p><p><strong>This is not theater. It is real. But it is not entirely new.</strong></p><p>Finding bugs in open source code has been possible for decades through static analysis, fuzzing, and skilled manual research. The real shift is not that AI can find bugs. The Exploitation Timeline Collapse: Numbers Defenders Must Know</strong></h1><p>The speed asymmetry between attackers and defenders has been widening for years. AI has turned a trend into a structural crisis. The Zero Day Clock &#8212; which tracks time-to-exploit across more than 83,000 CVE-exploit pairs drawn from CISA KEV, VulnCheck KEV, and XDB &#8212; tells a story that reframes every vulnerability management conversation.</p><blockquote><p><strong>2018 median TTE:  </strong>771 days &#8212; organizations had over 2 years to patch</p><p><strong>2021 median TTE:  </strong>84 days &#8212; 9&#215; compression in 3 years</p><p><strong>2023 median TTE:  </strong>6 days</p><p><strong>2024 median TTE:  </strong>4 hours</p><p><strong>2025 &#8212; majority of CVEs:  </strong>Weaponized before public disclosure</p><p><strong>2026 zero-day rate:  </strong>67.2% of exploited CVEs are zero-days (up from 16.1% in 2018)</p><p><strong>Average org patch time:  </strong>20 days to test and deploy a single patch</p><p><strong>CISA KEV remediation:  </strong>55 days to remediate 50% of known-exploited vulnerabilities</p><p><strong>Pre-disclosure exploitation:  </strong>~30% of CISA KEVs exploited on or before the day the CVE was published</p><p><strong>CVE volume growth:  </strong>40,000+ CVEs in 2024 &#8212; a 520% rise over a decade</p></blockquote><p><strong>The math is unambiguous: </strong>when attackers weaponize vulnerabilities in hours and defenders need 55 days to remediate 50% of critical findings, the traditional disclosure-to-patch model has inverted. When a vendor releases a security patch, AI can now reverse-engineer it, identify the underlying vulnerability, and generate a working exploit &#8212; in minutes. Attacks can begin propagating within hours. The entire model our security programs were built around no longer holds.</p><h1><strong>4.  The Other Side of Mythos: AI Is Writing Vulnerable Code at Scale</strong></h1><p>There is a second story receiving far less attention. The same AI systems that find vulnerabilities are also writing a very large amount of new vulnerable code into production, every day.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Veracode (Sep 2025):  </strong>45% of AI-generated code samples failed security tests and introduced OWASP Top 10 vulnerabilities</p><p><strong>Apiiro (Sep 2025):  </strong>AI-generated code produced ~10&#215; more security findings per month by mid-2025 vs. late 2024</p><p><strong>CodeRabbit (Dec 2025):  </strong>AI-authored code had 1.7&#215; more major issues: improper authentication, IDOR, XSS</p><p><strong>ProjectDiscovery (Apr 2026):  </strong>66% of security practitioners spend more than half their time manually validating findings, not fixing them</p></blockquote><p><em><strong>&#8220;AI finds old bugs&#8221; gets the headlines. &#8220;AI ships new bugs&#8221; gets a footnote.</strong></em></p><p>There is a reason: finding old bugs makes for a compelling demo; fixing new bugs is Jira tickets. But the security consequence is severe. Defenders are now facing two simultaneous and compounding challenges:</p><p><strong>Challenge 1 &#8212; Offense is accelerating. </strong>AI-driven attackers can identify viable paths into environments at machine speed, chain multi-step exploits autonomously, and move from disclosure to weaponization in hours.</p><p><strong>Challenge 2 &#8212; The attack surface is spreading. </strong>AI-generated code is expanding the defender&#8217;s exposure faster than traditional AppSec programs can manage. Every sprint cycle adds new vulnerabilities that conventional scanners struggle to prioritize.</p><p>Mythos makes the first problem more visible. Applied to increasingly AI-generated codebases, it <em>compounds</em> the second problem. This is the double asymmetry AI has introduced &#8212; and it is the reason continuous, automated validation is no longer optional.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The Double Asymmetry: Attackers find more, faster. Defenders inherit more to defend, slower.</strong></p></blockquote><h1><strong>5.  What AI Has Not Changed: The Defender&#8217;s Asymmetry</strong></h1><p>The asymmetry between attackers and defenders remains unchanged &#8212; and is now amplified.</p><p><strong>An attacker needs to find one working path. </strong>One exploitable vulnerability. One misconfigured asset. One forgotten service account. One unpatched middleware instance. That is all.</p><p><strong>A defender needs to know about all of them. </strong>Every vulnerability in every system, validated for real-world exploitability in the context of their specific environment. Every change, every deployment, every third-party integration &#8212; continuously.</p><p>The industry&#8217;s response for the last two decades has been to add more visibility &#8212; more scanners, more SIEMs, more EDR, more cloud posture tools. Every new tool generates more signals, but not necessarily clarity. The Cyentia Institute found that organizations can only remediate about 10% of new vulnerabilities per month, meaning exposure remains for 99.9% of the vulnerability lifecycle.</p><p>Most security leaders describe the same pattern: heavy investment in detection and observability that results in more alerts than their teams can act on. The gap between <em>knowing a vulnerability exists</em> and <em>knowing whether an attacker can reach it in your environment</em> has never been wider. And 92% of vulnerabilities on enterprise dashboards are, in context, pure noise &#8212; not reachable, not exploitable, not a real risk.</p><p>AI widens this gap. Attackers can now search the possibility space faster than defenders can investigate their own alerts. What defenders need is not more signals, but a <strong>decision layer</strong>: one that translates raw signals into validated, prioritized, actionable risk. That shift begins with a different question: not &#8220;does this vulnerability exist?&#8221; but &#8220;<em><strong>can it actually be used against me, right now?</strong></em>&#8220;</p><h1><strong>6.  Why Finding Is Not Defending</strong></h1><p>Three observations from real enterprise environments highlight the limitations of traditional vulnerability management:</p><h2><strong>Finding is not the same as validating.</strong></h2><p>A high CVSS score does not mean an attacker can reach the vulnerable component. Lower CVSS scores can still be critical in chained attacks &#8212; and many enterprise VM programs prioritize incorrectly as a result. The ProjectDiscovery 2026 AI Coding Impact Report found two-thirds of security practitioners spend more than half their time manually validating findings &#8212; not fixing vulnerabilities. That is the definition of a broken prioritization model.</p><h2><strong>Validating once is not the same as validating continuously.</strong></h2><p>Periodic penetration tests provide a snapshot, while your environment changes daily. An attack path that did not exist last quarter exists today &#8212; introduced by a new deployment, a cloud misconfiguration, or a third-party component. You will not know about it until your next scheduled test. Or until an attacker tells you, by breaching.</p><h2><strong>Validating your own environment is not the same as validating your supply chain.</strong></h2><p>This is especially true in Asia and other fast-growing markets, where supply chains are hyper-connected and oversight is fragmented. The product is the scaffolding. The scaffolding is what enterprise defenders actually need.</p></blockquote><h1><strong>11.  A Practical Path Forward</strong></h1><p>For security leaders, the practical question is not whether to adopt AI, but how to apply AI-driven validation within an existing stack &#8212; without creating yet another dashboard for the team to ignore. Three principles, based on what we observe working in the field:</p><h2><strong>Start with exploitability, not volume.</strong></h2><p>Do not try to validate every finding from every tool. Start with the assets that matter most &#8212; crown jewels, regulated systems, internet-exposed infrastructure, and supply chain connections. Expand from there. The goal is a shorter, higher-confidence list of validated attack paths, not a larger list of potential exposures.</p><h2><strong>Integrate validation into remediation, not reporting.</strong></h2><p>Validation should feed directly into patching workflows, ticketing systems, and change management. Validated, exploitable findings should move quickly. Unvalidated or unreachable findings should be deprioritized automatically. The Mondoo 2025 State of Vulnerability Remediation Report found 62% of teams still use manual remediation workflows and only 2% are fully automated. That gap is where breaches occur.</p><h2><strong>Measure the right things.</strong></h2><p>Not the number of vulnerabilities found, but: how quickly exposures are validated; how many exploitable attack paths are reduced; how fast environments are re-tested after change. These metrics reflect whether your security posture is actually improving &#8212; not just whether your scanner found more.</p><h2><strong>The Three Questions Every Security Leader Must Answer</strong></h2><p>Before evaluating any new security investment, every CISO and IT leader should be able to answer with confidence and evidence:</p><p><strong>How often are you testing? </strong>If the answer is quarterly, annually, or ad hoc, your security posture between assessments is effectively unknown. With exploitation timelines measured in hours, threat actors do not wait for your next scheduled test.</p><p><strong>Are you validating exploitability? </strong>Identifying vulnerabilities is not the same as understanding which ones are actually reachable and exploitable in your environment. Most organizations cannot answer this with confidence.</p><p><strong>How fast can you respond? </strong>If your response requires manual triage, ticketing, and committee approval &#8212; your remediation time is measured in weeks. AI-driven exploitation is measured in hours. The math does not work.</p><blockquote><p><strong>If you hesitate on any of these three questions, the gap between what your security program assumes and what an adversary can actually do is measurable &#8212; and increasingly exploitable.</strong></p></blockquote><h1><strong>12.  Closing</strong></h1><p>The Mythos moment is not the end of traditional security. It is not the arrival of a silver bullet for defenders either. It is a clarifying event &#8212; a signal that the asymmetry between attackers and defenders has widened, and that the slow, periodic, snapshot-driven security models that served this industry for two decades are no longer sufficient.</p><p>The data is unambiguous. Exploitation timelines have compressed from 771 days to sub-hourly. The zero-day rate has grown from 16% to 67% of exploited CVEs. AI-generated code is producing new vulnerabilities at 10&#215; the prior rate. And two-thirds of security practitioners are spending more than half their time manually validating findings instead of fixing them.</p><p>Most of what the industry has debated about Mythos &#8212; whether AI can find bugs, whether this is marketing or substance, whether the next model will replace existing security products &#8212; misses the harder question: what do defenders do now?</p><p>The answer is not to buy more detection tools or generate more alerts. It is to close the gap between knowing and defending, <strong>continuously and at scale.</strong></p><p>Automated security validation is the discipline that closes that gap. Scanners without validation produce noise. Detection without validation produces alert fatigue. Penetration testing without continuity produces snapshots that expire the moment they are delivered. Validation &#8212; automated, continuous, and exploitability-driven &#8212; is what turns a security program from a compliance exercise into a defense capability.</p><p>Mythos raised the offensive ceiling. The defender&#8217;s response is not to race AI against AI for its own sake. It is to rebuild the security program around what defenders have always needed and rarely had: continuous, evidence-based clarity about which of their thousands of potential exposures an attacker can actually use against them today.</p><blockquote><p><strong>That clarity is now achievable.
