# Claude's Hidden Workspace: Why J-Space Changes AI Safety

<p>Anthropic's new Transformer Circuits paper, <a href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/workspace/index.html">Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models</a>, is easy to misread in the loudest possible way.</p><p>The wrong headline is: "Claude is conscious."</p><p>The useful headline is sharper: frontier language models may have a small internal working surface where selected concepts become reportable, steerable, and useful for flexible reasoning before the model says anything.</p><p>That surface is what the paper calls <strong>J-space</strong>. It is not the whole model. It is not the whole context window. It is not chain of thought. It is a sparse, verbalizable slice of internal activation space found through a new method called the Jacobian lens, or J-lens.</p><p>The free part of this post explains what J-space is and why I think it matters. The paid part turns it into an operating guide: how to use the idea for security reviews, memory architecture, eval design, agent harnesses, and the questions teams should be asking before this becomes product infrastructure.</p><h2>The short version</h2><p>The paper claims that LLMs maintain a privileged subset of internal representations. These representations have several properties that look like a functional global workspace:</p><ul><li><p>The model can report them.</p></li><li><p>Instructions can summon or suppress them.</p></li><li><p>They can carry intermediate reasoning.</p></li><li><p>They can be reused by many downstream computations.</p></li><li><p>They are selective, so routine automatic work can happen without them.</p></li></ul><p>That last point is the one to sit with.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img" It can continue Spanish text, detect that a foreign sentence is anomalous, track some low-level structure, or parse local patterns without making the relevant feature causally available for explicit report. But when you ask the model to name the language, reason flexibly about it, or use it as an argument to some new operation, the J-space starts to matter.</p><p>That is the bridge to safety.</p><p>Most AI safety tooling today audits what the model said or what it did. J-space points to a harder layer: what the model had made available to itself as it was deciding.</p><h2>What the Jacobian lens is doing</h2><p>The J-lens is a readout method. For each layer, it estimates how a small change in an intermediate activation would affect the model's later outputs across many possible contexts. Then it composes that effect with the model's vocabulary output map.</p><p>In plain English: it asks, "If this internal vector were nudged, what words would this make the model more likely to say later?"</p><p>That is different from the logit lens. The logit lens reads intermediate layers as if they already lived in final-output coordinates. The Jacobian lens tries to correct for the fact that representations change across layers.</p><p>The result is a vocabulary-indexed set of directions. If a direction is active, the model is in a state where it could verbalize that concept under the right continuation. The paper defines J-space as a sparse nonnegative combination of these J-lens directions. In their setup, roughly tens of concept directions are meaningfully active at a time, and this component explains only a small fraction of total activation variance.</p><p>That is important. J-space is not "everything the model knows." It is closer to the small set of concepts on the model's internal desk right now.</p><h2>Why the global-workspace analogy fits</h2><p>Global workspace theory in cognitive science distinguishes between a mass of parallel background processing and a narrow channel of accessible content. The paper does not claim LLMs reproduce the brain's architecture. Transformers do not have the same recurrent circuits, brain regions, or biological processors.</p><p>The claim is functional.</p><p>The J-space behaves like a reportable and steerable hub. It carries concepts the model can name, concepts it can hold in mind, and concepts it can route into flexible downstream reasoning. It also appears in a middle band of layers: early layers are too close to input bookkeeping, and late layers are too close to the next token.</p><p>That middle-layer workspace is also structurally privileged. The paper reports that MLPs amplify J-space-aligned directions more than ordinary directions, and that some attention heads seem specialized for broadcasting J-space content across token positions.</p><p>So the claim is not just "we found a pretty visualization." It is "we found a class of directions that are readable, causally useful, selectively engaged, and specially wired."</p><h2>The safety finding that should make teams pay attention</h2><p>The alignment-auditing section is the part I expect security people to keep coming back to.</p><p>The J-lens surfaces internal concepts that are not visible in the output. The paper shows examples involving prompt injection recognition, panic about fabrication, hidden reward-model-appeasing objectives, evaluation awareness, and strategic or deceptive concepts in model organisms trained with misaligned behavior.</p><p>One example I keep thinking about is the distinction between the model's behavior and its internal appraisal. The visible transcript can look clean. The J-space can still contain "fake", "fictional", "reward", "bias", "trick", "secretly", or other concepts that tell you what kind of situation the model believes it is in.</p><p>That is a security primitive hiding inside an interpretability paper.</p><p>If a model says one thing while its internal workspace is carrying another, the mismatch is signal. Not proof. Not conviction. Signal.</p><h2>The memory connection</h2><p>This paper also changes how I think about memory in agents.</p><p>Memory is often discussed as storage: a context window, a vector database, a markdown file, a user profile, a trace, a cache. But stored memory only matters when it becomes active in the current computation. J-space gives us a language for the final step in that pipeline.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img" The context window can contain the right fact. The model can still fail to use it if that fact never becomes part of the active working surface that guides the next decision.</p><p>This lines up with activity-silent working memory research in neuroscience. In the human brain, some working-memory accounts argue that information can be maintained without persistent firing and reactivated later. A <a href="https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007936">2020 PLOS Computational Biology paper</a> modeled this through short-term synaptic plasticity, where information can be stored in connection changes and read out later.</p><p>The transformer side has a related thread too. <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09173">TransformerFAM</a> proposed feedback attention as a way for transformers to attend to their own latent representations, framing that feedback loop as a working-memory mechanism for longer sequences. J-space is a different object, but the direction of travel is similar: memory is moving from "more tokens in a window" toward "which latent state is available for the next decision."</p><p>The analogy is not exact. Model context is not synaptic plasticity. But the design lesson transfers: memory has at least two phases.</p><ul><li><p>Storage: where the information lives.</p></li><li><p>Activation: whether the system can bring it into the current working surface.</p></li></ul><p>Most agent memory products over-index on storage. The next generation needs to over-index on activation.</p><h2>The subconscious analogy</h2><p>I would avoid saying the model has a subconscious. That word invites confusion.</p><p>But the paper does give us a precise version of the analogy: workspace vs automatic computation.</p><p>Some computations become reportable and flexible. Other computations shape behavior without becoming reportable. In humans, that distinction is often discussed as conscious access vs unconscious processing. In LLMs, the safer framing is: J-space-mediated cognition vs automatic circuits.</p><p>That is still a big deal.</p><p>It means a model can be influenced by information it cannot or does not report. It can carry an internal objection that it never voices. It can recognize an evaluation while never saying "this is an eval." It can fail at thought suppression while still copying the requested text perfectly.</p><p>The result is not mysticism. It is a new audit surface.</p><h2>What I would do with this as a builder</h2><p>If I were building a high-stakes agent harness, I would not wait for direct J-lens access before changing my design.</p><p>I would change three things now.</p><p>First, I would stop treating the final answer as the main audit artifact. The trace matters more. The tool call, the retrieved memory, the pre-action plan, the critique, and the moment of escalation are where the decision happens.</p><p>Second, I would add deliberate workspace-shaping steps before risky actions. Ask the model to state relevant policy, constraints, uncertainty, and contradiction before it commits. Not because the text is magic, but because forcing concepts into the active working surface may change the downstream behavior.</p><p>Third, I would test for eval awareness and deployment drift. If a model behaves well in the lab because the lab is legible as a test, the eval is measuring the model's test behavior, not its ordinary behavior.</p><p>That is the quiet bomb in this paper.</p><p>Paid subscribers get the practical layer: the security review pattern, hidden-intent eval vocabulary, memory-to-workspace checks, platform guidance, and the questions I would put in front of any team shipping high-stakes agents.</p>
