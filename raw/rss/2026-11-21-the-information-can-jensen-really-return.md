# Can Jensen Really Return?

<p><b>Welcome, Weekenders!&nbsp;</b></p><p>Jensen Huang wants a mulligan, and a good number of people seem keen to let him have one. </p><p>On Tuesday, just days after the longtime Nvidia CEO resigned the job, Huang said in a statement <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/briefings/jensen-huang-would-honored-return-ceo-board-asks?rc=sslhyj">he’d be open to taking back</a> the job. Depending on your perspective, that suggestion is akin to letting a skunked dog back into bed—or a veritable Second Coming. For now, Nvidia’s board is holding firm and <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/newsletters/the-briefing/nvidias-board-rebuffs-jensen?rc=sslhyj">has continued to insist</a> it won’t rehire Huang.&nbsp;</p><p>One of the most prominent calls for Huang’s return came on Friday, with President Donald Trump saying he thought Nvidia should give Huang his job back. “We’re a country of second chances,” Trump said. “He’s a man … who made some bad decisions.” (Surely a situation Trump himself could understand.)&nbsp;</p><p>In addition to Trump, a good number of industry pooh-bahs have continued to voice support for their fallen comrade and advocating for Huang was definitely a major topic on this week’s very busy conference circuit.&nbsp;</p><p>That support came in both implicit and explicit terms. At the New York Times’ DealBook Summit, Elon Musk <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/live/2026/11/18/business/dealbook-summit-news">was blunt</a>, saying “there’s no one who can really replace” Huang. At the TomorrowX conference, former Uber CEO Travis Kalanick and Musk groupie Antonio Gracias <a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/travis-kalanick-jensen-huang-nvidia-hq-employee-walkout-2026-11">donned</a> black leather jackets to show their solidarity. And during a Semafor shindig in San Francisco, Sam Altman <a href="https://sfstandard.com/2026/11/16/jensen-huang-semafor-silicon-valley-conference/">described</a> Huang as a personal hero, Jeff Bezos <a href="https://apnews.com/article/nvidia-jensen-huang-tech-ceo-reactions-063310aae44182e67e55c04c5b1e29e4">said</a> he was a “remarkable guy” and investor Bill Ackman labeled him as “one of the most iconic CEOs of all time.”&nbsp;</p><p>On X, Ackman <a href="https://x.com/BillAckman/status/2123101979375235386">has been theorizing</a> about how Huang could actually return. Ackman (and <a href="https://x.com/garrytan/status/2122910329537728747">venture capitalist Garry Tan</a>) has been trumpeting a roundabout plan that involves getting together a 15% ownership block of Nvidia to call a special shareholder meeting and then … vote out the anti-Huang board members … and swing the board to a pro-Huang majority? It’s somewhat unclear. (Apparently, Huang still <a href="https://www.semafor.com/article/11/18/2026/nvidias-board-divided-over-calls-for-huangs-return">has some allies</a> on the board; remember, we told you that the board <a href="https://www.theinformation.com/articles/sex-lies-ai-chips-jensen-huangs-last-days-nvidia-ceo?rc=sslhyj">hadn’t initially wanted</a> to act at all.)</p><p>“Ackman’s idea is somewhere between extremely unlikely and remote,” said Jeffrey Gordon, a Columbia University law professor and former Treasury Department attorney. (Or, in other words, as likely as defrosting a 20-pound Thanksgiving turkey overnight.) The proposal does seem to assume Huang supporters could really rile up Wall Street institutions with major Nvidia stakes, such as BlackRock, and get them to go activist—not a certain thing at all. It also conveniently ignores how difficult the math for a board-ousting vote looks when factoring in how much institutional money is held in passive-investing funds, which will never throw support behind an insurrection.&nbsp;</p><p>Rather than a board coup, Huang’s clearest route to returning would be if the board caves and changes its mind. Sure, it might, eventually. But we have to get through the many, many inquiries into Huang and Nvidia that have popped up and who knows what else we’ll learn in the meantime, especially since there are a lotttt<i> </i>of people newly determined to peer into every Nvidia crevice: journalists galore, <a href="https://www.wsj.com/business/sec-is-probing-nvidia-amid-departure-of-ceo-jensen-huang-ee7643f8">the SEC</a>, <a href="https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/joint-statement-department-commerce-and-department-justice-megaspeed-investigation">the Commerce and Justice Departments</a>, a&nbsp;<a href="https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/jensen-huang-nvidia-house-committee-return-ceo-testimony/">trio of Congressional committees</a> and possibly <a href="https://apnews.com/article/china-denies-spy-jensen-huang-nvidia-3345b9f05c54d58909e88f6dcb9c1cd8">the FBI</a>.</p><p>The Huang situation keeps getting likened to Altman’s ouster from OpenAI in 2023, which obviously ended with Altman’s return. I don’t think the comparison holds up. Most obviously, OpenAI was a private company—its board had more freedom to change its mind. And as Gordon pointed out to me, Altman’s reinstatement came after a true mass rebellion of OpenAI staff: roughly 745 out of 770 employees <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20250805150715/https://www.axios.com/2023/11/21/openai-sam-altman-microsoft-satya-nadella">signed a letter </a>threatening to go with Altman to Microsoft, more than 95% of the company. Now, a letter supporting Huang’s reinstatement is floating around the internet, and it purports to have signatures from 10,263 Nvidia employees. Even if they were all legit signatures, they represent only a quarter of the company’s workforce.Maybe most Nvidians, as they call themselves, actually didn’t like Huang’s rampant humiliation tactics, which reportedly <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/20/technology/jensen-huang-nvidia-culture.html">once included a threat </a>to scalp someone and piss on their brain. Maybe most of them are comfortable living without him. href="https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLXX1zP60qUCywgtxQOBf8KKt2KmraCOi4">Stories of Art</a>”</b></p><p>After weeks like the ones we’ve just had, I like to make it a point to visit a museum and spend some time staring at ancient, beautiful things—a chance to relish the creative ingenuity of humans who never once heard the phrase “forward-deployed engineer.”</p><p>Truthfully, though, the tech action has lately been so fast-paced that I still haven’t gotten to make one of those pilgrimages, so I’ve been counterprogramming my brain with a binge through “Stories of Art,” a podcast about, yes, art—from Renaissance Florence to Basquiat’s lower Manhattan loft—that launched earlier in the year. (Apple recently declared it one of 2026’s best.) The hosts are a pair of amiable Brits, art historian James Fox and art critic Alastair Sooke, who take a cheerful, conversational approach to what might otherwise be dry recountings. In effect, they are to Renoir what “The Rest Is History” lads are to the Romans. (Given the subject matter of “Stories of Art”—namely, art—I suggest you watch the podcast’s video version when you can. While Fox and Sooke are quite adept at describing the artworks they mention, it’s nice to actually see what they’re talking about.)&nbsp;</p><p>Nearly all the artists who get mentioned achieved their fame through real entrepreneurial verve, challenging the entrenched status quo. (Sounds like a familiar motivation, right?) Take Claude Monet and the birth of Impressionism: The movement traces its origins to a decision in 1874 by Monet and a group of other artists to refuse to exhibit their work at an established gallery. Instead, they formed their own company (yes, founder mode Monet!) and then set up their own Paris exhibition space that featured newfangled technology, such as elevators. Many of you will enjoy one of the most recent episodes on James Whistler, a fellow who took a real dim view of journalists and pursued a high-profile libel lawsuit against one critic. A warning about how that turned out for Whistler: He did triumph in court. He then promptly went bankrupt.—<i>Abram Brown&nbsp;</i></p><p><b>Reading: “<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FZSQYNB8?lv=shuf&amp;channelId=500&amp;plpRedirect=mhFallback">Fruit Fly</a>” by Josh Silver</b></p><p>In suburban north London, author Mallory Maddox seems to have a rich, full life: a handsome Netflix-executive husband, a townhome with $2,000 armchairs and dusky-pink walls and a literary world standing as a “modern-day Virginia Woolf,” as one reviewer has christened her. Yet appearances are almost always deceiving in “Fruit Fly,” a spry, tart satire of sex and 21st century storytelling: Mallory’s relationship with hubbie Ronan has turned stiff and chilly, and as much as he thinks about the bedroom, it’s to insist that it’s time for kids. (“I haven’t had an orgasm in years,” she recalls in an ongoing inner monologue.) Professionally, Mallory is dogged by “a shadowy, persisting darkness”—a profound case of writer’s block. To fill the time, she moonlights as an online therapist and hunts exhaustively for those expensive home furnishings.</p><p>Desperate for writerly inspiration, she peruses Reddit—uh oh—and takes to heart another author’s comment about the state of the publishing industry: “go dark, go gay.” She downloads Grindr, sets up an account with a few of her husband’s nudes and scores an invite to an orgy at a house down the street. She never actually sets foot inside but nevertheless makes the acquaintance of Leo, an attractive but homeless and drug-addicted gay man who sells himself to other men. (As the novel shifts perspectives, Leo contributes his own inner thoughts, which are often mordant and felonious. “Old women carry cash. Everyone knows that,” he thinks, snatching a purse. “Someone should tell them to stop.”) Mallory offers to counsel Leo—pro bono, bless her—and begins writing a novel that lightly fictionalizes his frayed life. For a time, he believes she’s just a daffy do-gooder. (“This is weird,” Leo thinks as she leads him through a silent-breathing exercise. “This is very fucking weird, but I guess I’ve done weirder things.”) And her publishers absolutely adore what she is finally putting on the page. As one editor remarks: “It’s so bleak—but also somehow so funny?” Much the same can be said about “Fruit Fly” itself.—<i>A.B.</i></p><p><b>Watching: “<a href="https://tv.apple.com/us/movie/the-weight/umc.cmc.1tcg4kjcm3liehzk4et1sh7lp">The Weight</a>”</b></p><p>President Franklin Roosevelt attempted to govern Depression-era America through no small number of novel schemes: A good many of them are quite famous, such as Social Security. A few have disappeared into history, including Roosevelt’s 1933 executive order that banned gold hoarding, forcing Americans to turn over large stockpiles of the precious metal. (No one could keep more than $100 worth.) That presidential decision is the inciting force in “The Weight,” a tense wilderness thriller starring Ethan Hawke as Murphy, leader of a group of convicts hired by their jailer (Russell Crowe) to heist a gold mine’s reserves. It’s part of a plan that’s complex in the same way that “Goldfinger” is not actually a film about robbing Fort Knox.&nbsp;</p><p>Often, heist films are fabulous capers, with champagne, tuxedos and bon mots. “The Weight” very much is not, depicting a bloodied-knuckled trek across the Oregon backwoods, with Murphy and his thieves weighed down, literally, by their bullion-filled backpacks and, metaphorically, by their past misdeeds and inner demons. Still, Murphy is well incentivized to make it through. In a true miscarriage of justice, he has been separated from his plucky daughter, Penny (Avy Berry); part of his reward for orchestrating the robbery is early release, which will let him reclaim her. “The Weight” does take a moment to get going. Then it really rips, with a pair of memorable fight scenes that are a real credit to both the film’s editing and cinematography.—<i>A.B.</i></p>
