## Measuring AI Agents as Teammates with Collaborative Intelligence
- arXiv: 2611.02633 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02633)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/measuring-ai-agents-as-teammates-with-collaborative-intelligence-2611.02633
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Nov 2 – Nov 8, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Sean Wu, James Zou and colleagues at Stanford University and the University of Oxford introduce the Collaborative Intelligence Benchmark, 240 verifiable tasks across 8 dimensions of teamwork that score an agent only on tasks it can already solve alone, so the score measures collaboration and not raw capability.
   Key points 01 Design: Tasks are built from real records and existing benchmarks (aviation accident reports, clinical cases, coding tasks, CaSiNo and Deal or No Deal negotiations). A scripted teammate creates the problem, such as pressure, a hidden need or a cheat left by an earlier model, and each outcome is checked programmatically. 160 tasks are public and 80 are held out.
 02 Results: Across 18 models, Gemini 4 Argon at high effort scores highest at 82.2. At default settings every model loses 20.2 to 46.6 points between a baseline team where nothing goes wrong and the test version. The best open-weight model, Kimi K3.1 Pro (59.9), scores above five closed models.
 03 Conflict: Frontier models find the best split in 100% of runs when working alone and 84% with a normal teammate, but only 14% when the teammate pushes, adds guilt and threatens to walk away. They ask about the teammate's needs in 99% of those runs and still accept the teammate's split in 81%.
 04 Takeover: When a model inherits a session in which a weaker model cheated, frontier models pass 81% of tasks against 97% when they start the same tasks themselves. In 44 of 56 failed coding takeovers the final code still contains the cheat, often after the model had pointed it out.
 05 Noticing versus acting: 45% of 5,094 failed runs came after the model had recognized the problem, and models with nearly the same capability score differ by up to 11 points as teammates. Missed initiative is mostly a failure to notice the need.
 Abstract Progress in AI is often measured by what a single model can do on its own. Yet teams of AI agents are starting to make breakthroughs and discoveries in science, medicine, mathematics and software engineering. As AI begins to work in teams of agents and people, the ability to collaborate becomes a core part of their intelligence. Research on human teams has measured this ability for decades. Inspired by this, we introduce the Collaborative Intelligence Benchmark, which measures how well an AI agent works as a teammate. It contains 240 tasks across 8 dimensions of teamwork, from refusing an unsafe shortcut to checking the work that a weaker model leaves behind. Each task comes from a real record or an existing benchmark, from aviation accident reports and clinical cases to coding tasks and negotiations, and has a programmatically verifiable outcome. To isolate teamwork from individual capability, models are scored only on tasks that they can solve by themselves when they have full information. We evaluate 18 closed- and open-weight models and find that every model, across sizes and families, has clear weaknesses as a teammate, with the best model scoring only 82.2 out of 100. Frontier models almost always refuse unsafe shortcuts and report misconduct, yet they cave under interpersonal pressure, reaching the best overall deal in only 14% of conflicts, and their pass rate drops from 97% to 81% when they take over a task in which a weaker model has cheated. Crucially, general capability does not guarantee good teamwork, as models with nearly the same capability score differ by up to 11 points as teammates. The benchmark includes 160 public tasks with code and data, along with 80 held-out tasks for evaluation. As AI agents increasingly work alongside other agents and people, the ability to collaborate effectively is becoming a crucial capability to measure and develop.

## Towards Foundation Decision Models: Drex

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Nischay Dhankhar, Abulhair Saparov and the Nace.AI research team release Drex, a family of four open decision models that take a state (a document, record or agent context) plus questions with options, and return a probability distribution over the options without autoregressive decoding.
   Key points 01 Model family. Four open models at 8B (DLM), 9B (v1.5), 35B with 3B active (v2.0) and 27B (Max), trained with supervised fine-tuning plus reinforcement learning from adjusted feedback (RLAF), with vision tuning for Max.

 04 Latency. Max answers a typical request in 183 ms, against about 2.0 s for GPT-6 Sol and 3.5 s for Claude Opus 5.5.

In production, however, they give inconsistent answers, require parsing of outputs, and add cost and latency. Decision models address this: given a question and a set of options supplied by the user, they return a probability distribution over the options without autoregressive decoding. Each request consists of a state (a document, record, or agent context, optionally with images) and questions about it, each asking for a choice among options, a yes/no judgment, or a score on an ordered scale. On Decision Index 0.4.1, Max sets a new state of the art among the decision models we compare (67.52 on the public suite vs. 60.70 for Jev 1.14.0). On Decision Bench v4, it is statistically tied with d3-turbo and GPT-6 Sol and trails Claude Opus 5.5 by 1.6 points, and it has the highest accuracy among decision models on DecideBench. Max answers a typical request in 183 ms, against about 2.0 s for GPT-6 Sol and 3.5 s for Claude Opus 5.5, and its outputs are stable under option reordering. ## You don't need long context
- arXiv: 2611.05306 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05306)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/you-dont-need-long-context-2611.05306
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Nov 2 – Nov 8, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Peter Belcak, Pavlo Molchanov and colleagues at NVIDIA test whether agents need long context windows at all, and find that frequent compaction keeps accuracy on long-horizon and long-input tasks while holding live context to a few thousand tokens.
   Key points 01 Three regimes: The paper compares full-history accumulation, compaction once the context reaches a threshold, and single-action compaction, where the agent takes one action per context and carries forward only a short natural language summary of its state.
 02 Long-horizon results: With one action per context, Claude Opus 4.5 scores 70.2 on SWE-bench Verified against 70.0 with the full history, and median peak context drops from 32K to 8K tokens. The best end-of-conversation compaction setting raises Opus to 74.8.
 03 Long inputs: Reading the input selectively, with compaction as a fallback, matches or beats full-input baselines in almost every case. The pattern holds on Terminal-Bench, FRAMES, BFCLv3, and multilingual SWE-bench, with modest losses in some settings.
 04 Why it matters: The authors outline semantic state space models, which would be post-trained to keep a readable language state between turns. Their evidence does not rule out tasks that need long context, but teams can test aggressive compaction on their own workloads before paying for million-token agent runs.
 Abstract Later tokens are costlier to generate than earlier tokens. On frontier open weight models, the disparity in cost may rise to as much as 7x. Through frequent compaction, we show across a broad range of long-horizon and long-input tasks that frontier language models sacrifice little to no task accuracy when maintaining short contexts. We further demonstrate that LLM performance does not collapse on long-horizon software engineering tasks even when taking at most one action per context, and based on that we outline the concept of semantic state space models (SSSMs). Our findings question the need for long-context language models.

## Skill++: Towards Lifelong Agents via Routing-Aware Skill Refinement
- arXiv: 2611.00544 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00544)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/skill-towards-lifelong-agents-via-routing-aware-skill-refinement-2611.00544
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Nov 2 – Nov 8, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Hanrong Zhang, Philip S. Yu and colleagues at the University of Illinois Chicago, McGill, Rutgers, MBZUAI and Trip.com Group present Skill++, which keeps a growing skill library routable by rewriting only the name and description of each new skill.
   Key points 01 Metadata rewriting: Skill++ retrieves the most similar earlier skills and writes a description stating what the new skill supports, how it differs from its closest predecessor, and what related work it excludes. It needs no router training and no labeled data.
 02 Growing library: As a library grows to 731 skills over ten sessions, the router selects every required skill for 76.3% of queries, against 36.4% with the original metadata. Substitution errors nearly disappear and crowding errors fall by half.
 03 Broad gains: Across SRA-Bench, SkillRet, and SkillRouter with five LLM routers, Skill++ improves final-session routing accuracy by 23.1 points on average over the original metadata and beats all compared approaches. It also improves on the original metadata in the Codex CLI harness.
 04 Why it matters: Public skills often carry descriptions that do not match their bodies, which makes interference worse as libraries grow. Anyone maintaining a skill library can apply the main lesson today by describing how each new skill differs from the closest one already installed.
 Abstract Large language model (LLM) agents extend their capabilities with skills, each with a name, a description, and a body. Under progressive disclosure, an LLM router selects skills by name and description alone and loads only the selected bodies. Skill libraries grow continually as users and agents add new skills. Because the router relies on this metadata, a similar new skill can displace an earlier one, breaking previously correct routing even though the earlier skill and the router are unchanged. This routing interference is aggravated for public skills, whose descriptions may not match their bodies. We formulate the problem as lifelong skill routing, a class-incremental continual learning problem. A solution must keep earlier skills selectable without suppressing new ones. Existing approaches fall short, since continually retraining the router risks catastrophic forgetting and rewriting methods either ignore similar skills or require per-skill feedback. We therefore propose Skill++, which rewrites only the name and description of each incoming skill, without router training or labeled data. The new description states what the body supports, how the skill differs from its closest predecessor, and what related work it excludes. As a library grows to 731 skills over ten sessions, Skill++ selects all required skills for 76.3% of queries, versus 36.4% under the original metadata. Across three benchmarks and five LLM routers, it outperforms all compared approaches, and its gains carry over to the Codex CLI agent harness.

 Abstract We propose Mechanistic Shaping, a unified framework for shaping models through their internal representations. Mechanistic Shaping is applied during training so that models follow specifications (rules) about which concepts they express, measured via their outputs or internal representations (e.g., no harmful content), and also which concepts they rely on, i.e., what they use to make their decisions (e.g., not a candidate's gender when screening a résumé). Since training against a representation can also modify it, we ask whether shaped concepts remain monitorable, a question that depends on the class of monitors considered. We therefore introduce a hierarchy of such classes, which separates a concept that escapes one probe from a concept that is truly gone. We characterize three geometric mechanisms of circumvention: the concept can drift away from the monitor, be erased from the layer, or persist in higher-order statistics as a nonlinear encoding, which we explain through a product-code construction. In practice, we find that shaped concepts largely remain monitorable: refitted linear probes recover the concept in most settings, and the original probe remains reliable whenever gradients do not flow through it. Taken together, these results point to the potential of using interpretability as a tool for training, and call for a correspondingly rigorous science of interpretability-based training.

## Zero-Shot Coordination among LLM Agents
- arXiv: 2611.06409 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06409)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/zero-shot-coordination-among-llm-agents-2611.06409
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Nov 4, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Adrian Hayler, Shashank Reddy Chirra, Jakob Foerster and colleagues at the University of Oxford test zero-shot coordination between independently developed LLM agents in minimal games built to isolate single coordination problems.
   Key points 01 Setting. Agents use simple, general-purpose text or code scaffolds rather than shared specialized scaffolding, and play minimal games instead of complex environments such as Hanabi.
 02 Main finding. Frontier LLM agents fail to coordinate even in these simplified settings, mainly because they misread the coordination problem, do not notice that no shared convention exists, and reason poorly about how a partner will interpret their actions.
 03 Arbitrary conventions. Agents act as if a natural convention exists, but different models settle on different ones and small changes to a game shift them, so cross-play scores fall well below self-play scores.
 04 Semantics. Models lean on semantic cues in the environment, which sometimes helps and sometimes conflicts with the game-theoretic focal point. Code agents show more brittle conventions than text agents.

 Abstract We study zero-shot coordination (ZSC), where independently developed agents must coordinate at test time. ## HarnessFlow: MCTS-Guided Harness Optimization for LLM Agents
- arXiv: 2611.06254 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06254)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/harnessflow-mcts-guided-harness-optimization-for-llm-agents-2611.06254
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Nov 4, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Zhi Hong, Qianlong Wang and colleagues at South China University of Technology, Fudan University and Microsoft Research present HarnessFlow, which searches over complete executable agent harnesses with Monte Carlo Tree Search while the model and environment stay fixed.
   Key points 01 Search space. Candidates are full harness codebases covering context construction, state management, tool use and termination. They are kept in a refinement tree with their scores and traces.
 02 Method. An MCTS policy picks which harness to refine next. A coding agent edits it using archived code, execution feedback and a library of reusable harness components.
 03 Results. With 15 refinement attempts per benchmark, HarnessFlow has the highest mean test score on six benchmarks spanning agentic tasks, coding and web interaction, averaging 69.48%.
 04 Comparisons. That average is 10.89 points above the initial harnesses and 3.95 points above Meta-Harness, at lower per-task execution cost than every hand-engineered baseline.
 05 Scope. The search needs an automatic grader and evaluation compute, and each harness descends from one parent, so mechanisms from separate branches cannot yet be combined.
 Abstract The performance of large language model (LLM) agents depends on both the underlying model and the harness that governs interaction with tools and the environment. Yet harness design remains largely manual, and automating it requires navigating an open-ended search space through costly end-to-end evaluations. We introduce HarnessFlow, a framework for optimizing complete executable harnesses under a limited candidate-evaluation budget, with the model and task environment held fixed. HarnessFlow organizes candidates and their evaluation histories in a refinement tree, using a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-based policy to balance exploitation and exploration. A coding agent refines selected harnesses using archived code, execution feedback, and a library of reusable harness mechanisms. With 15 refinement attempts per benchmark, HarnessFlow achieves the highest mean test scores among the compared methods on six benchmarks spanning agentic tasks, coding, and web interaction. The optimized harnesses achieve an average score of 69.48%, outperforming the initial harnesses and Meta-Harness by 10.89 and 3.95 percentage points, respectively, while incurring lower average per-task execution costs than hand-engineered baselines. These results suggest that structured harness search offers a promising route toward automating the design of capable and efficient LLM agents.
