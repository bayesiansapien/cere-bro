## Are Self-Evolving Agents Continual Learners?
- arXiv: 2610.25858 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25858)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/are-self-evolving-agents-continual-learners-2610.25858
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 19 – Oct 25, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Xiaoyin Chen (Mila and Université de Montréal, work done as a student researcher at Google DeepMind) and colleagues test whether nonparametric self-evolving agents, which rewrite prompts, memory and skills while weights stay frozen, behave as continual learners on drifting task streams.
   Key points 01 Drifting streams: The study builds four task streams from BIRD, BFCL, and ScienceWorld that shift in topic, workflow, or domain. It evaluates ReasoningBank, GEPA, and Meta-Harness, covering both experience retrieval and self-modification.
 02 Plasticity costs stability: Self-evolution improves aggregate performance on most streams, but higher plasticity consistently comes with more forgetting. Across all 12 method and scenario pairs, plasticity and stability show a correlation of r = -0.72.
 03 Two failure mechanisms: Self-modifying methods apply global edits to a shared prompt or workspace, which interferes with unseen tasks and overwrites older knowledge as the stream shifts. Similarity-based memory retrieval keeps updates more local, but it still retrieves memories from other domains and suffers collisions when similar queries need conflicting workflows.
 04 Why it matters: The authors conclude that current self-evolving agents remain incomplete continual learners. Static benchmarks hide both problems, so teams building self-improving agents should test them on streams that change over time.
 Abstract Large language model agents are often deployed in environments where new knowledge, tools, and workflows keep arriving. Such an agent is therefore expected to adapt continually, learning new tasks as they arrive without forgetting earlier ones. This resembles the continual learning problem, but at a pace that conventional gradient updates cannot sustain. Nonparametric self-evolving agents are a natural alternative: they adapt by rewriting their own harness, such as prompts, memory, and skills, from task feedback while the model weights stay frozen. We study whether such agents are effective continual learners across four drifting task streams that shift in topic, workflow, or domain (built from BIRD, BFCL, and ScienceWorld). Evaluating three representative methods spanning experience retrieval and self-modification (ReasoningBank, GEPA, and Meta-Harness), we find that although self-evolution improves aggregate performance on most streams, higher plasticity consistently comes at the cost of stability. Specifically, self-modifying methods apply global edits to a shared prompt or workspace, interfering with unseen tasks and overwriting earlier knowledge as the stream shifts. Conversely, similarity-based memory retrieval localizes updates across distant domains, but still retrieves foreign memories on unseen tasks and suffers from retrieval collision when similar queries require conflicting workflows. These findings show that current self-evolving agents remain incomplete continual learners.
```

## DiscoveryRSI: Recursively Learning to Discover Valuable Hypotheses
- arXiv: 2610.23839 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23839)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/discoveryrsi-recursively-learning-to-discover-valuable-hypotheses-2610.23839
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 19 – Oct 25, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Jianyuan Zhong, Chenwei Wu, Zhaochen Yu, James Zou, Ling Yang and colleagues (PhAI Labs, CUHK, Stanford, Princeton, Michigan and others) introduce DiscoveryRSI, a framework that develops research hypothesis trees by multi-agent evolutionary search and recursively learns from the investigations it runs.
   Key points 01 Hypothesis trees: The system grows trees of hypotheses with multi-agent evolutionary search and compiles the selected investigations into executable Harbor tasks. The authors define a valuable hypothesis as an evidence-grounded claim about a consequential uncertainty, paired with a feasible investigation, explicit decision criteria, and alternative explanations.
 02 Three learning mechanisms: Hypothesis credit optimization attributes scientific assessments to the research contributions behind them. Harness replay finds productive strategies and recurring failures in the research workflow, and rediscovery applies accepted improvements to new investigations.
 03 Results: Automated hypothesis quality rises from 4.86 for the base model to 5.39 after two rounds of self-improvement, with the largest gains in falsifiability and data resolvability. The 27B model's hypotheses match research published after its training cutoff 6.3% of the time, compared with 2.5% for GPT-6 and 2.3% for Claude Opus 5.
 04 Why it matters: Preliminary expert review rates DiscoveryRSI-27B above GPT-6 Luna on hypothesis importance and on whether claims are plausible and evidence-bounded. The paper also releases DFM-Bench for scoring hypotheses, revisions, and whole research runs.
 Abstract Scientific discovery requires developing worthwhile hypotheses into practical investigations, yet learning from this evolving research process remains challenging. We introduce an operational definition of valuable and testable research hypotheses: evidence-grounded claims about consequential uncertainties, coupled with feasible investigations, explicit decision criteria, and alternative explanations. Our DiscoveryRSI framework develops hypothesis trees through multi-agent evolutionary search, compiles selected investigations into executable Harbor tasks, and recursively learns through three mechanisms: hypothesis credit optimization attributes scientific assessments to documented research contributions; harness replay identifies productive strategies and recurring failures to refine research workflows; and rediscovery applies accepted improvements to new investigations, generating further experience for subsequent learning. We also introduce DFM-Bench, which provides comprehensive assessment of hypothesis quality and cumulative research progress at the levels of hypotheses, revisions, development paths and research runs. Across four life-science domains, automated evaluation shows that hypothesis quality rises from 4.86 for the base model to 5.39 after two rounds of self-improvement, with the large gains in falsifiability and data resolvability. We further validate hypothesis quality through expert review, comparing our DiscoveryRSI-27B with other research agents. Preliminary human assessments show that DiscoveryRSI-27B scores higher than GPT-6 Luna on hypothesis importance and whether claims are plausible and evidence-bounded. DiscoveryRSI-27B achieved a 6.3% rate of hypothesis correspondence with research published after the model cutoff, largely outperform GPT-6 with 2.5% and Claude Opus 5 with 2.3%, while delivering greater human-rated improvement along research paths.

## JevAgentHarness: Benchmarking, Deploying and Training Decision Models That Share Control with LLMs
- arXiv: 2610.21412 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/jevagentharness-benchmarking-deploying-and-training-decision-models-that-share-c-2610.21412
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 19 – Oct 25, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Kunlun Zhu, Tao Feng and colleagues at UIUC (with Jiaxuan You and Heng Ji) test whether an agent harness can hand routine multiple-choice decisions to a fast decision model such as Jev, and build a harness and an open 4B model that do so with little loss of task success.
   Key points 01 AgentDecisionBench: The benchmark tests decision models from isolated questions up to control of a running agent. The model most accurate on isolated questions solved only 69.5% of agent tasks, and delegating tool choices without a fitted confidence threshold lowered success by 19.0 points.
 02 Fitted thresholds: JevAgentHarness sends each routine choice to the decision model and uses its answer only when confidence clears a threshold fitted on the agent's own trajectories. Below the threshold, the LLM decides. With fitted thresholds, tool and argument cascades cut cost by 14.2% without changing the outcome of any of 500 tasks.
 03 AgentDecision-4B: Existing decision models never see the states an agent reaches after its own mistakes, so the authors train an open 4B model on those states with Jev's answers as labels. On tasks unlike its training data, the agent solved 91.5% with AgentDecision-4B and 82.0% with Jev.
 04 Why it matters: On tasks like their training data, 4B models trained this way made agents 81.1% to 89.0% cheaper and up to 2.5x faster while staying within 3 points of task success.
 Abstract Large language model (LLM) agents now fix software, operate computers and serve customers, but their harnesses, the programs that run them, call a large LLM at every step, which makes long tasks slow and costly. Most of these calls end in a multiple-choice decision, such as which tool to call next. Decision models such as Jev answer these questions directly, at a fraction of an LLM call's time and price. We ask how a harness can delegate these decisions to a decision model with little loss of success. To evaluate decision models in agentic tasks, we build AgentDecisionBench, which tests them from isolated questions up to control of a running agent, on public benchmarks and our DecisionGym environment. The decision model most accurate on isolated questions solved only 69.5% of agent tasks, and without a fitted confidence threshold, delegating tool choices lowered success by 19.0 points. Building on these findings, we then design JevAgentHarness, which fits this threshold for each choice on the agent's own trajectories and lets the LLM decide below it. Because existing decision models never see the states an agent reaches after its own mistakes, we train AgentDecision-4B, an open 4B model, on such states with Jev's answers as labels. On tasks unlike its training data, the agent solved 91.5% of them with AgentDecision-4B and 82.0% with Jev. On tasks like their training data, 4B models trained this way made agents 81.1-89.0% cheaper and up to 2.5 times faster, without losing 3 or more points of task success. The models, data, and harness will be released at https://github.com/JevAgentHarness/JevAgentHarness.

## Terminal-RSI: Recursive Self-Improving Terminal Agents via Recursive Self-Rewrite and Policy-Refine
- arXiv: 2610.22192 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22192)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/terminal-rsi-recursive-self-improving-terminal-agents-via-recursive-self-rewrite-2610.22192
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 19 – Oct 25, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Junyao Yang, Zongxia Li, Yucheng Shi and colleagues (Tencent Hy with university co-authors) train a 27B terminal agent through five alternating stages of verified-trajectory rewriting and PPO refinement, raising its four-suite Terminal-Bench mean from 20.68% to 50.58%.
   Key points 01 Two alternating phases. Recursive Self-Rewrite (RSR) fine-tunes on rewritten verified trajectories; Recursive Policy-Refine (RPR) runs PPO with a critic inherited from the previous stage. The sequence Base, RSR-1, RPR-1, RSR-2, RPR-2 starts from Qwen3.8-27B.
 02 Results. Terminal-RSI-27B scores 86.52% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, 79.00% on Terminal-Bench Hard, 16.95% on 3.0 and 19.84% on 4.0, where Gemini 3.8 Flash scores 19.70% in the paper's reference table. The four-suite mean is 29.90 points above the base model and 16.33 above RSR-1.
 03 Reward accounting. In 4,352 audited trajectories, failed runs received 50.09% of the dense reward mass under the earlier reward, and 48 failures passed every executed check but missed the terminal gate. Success-Prioritized Reward separates strict success from bounded partial credit, and rescoring cuts the failed share to 28.72%.
 04 Rewriting stabilizes RL. RPR-1 initialized from the rewritten-trajectory stage beats direct PPO by up to 29.2 points on Terminal-Bench 2.1 at step 20.
 05 Long-horizon limits. On LHTB strict completion moves from 0/46 to 5/46, and SWR100 success from 3% to 6%, so long tasks remain mostly unsolved.
 Abstract Converting verifier outcomes into useful policy updates and partial progress into complete task resolution is crucial for terminal agents. We present Terminal-RSI, which alternates Recursive Self-Rewrite (RSR) of verified trajectories with critic-inheriting Recursive Policy-Refine (RPR). We formulate Turn-Boundary Skip-Observation GAE (TB-GAE) to retain token-specific baselines while applying decay across interaction turns. Family Dense Reward (FDR) calibrates semantic progress across task families; Success-Prioritized Reward (SPR) separates strict success from bounded partial credit. A Warm-up Critic Model, recursive critic handoff, and stage-specific learning rates coordinate the two refinement phases. The five-stage sequence from Qwen3.8-27B base model through RSR-1, RPR-1, RSR-2, and RPR-2 yields Terminal-RSI-27B: 19.84% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, 86.52% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, 16.95% on Terminal-Bench 3.0, and 79.00% on Terminal-Bench Hard. On LHTB, process reward rises from 0.21 to 0.37 and strict completion from 0/46 to 5/46; SWR100 success doubles from 3.00% to 6.00%. By recursively coupling verified trajectory rewriting with critic-inheriting policy refinement, each retained stage consolidates discovered behaviors and progressively advances the agent from partial local repairs to strict terminal verification.

## A team of Gemini 4 Argon agents beats Minecraft
- arXiv: 2610.22319 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22319)

- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 19 – Oct 25, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Maxence Faldor, Satinder Singh and Roberta Raileanu (Google DeepMind) report the first end-to-end autonomous completion of Minecraft: four Gemini 4 Argon agents, coordinating through chat and shared memory, wrote their own control programs and defeated the Ender Dragon in 27 hours and 14 minutes.
   Key points 01 Setup: Each agent controlled one player through Mineflayer and coordinated with its teammates through in-game chat and a shared memory. No one assigned roles or wrote game logic, and the agents planned, wrote, and debugged every program themselves.
 02 The run: Starting from a random world on Easy difficulty, the team wrote 2,783 programs, died 605 times, and defeated the dragon in 27 hours and 14 minutes. Researchers watched on a live dashboard and never needed to intervene.
 03 Team strategies: The agents divided the work, shared what they learned, and recovered each other's items after deaths. A fifth Observer agent watched the session for cheating or specification gaming and found none.
 04 Why it matters: Winning required keeping one plan coherent across thousands of dependent steps for more than a day, the same long-horizon coding and coordination that real software and enterprise tasks demand. The authors are clear about the limits, which include a single run, Mineflayer exposing hidden game state, Easy difficulty, and the model's prior knowledge of Minecraft.
 Abstract We placed four Gemini 4 Argon agents in a sandboxed Minecraft survival world and gave them one goal: defeat the Ender Dragon. Each agent controlled a player via an open-source library called Mineflayer and coordinated with its teammates through in-game chat and shared memory. No one assigned roles or wrote game logic. The agents planned, wrote, and debugged every program themselves. Researchers could watch the run on a live dashboard and pause or stop it at any time, but they had no cause to intervene. Starting from a randomly generated world on Easy difficulty, the team wrote 2,783 programs and defeated the dragon in 27 hours and 14 minutes, dying 605 times along the way. To our knowledge, this is the first end-to-end autonomous completion of Minecraft by AI agents, achieved using only the standard Mineflayer library without pre-scouted seeds or other game-specific tools written by humans. Winning required the agents to keep a coherent plan across thousands of dependent steps for more than a day. Complex real-world software engineering and enterprise tasks demand the same long-horizon coding and multi-agent coordination. We describe the setup, how the run unfolded, the team's strategies (dividing the work, sharing what they learned, recovering each other's items), and the limits of the result.

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Rui Melo, Andre Duarte, Carolyn Rose and colleagues (Carnegie Mellon University, University of Porto) present SESAA, in which an LLM agent starts with no rules and iteratively writes joern queries for static analysis using only feedback from a second LLM critic.
   Key points 01 Method. The critic inspects flagged and unflagged code to identify likely false alarms and suspected misses without access to ground-truth labels, and the agent revises its queries from that feedback.

## ReflectPO: Reflection-Guided Policy Optimization for Long-Horizon LLM Agents
- arXiv: 2610.30933 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30933)

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Rumei Li and Xunliang Cai (Meituan) propose ReflectPO, an RL framework that trains long-horizon tool agents to recover from their own early errors.
   Key points 01 Reflection-guided sampling. Branch differences and repeated visits in trajectories are turned into hints for error recovery during rollout.
 02 Tree credit. Sampled trajectories are organized into trees, and action-level credit comes from parent-child reward differences plus recovery gains.
 03 Result. Outperforms existing baselines in most domains of two long-horizon tool-use benchmarks and has the best average.

However, these tasks involve sequential, interdependent decisions and changes in environmental states. Early execution errors affect subsequent interaction histories and environmental states, and their effects accumulate along the decision chain. To address this challenge, we propose Reflection-Guided Policy Optimization (ReflectPO), a reinforcement learning framework for autonomous error recovery in long-horizon tasks. The second is Tree-Structured Credit Assignment. We organize sampled trajectories into trees and assign action-level credit using parent--child reward differences and recovery gains. Experiments over two long-horizon tool-use benchmarks show that ReflectPO outperforms multiple existing baselines in most domains. It also achieves the best overall performance on average. DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Ferdinand Kapl (TU Munich, Helmholtz AI) with Kaitlin Maile, Stefan Bauer and Johannes von Oswald (Google Paradigms of Intelligence) show that language models which reuse computation across depth, by growing depth during training or looping a shared block, learn more from context than a dense model of the same depth.
   Key points 01 Language-model comparison. Our analysis spans pretrained language models up to 3B parameters and controlled tasks. The experiments provide evidence for a shared inductive bias toward learning from context: grown and looped models rely more on information in context and, compared with a full-depth dense reference, acquire ICL earlier in controlled tasks and retain it more strongly when learning fixed associations in the weights is also possible. Ablations show that grown and looped models rely more strongly on induction-ranked heads for both few-shot performance and later-token prediction. ## Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3
- arXiv: 2610.31624 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31624)

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Baris Alparslan, Sandy Tanwisuth and Caleb Petterson track instrumental-convergence behavior across the post-training checkpoints of OLMo-3-7B-Think and find that RLVR does not increase it monotonically.
   Key points 01 Setup. InstrumentalEval is run on the SFT model, the DPO model, 13 intermediate RLVR checkpoints and the final RLVR model, treating safety as a trajectory instead of a single before/after score.
 02 Trajectory. The instrumental rate rises from 25.0% at SFT to 46.1% near RLVR step 200, then falls to 11.8% at the final checkpoint. Paired item-level tests and step-wise regression support the SFT-to-final reduction.

Does reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) make a model more likely to express instrumental-convergence behavior, or does this depend on the training regime, evaluation frame, and stage of post-training? - arXiv: 2610.30461 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30461)

## Network topology shapes consensus and vulnerability in LLM populations
- arXiv: 2610.31165 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31165)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/network-topology-shapes-consensus-and-vulnerability-in-llm-populations-2610.31165
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 25, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Roberto Passaro, Andrea Baronchelli, Luca Maria Aiello and colleagues (IT University of Copenhagen, City St George's University of London, UPC Barcelona) place policies extracted from four LLMs on networks of 1,000 to 100,000 agents playing the naming game and measure how topology changes consensus and vulnerability.
   Key points 01 Consensus speed. Topology changes how fast consensus forms, sometimes whether it forms and occasionally which convention wins. The sparsest networks slow consensus up to twelvefold.

Zhizhuo Kou, Yike Guo and colleagues (HKUST, Huawei Noah's Ark Lab) trace where the word wait in reasoning traces comes from, and find that models inherit it from training data rather than inventing it during RL.

## Simulated Control Evals

- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 23, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Jeffrey Olmo, David Zhang and Tyler Tracy (Redwood Research) introduce simulated control evaluations, which replace a real environment with an LLM that simulates tool responses so control evaluations can be run for a specific deployment.
   Key points 01 Setup. SCEs are validated on LinuxArena, where real execution gives ground truth, and compared with BashArena as a separate purpose-built control setting.
 02 Accuracy. For independent evaluation and deployment task sets, SCEs predict safety with 3.6 percentage points RMSE across five monitors at a 5% audit budget, versus 10.1 for BashArena.

 Abstract Control evaluations measure whether safeguards can prevent an AI model from causing undesired outcomes even when it is intentionally trying to do so. These evaluations typically use purpose-built control settings and extrapolate their results to a model's deployment. However, the effectiveness of safeguards in deployment can depend on features of environments and tasks that control settings do not represent. To broaden where control evaluations can be run, we introduce simulated control evaluations (SCEs), which use a language model to simulate tool responses in place of an environment. We evaluate SCEs on LinuxArena, where execution provides ground-truth outcomes.
