## Discovering state-of-the-art quantum circuits with large language models
- arXiv: 2610.22367 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22367)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/discovering-state-of-the-art-quantum-circuits-with-large-language-models-2610.22367
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 12 – Oct 18, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Andi Gu, Alek Kemeny, Susanne Yelin and Mikhail Lukin (Harvard) use an evolutionary loop in which LLMs propose quantum circuits and an independent evaluator verifies and scores them, and report circuits that beat the state of the art on seven of eight tasks.
   Key points 01 Evolutionary search: The search runs over circuit-generating programs with OpenEvolve, using Claude Mythos 5 to propose architectures and program rewrites. Each search starts from a hand-written task specification and a blank program, and the best designs seed the next round.
 02 Verified circuits: The evaluator checks each circuit for correctness before scoring it by Toffoli count and peak qubit count. Every reported gain is a circuit that passed this check.
 03 Results: On seven of eight tasks spanning numerical computation, chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation, and number theory, the discovered circuits use fewer Toffoli gates and fewer qubits than the best-known designs, cutting the combined cost by 4.1x to 39x. For a Reed-Solomon decoder, the search switched to the Berlekamp-Massey recurrence and lowered the Toffoli count from 5.1 million to 440,000.
 04 Why it matters: On the eighth task, the search found a new reversible architecture for prime-field modular inversion that makes divsteps, a classical algorithm previously impractical for reversible integer arithmetic, usable and improves the Toffoli-qubit trade-off. The authors suggest that some apparent costs of quantum arithmetic reflect limits of existing reversible constructions rather than of the algorithms themselves.
 Abstract Significant efforts are currently being directed toward the implementation of large-scale quantum information processors, but realizing useful quantum advantage also requires the development of efficient quantum algorithms. While for many practical problems the dominant resource costs are associated with a small set of computational primitives, optimizing them in the quantum setting presents unique challenges, including a reversibility constraint absent from ordinary classical computation. Here we introduce an evolutionary framework in which large language models (LLMs) propose circuit designs and an independent evaluator verifies and scores them. We apply this framework to tasks spanning numerical computation, chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation, and number theory. In seven of the eight tasks, the discovered circuits significantly reduce both Toffoli and qubit counts relative to the state of the art. In the eighth, the search discovers a new reversible architecture for prime-field modular inversion. By handling reversibility differently, the architecture makes divsteps -- a classical algorithm previously impractical for reversible integer arithmetic -- viable and substantially improves the Toffoli-qubit trade-off for modular inversion. These results open the door for evaluator-guided LLM co-design of new algorithmic structures for quantum computation.

## Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting
- arXiv: 2610.20563 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20563)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/scaling-discovery-requires-forgetting-2610.20563
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 12 – Oct 18, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Shangyin Tan, Ben Zhou, Lakshya A Agrawal, Alex Dimakis, Matei Zaharia and colleagues (UC Berkeley, Stanford, Bespoke Labs) show that automated-discovery agents plateau as they accumulate history, and that periodically discarding all sessions and search state, keeping only the best candidate, lets the same budget go further.
   Key points 01 The plateau: Letting one agent iterate longer, running agents in parallel, and letting parallel agents share candidates all plateau as history accumulates. Each agent anchors on its own past attempts and makes fewer and smaller edits, so extra budget brings little improvement.
 02 Forgetting: The method periodically discards every agent's session and the candidate history, then restarts the team from the best candidate found so far. The authors implement this in a team of agents that communicate with each other.
 03 Results: Across four tasks in heuristic programming, kernel optimization, and model training, forgetting gives up to 15% over parallel agents and up to 39% over a single agent with the same budget. On Anthropic's VLIW kernel take-home, the team with forgetting reaches 1,077 cycles, against 1,172 for four parallel agents and 1,262 for one agent.
 04 Why it matters: Most research-agent designs try to keep as much history as possible. This paper suggests that, for open-ended discovery, what an agent is allowed to remember matters as much as how much compute it gets.
 Abstract Automated research with LLM agents has shown promising results in various domains. Through iterative refinement and scored verifiers, LLM agents are capable of discovering new ideas. There are different ways to scale automated research: let one agent iterate for longer, run several agents in parallel, or further let the parallel agents communicate and build on one another's candidates. However, we find that all three strategies eventually plateau as the agents accumulate history. Each agent becomes anchored to its own past attempts, making fewer and smaller edits. Beyond this point, additional budget brings little further improvement. In this paper, we show that the key to scaling discovery further is to forget. Periodically discarding the search state, including every agent's session and the candidate history, and restarting from the best candidate found so far, unblocks the search and pushes the same budget further. We implement forgetting in a team of agents with communication and evaluate it on four tasks spanning heuristic programming, kernel optimization and model training. The results show up to 15% improvement compared to parallel agents, and up to 39% compared to a single agent. Our results suggest that, for open-ended discovery, what an agent is allowed to remember matters as much as how much compute it is given.

## Hybrid Reasoning Agents
- arXiv: 2610.18861 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18861)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/hybrid-reasoning-agents-2610.18861
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 12 – Oct 18, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Jiaru Zou, Pan Lu, James Zou and colleagues (Stanford University, UIUC, Together AI) present Hybrid Reasoning Agents (HybridAgents), which switch between reasoning in latent space and acting through text within one generation, so an agent can think without writing every step out while keeping tool calls in text.
   Key points 01 HybridLink: A lightweight module moves the model between latent and text modes within a single generation process. A continuous working memory keeps both kinds of context across turns, tool calls stay in plain text, and the latent steps can still be inspected.
 02 Two-stage training: HybridSFT first teaches the backbone to work with HybridLink during tool-integrated generation. HybridRL then teaches the model when to think in latent space and when to act through text.
 03 Results: Across 3 model scales and 11 benchmarks in math, science, medicine, search, and agentic reasoning, it improves over frontier LLM agents and agentic frameworks, both text-based and latent, by 10.4% on average. The 14B model beats the strongest agentic baseline by 17.4% on agentic reasoning tasks.
 04 Why it matters: The agents use 35.5% to 54.7% fewer tokens, run 1.8x to 2.6x faster, and cost 2.7x to 3.7x less. Latent reasoning has mostly been studied without tools, and this work shows a way to combine its efficiency with standard tool use.
 Abstract How can we build an agent that thinks beyond human language, yet still acts through it in the external environment? Modern LLM agents still reason and interact through text, while latent reasoning offers an efficient internal medium but lacks native tool access and reasoning transparency. We bridge these two directions with Hybrid Reasoning Agents (HybridAgents), which dynamically navigate between latent reasoning and text-based interaction throughout a unified generation process. Architecturally, a lightweight HybridLink enables seamless transitions between text and latent modes, while the agent's continuous working memory preserves lossless hybrid context across multiple turns. These designs preserve tool-integrated agentic capabilities while making latent thoughts directly inspectable. Building on this architecture, we further develop dedicated post-training algorithms for hybrid reasoning: HybridSFT stabilizes tool-integrated hybrid generation by teaching the backbone to coordinate with HybridLink, then HybridRL scales reasoning by learning when to think in latent space and when to act through text. We evaluate HybridAgent at 3 model scales across 11 benchmarks covering mathematics, science, medicine, search, and agentic reasoning. Compared with frontier LLM Agents and agentic frameworks spanning both text-based and latent methods, HybridAgent consistently improves performance by 10.4%, while delivering 35.5%-54.7% token reduction, 1.8x-2.6x faster inference, and 2.7x-3.7x cost savings. Code and data are available at https://github.com/togethercomputer/HybridAgent.

## Harness^2: Recursive Agent Harnessing for an Open World
- arXiv: 2610.14224 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14224)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/harness-2-recursive-agent-harnessing-for-an-open-world-2610.14224
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 12 – Oct 18, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Ruiyao Xu, Chen-Yu Lee, Tomas Pfister and colleagues (Google Cloud AI Research) present Harness^2, a plug-in framework that edits an agent's harness for the current task without a ground-truth verifier and carries what it learns from those edits to later tasks.
   Key points 01 Task-level recursion: A contrastive Propose-Probe-Compose step proposes several edits to the harness for the current task, tests them against each other, and keeps the combination that works best. This guides edits reliably without a ground-truth verifier.
 02 Harnessing-level recursion: Lessons from those edits update a second, continual improvement harness that the agent carries to future tasks, so improvements carry over instead of staying task-specific.
 03 Results: A single step improves GLM-5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash over their base harnesses in every configuration tested, with gains of up to 11.5 points on WorkBuddy-Bench, 4.5 points on JobBench, and 11.8 points on LAB. Running five candidate edits in parallel adds another 2.9 to 3.9 points on WorkBuddy-Bench.
 04 Why it matters: ReasoningBank, a memory baseline, fell up to 6.6 points below the base harness on coding tasks in the same comparison. For teams evolving their own harnesses, the paper shows that test-time harness edits can scale with more recursion budget even when no verifier is available.
 Abstract LLM agents' capabilities depend not only on their underlying models but also on their execution harnesses. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) offers a promising approach to automatically evolve these harnesses. However, deploying RSI in open-world settings still faces two critical challenges. First, open-world tasks are typically unseen during the harness design phase and novel at test time, leaving no ground-truth verifiers to reliably guide online harness edits. Second, the RSI process remains highly task-specific and fails to generalize to novel scenarios. We introduce Harness^2, a plug-and-play, recursive agent harnessing framework that addresses these challenges through two connected levels of recursion. Task-level recursion refines the current task's harness using a contrastive Propose-Probe-Compose step that reliably guides edits without ground-truth verifiers, and can be scaled further via parallel or sequential recursion. Harnessing-level recursion then extracts experience from these edits to update a continual improvement harness, enabling the agent to better adapt to future tasks. Across three professional benchmarks and four model-harness configurations, a single step consistently improves GLM-5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash over their base harnesses, yielding gains of up to 11.5 points on WorkBuddy-Bench, 4.5 points on JobBench, and 11.8 percentage points on LAB, with further gains as the task-level recursion budget increases.

## AutoEnvScaling: Automating the Data Flywheel with Terminal Agents

```text
Simon Yu, Baolin Peng, Jianfeng Gao and colleagues (Microsoft, Northeastern, Stanford, UW, UNC) present AutoEnvScaling, in which a proposer agent builds new RL environments for terminal agents from the rollouts of the solver being trained.

- arXiv: 2610.12668 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12668)

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Yuxin Xiao (MIT, work done at Meta), Bailin Wang and colleagues (Meta MRS Research) propose ReHarness, which revises the reflections an agent retrieved for an instance based on how that instance turned out, so harness state gets backward feedback instead of only accumulating.
   Key points 01 Backward revision: After each task, ReHarness uses the finished trajectory as feedback on the specific reflections that were retrieved for it and rewrites them. Feedback flows backward through the textual harness state, much like a recurrent update.

We ask what information could have been captured differently in the harness state to better inform the current instance in the stream. Specifically, after each instance, we leverage the resulting trajectory as feedback to revise the reflections retrieved for that instance, thereby propagating feedback backward through the textual harness state. Across three continual learning benchmarks and three language model backbones, ReHarness consistently outperforms strong raw-context baselines, memory- and reflection-based methods, and prompt optimization approaches. Further analysis shows that ReHarness produces source-grounded and meaningful revisions, corrects overgeneralized or outdated experience under distribution shifts, and improves both stability and plasticity during continual learning.

## LongHarness: Advancing Agentic Reasoning over 100M-Token Corpora via Stateful Evidence Management
- arXiv: 2610.13075 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13075)

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Songtao Huang, Shiyang Feng and colleagues (Shanghai AI Laboratory) present LongHarness, a model-agnostic agent harness for reasoning over corpora up to 100M tokens that stores evidence and conclusions as explicit state and updates each from the other.
   Key points 01 Two failure modes: The authors identify unbounded exploration, where retrieval keeps expanding the history without closing the information gap that matters, and evidence-state drift, where earlier evidence or later corrections stop reaching the current conclusion.
 02 Evidence-Conclusion Coevolution: Open conclusions decide what to retrieve next, and each new piece of evidence strengthens or revises the conclusions that asked for it. An evidence lifecycle step keeps accumulated evidence current and traceable throughout the interaction.

Against state-of-the-art agent harnesses, it surpasses Claude Code by 12.0 percentage points on V1 and Codex by 18.9 points on V2. ## LoGRA: Scaling LLM Reinforcement Learning with Low-Rank Gradient Sketches
- arXiv: 2610.06647 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06647)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/logra-scaling-llm-reinforcement-learning-with-low-rank-gradient-sketches-2610.06647
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 12 – Oct 18, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Shaokun Zhang, Jan Kautz, Yi Dong and colleagues at NVIDIA propose LoGRA, which cuts RL post-training memory by accumulating gradients as low-rank random sketches instead of full gradient buffers, and adds predicted-KL step control to keep updates stable.
   Key points 01 Gradient sketches. A rank-256 random-sign projection, redrawn each step and held fixed across that step's microbatches, compresses each selected weight gradient as microbatches accumulate, so no full gradient buffer is stored for those matrices. The update is reconstructed as the product of two small matrices, which also makes policy synchronization to rollout workers cheaper.
 02 Step control. Before applying an update, LoGRA predicts the resulting KL change in the policy and scales the step to stay within an annealed budget. This replaces learning-rate annealing as the stability mechanism.
 03 Memory. Average training memory falls 21.8% at 1.5B (9.18 to 7.18 GiB) and 45.7% at 7B (31.82 to 17.29 GiB) with peak accuracy close to or above dense Adam (7B Pass@1 72.33% vs 72.48%; 1.5B Pass@1 67.87% vs 63.77%). At 1.5B it also uses less memory than rank-256 LoRA (7.18 vs 13.21 GiB) at similar Pass@4.
 04 27B on one node. LoGRA trains a 27B model for more than 1,100 steps on a single eight-GPU node, where dense Adam runs out of memory.
 Abstract Reinforcement learning has greatly advanced the capabilities of large language models, but its memory demands remain a barrier to broader adoption. We introduce LoGRA, an approach to RL post-training that reduces memory by retaining useful learning signals in low-rank gradient sketches. These compact representations support both model updates and efficient policy synchronization. To prevent overly large updates from disrupting learning, we complement gradient compression with predicted-KL step control, which estimates policy changes before applying each update and adjusts its magnitude accordingly. With all techniques combined, LoGRA reduces average training memory usage by up to 45.7% across reasoning tasks without compromising performance. It also enables stable training of a 27B-parameter model for over 1,100 steps on a single eight-GPU node, where dense Adam runs out of memory, making previously memory-infeasible RL training practical. Code is available in the \href{https://github.com/skzhang1/labs-molt/tree/logra/examples/scripts/logra}{Molt library}.

## GitSwarm: Decentralized Compounding Inference
- arXiv: 2610.04862 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04862)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/gitswarm-decentralized-compounding-inference-2610.04862

```text
Vedant Shah, Carole-Jean Wu, Remi Munos, Rob Fergus, Jakob Foerster, Jason Weston and colleagues at Meta Superintelligence Labs (with Mila, CMU and Princeton) propose compounding inference, where intermediate agent work persists in a shared Git repository so later agents can extend, combine or challenge it, and implement it as GitSwarm.
   Key points 01 Substrate. Homogeneous asynchronous workers share one branchable Git repository. Each contribution is an atomic commit, and an `informed_by=` field records which earlier commits on other branches it depends on. No central planner assigns roles; workers decide whether to explore, verify, refine or combine.

## Agent Mosaic: Recognizing Confidentiality Leaks in Agentic LLM Systems
- arXiv: 2610.30418 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30418)

```text
Daniel Ruiz, Luca Luceri and Emilio Ferrara (USC Information Sciences Institute) measure the mosaic effect, where agents combine individually permitted documents into a forbidden sensitive conclusion.
   Key points 01 Setup. 30 synthetic cybersecurity, medical and military examples yield 10,800 answers from three agents in simulation and 1,440 from live agents in two open-source harnesses.
 02 Result. Even with benign queries answerable from one document, GPT-5.6-Sol discloses forbidden composites in 7.08% of answers in simulation, with higher rates in live harnesses.
