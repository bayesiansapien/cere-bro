## Training Foundation Models with a Single Algorithm
- arXiv: 2610.40564 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40564)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/training-foundation-models-with-a-single-algorithm-2610.40564
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 26 – Nov 1, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Yuda Song, Alvin Zhang, Fahim Tajwar, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, Aarti Singh, Andrea Zanette and colleagues (Carnegie Mellon and Johns Hopkins, with Aurora Innovation) place pretraining, midtraining, SFT and suitable RL objectives under one likelihood-based algorithm, turning training into a choice of data, proportions and timing.
   Key points 01 One shared loss: Suitable RL objectives are derived as maximum-likelihood fitting to model-generated targets, so RL generations and static data share one loss and one trainer. The design problem becomes which data to train on, in what proportions, and when, with staged training as one special case.
 02 Reasoning puzzles: A 124M model trained from scratch beats staged training on the RL tasks and on larger puzzles it never saw in midtraining or RL. Under a fixed compute budget, unified training has the highest accuracy at the smallest and largest budgets, while staged training leads at intermediate budgets.
 03 Math RL: On a 0.76B model, mixing in 0% to 5% midtraining data keeps math accuracy similar while retention improves. Coding accuracy drops as the mix grows.
 04 Why it matters: After math training, all unified models beat staged training on final coding accuracy despite starting lower, and the 5% mix does best on all four metrics. The experiments are small-scale, and the authors have not found a mixture or schedule that works best across the board.
 Abstract Foundation models are typically trained in stages: pretraining, midtraining, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL), with changes in both data and learning algorithms. We show that this algorithmic separation is not fundamental. By deriving suitable RL objectives as maximum-likelihood fitting to model-generated target distributions, we place all of these stages under a single likelihood-based training algorithm, unified training. This perspective turns the foundation-model training lifecycle into a problem of choosing which data to train on, in what proportions, and when, with conventional staged training as a special case. We instantiate this framework in a unified training system that mixes static data and RL generations within a single trainer. Across controlled and real-world experiments, unified training improves the accuracy-compute tradeoff and task coverage while preserving broader capabilities, and provides a stronger starting point for continual learning. This work lays the groundwork for a unified science of foundation-model training, connecting mathematical principles, training infrastructure, and the development of model capabilities.
```

## Agentic Backdoors via Pretraining Poisoning
- arXiv: 2610.37741 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37741)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/agentic-backdoors-via-pretraining-poisoning-2610.37741
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 26 – Nov 1, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Xinyan Hu, Pingbang Hu, Chloe Loughridge, Xuanli He, Nicholas Carlini and colleagues (Anthropic Fellows Program, with UC Berkeley, UIUC, Deeptune and UCL) show that poisoning a small share of the pretraining corpus can plant a remote code execution backdoor that survives a clean post-training pipeline and fires in the deployed CLI agent.
   Key points 01 End-to-end threat. They pretrain Qwen3 models from scratch at 0.6B, 1.7B and 4B, then post-train each into a CLI agent with SFT, DPO and RLVR for tool use. The backdoor persists through all three stages.
 02 Attack rate. Poisoning 0.1% of the corpus reaches roughly 92% attack success on the 4B model. Success increases with model scale, and clean-input behavior stays normal.
 03 Low-dose result. At 0.0005%, about 2,500 documents in a 100B-token corpus, the injected trigger still fires on 1.4% of prompts in a single-seed 4B run.
 04 Ordinary triggers. The trigger can be an ordinary file path in the user's own request. The attacker does not need to inject a rare string at inference time.
 05 Harder to filter. The declaration-based poison states the trigger and payload rule in plain text. It is harder to catch with common data curation than a demonstration transcript, and it reaches higher attack success.
 Abstract Large language models are pretrained on vast, uncurated corpora scraped from the public web. This creates a security risk from attackers who plant poisoned content in the pretraining data. We show for the first time that such poisoning can plant a remote code execution backdoor in an agent, causing it to execute attacker-chosen commands on the user's machine. To measure the threat end to end, we pretrain Qwen3 models from scratch at sizes 0.6B, 1.7B, and 4B, and then post-train each into a CLI agent using a clean pipeline. Poisoning only 0.1% of the pretraining corpus with our new poison design implants the backdoor: the deployed agent executes the malicious command when the trigger is present while behaving normally on clean inputs. The attack success rate (ASR) increases with model scale, with the 4B model reaching roughly 92% ASR at a 0.1% poison rate and staying positive even when the poisoning rate drops to 0.0005%. Our code is available at https://github.com/safety-research/agentic-backdoor.
```

## AgentBaton: Efficient Model Collaboration in Agentic Tasks
- arXiv: 2610.35959 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35959)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/agentbaton-efficient-model-collaboration-in-agentic-tasks-2610.35959
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 26 – Nov 1, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Junlong Li (HKUST) and Jiarui Yao (UIUC), working as NVIDIA interns with Jindong Jiang, Yonggan Fu, Pavlo Molchanov and colleagues, study handing off a long-horizon agent task between a capable and a cheap model mid-run, and build AgentBaton, a detector that picks the handoff point.
   Key points 01 Direction matters. Handing off from a cheap model to a capable one generally underperforms capable-only runs and saves little or nothing. Handing off from capable to cheap at the right stage keeps or improves accuracy at much lower cost.
 02 One handoff is enough. A single well-timed capable-to-efficient handoff performs well, so the problem reduces to choosing when to switch.
 03 AgentBaton. A lightweight detector learns the handoff point from recurring task-progress patterns. It runs at a 338 ms median latency on one H100 for 2K to 121K token inputs.
 04 Results. Across 11 model pairs and two benchmarks it cuts API cost by 65.9% on average while largely keeping accuracy, with gains of up to 8.8 points over capable-only runs, and extends the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier across reasoning-effort settings.
 Abstract Solving long-horizon agentic tasks requires language agents to interact with their environments over multiple steps, and reliable performance often comes from the expensive capable models available through cloud providers. Meanwhile, alternative efficient models with inferior performance but a much lower cost are emerging, including lower-tier cloud models and smaller open-weights models deployable on local hardware. This raises a natural question that remains less explored than traditional query-level routing: can capable and efficient models collaborate within a single long-horizon agentic task to substantially reduce cost while preserving task accuracy? In this paper, we systematically investigate key design choices for such collaboration through model handoffs, where one model takes over from another, focusing on handoff direction and context transfer. We find that efficient-to-capable handoffs generally underperform capable-only execution with limited or even no cost reduction, whereas capable-to-efficient handoffs at suitable stages of task progress largely preserve or even improve accuracy while substantially reducing cost. These findings suggest that a one-time, simple, well-timed handoff strategy is good enough for cost-effective agent collaboration, motivating AgentBaton, a lightweight detector that learns when to hand off from recurring patterns in task progress. Across 11 model pairs and two representative benchmarks, AgentBaton reduces API costs by 65.9% on average while largely preserving task accuracy, with accuracy gains of up to 8.8 percentage points over capable-only execution. Experiments across various reasoning-effort settings further show that AgentBaton extends the empirical accuracy-cost Pareto frontier.
```

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Chao Wan, Paul Jünger, Beixi Du, Bodong Liu, Navaneetha Krishnan and Kilian Weinberger (Cornell University, ASAPP) propose DecisionRAG, which drops the first-stage retriever and instead uses a decision model to navigate a corpus hierarchy from topics to documents to passages.
   Key points 01 Hierarchical navigation: At each level, a decision model such as Jev scores every candidate in one forward pass without generating tokens, then keeps the most promising branches. Under a balanced hierarchy, the number of sequential decisions grows logarithmically with corpus size.
 02 Shared context: Judging candidates together lets the model compare them against one another. This adds 7.7 R@1 points on MultiDoc2Dial and 6.5 Hit@1 points on Natural Questions over judging them separately.
 03 Results: With no task-specific training, DecisionRAG beats dense retrieval plus an 8B reranker by 13.8 R@1 points on MultiDoc2Dial and 17.4 Hit@1 points on Natural Questions, which searches 2.68 million Wikipedia paragraphs. Retrieval takes 0.7 to 1.8 seconds and 0.2 to 2.3 cents per query.

The method depends on a well-organized corpus hierarchy, and a branch pruned early stays lost unless budgets grow or dense retrieval adds candidates.
 Abstract While language models have become dramatically more capable, retrieval still largely relies on lexical matching or similarity in fixed embedding spaces. This creates a capability asymmetry: what the retriever fails to find, even the strongest generator cannot recover. We propose DecisionRAG, which brings the capabilities of pretrained language models to retrieval using decision models that jointly evaluate candidate branches in a single forward pass, without generating any tokens, and show that judging candidates in a shared context makes their relevance estimates comparable. Like a person browsing a library, DecisionRAG navigates the corpus's inherent structure from broad topics down to individual passages, deciding at each level which branches are worth exploring. Without task-specific training or a first-stage retriever, DecisionRAG outperforms dense retrieval with an 8B reranker by 13.8 R@1 points on MultiDoc2Dial and 17.4 Hit@1 points on Natural Questions. Across four benchmarks, including a corpus of 2.68 million Wikipedia paragraphs, retrieval takes just 0.7-1.8 seconds per query.
```

## A Heart Means Recursion: Exploring Visual Meaning and Communication in VLMs
- arXiv: 2610.36449 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36449)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/a-heart-means-recursion-exploring-visual-meaning-and-communication-in-vlms-2610.36449
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 26 – Nov 1, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Alexa Tartaglini, Zhengxuan Wu, Thaddäus Wiedemer, Martin Wattenberg, Been Kim and colleagues (Google DeepMind, Stanford) report that simple images such as a red heart can change how VLMs solve unrelated coding problems, and that models cannot explain this effect when asked.
   Key points 01 Visual steering: A red heart in context moves Claude Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.4 to recursion in 100% of samples, and a yellow checkmark moves Gemini 3.1 Pro to 90%. The effect matches or approaches directly instructing the model to use recursion.
 02 Images over captions: Text captions of the same symbols do not reproduce the pattern, and random or naturalistic images steer less. The same 288 colored shapes also shift writing style and answers to simple preference questions.
 03 Hidden influence: For GPT-5.4, 74.7% of thinking traces misattribute the symbol's role. When images are inserted silently, Claude Opus 4.7 still reaches a full switch to recursion, and GPT-5.4's traces never mention the image.
 04 Why it matters: When models send images to each other, they choose shapes such as spirals and arrows that they and humans find intuitive, and these often fail to steer the receiver. Agents that read screenshots can change behavior in ways the reasoning trace will not show, although the tasks in this study are synthetic.
 Abstract Do vision-language models (VLMs) share our ability to communicate visually? We share preliminary findings, starting from a surprising observation: simple visual symbols like a red heart can strongly influence model behavior on seemingly unrelated tasks, such as steering models towards recursive solutions on simple coding problems. Notably, this steering effect is largely implicit, as models' direct interpretations and thinking traces about visual symbols diverge substantially from their observed behavior. In visual communication between models, this leads to a generation--interpretation gap: while models tend to send shape primitives that they (and humans) find intuitive, these visual cues often fail to steer other models. In further communication games involving abstract concepts and stories, models can creatively use visuals to transmit meaning to one another, although some communications remain non-intuitive to humans. These findings reveal that VLMs might be capable of rich, creative, and visually-grounded communication, but their interpretation and production of visual meaning diverges from human conventions in surprising ways. Together this work highlights both the promise and challenges of visual communication between humans and VLMs.
```

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Ruijia Niu, Kaiwen Liu, Rose Yu and Kristina Klinkner (Amazon, UC San Diego) present EMMA, which treats a multi-agent system's configuration as structured episodic memory and restricts each self-improvement edit to the component responsible for the diagnosed failure.
   Key points 01 Three memory levels: A controller plans each task one step at a time and draws on routing memory. Specialist agents inherit a generalist prompt, and skills hold short per-step procedures, all as text run by one fixed LLM.
 02 Scoped updates: After a failure, a reflector assigns the fix to the scope that owns it and proposes one edit there. Guards that do not depend on the backbone keep every accepted edit scoped, incremental, and bounded.

 04 Why it matters: Multi-agent search baselines fall sharply on open-weight models. Episodic memory updates at context level, but single agent adaptation struggles to alleviate cross-task interference, especially with complex tasks such as multi-application navigation or data pool analysis. Although multi-agent evolution alleviates this problem, existing methods lack clear definitions on update rules and rely on the backbone model to decide where and how much the system changes. This ill-scoped evolution makes learning follow the backbone's structural preferences rather than the observed failures: one backbone accumulates skills while another spawns agents. We present EMMA (structured Episodic Memory for Multi-Agent workflow synthesis), which treats the configuration of a multi-agent system (controller routing memory, specialists inheriting a generalist prompt, and per-step procedural skills) as episodic memory. EMMA makes the ownership and admissibility of updates explicit: a reflector assigns each diagnosed failure to the scope owning its correction and proposes one edit within it, and backbone-independent guards keep every accepted edit scoped, incremental and bounded. Across KramaBench, AppWorld and BBEH with five backbones and four baselines (ACE, GEPA, EvoMAS and ADAS), EMMA attains the best overall performance on every benchmark, up to 32.5% over the strongest baseline, maintains consistent improvements across backbones, and uses 17-38% fewer training executions than the most efficient baseline.
```

## Rethinking Verification in Agent Harnesses
- arXiv: 2610.34577 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34577)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/rethinking-verification-in-agent-harnesses-2610.34577
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 26 – Nov 1, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Zhengxin Zhang, Erkang Zhu, Yin Lin, Claire Cardie, Bolin Ding and Jingren Zhou (Alibaba Group, Cornell University) study verification as an axis of test-time scaling in agent harnesses, comparing 17 designs that differ in when the harness intervenes, who checks the work and how checks lead to revision.
   Key points 01 Instructions and reminders: Gains are modest and depend on the model. System-role instructions add 3.3 points on Terminal-Bench and 3.9 on SWE-bench, and system-role reminders add 3.1 to 3.8 points.
 02 Completion gates: Early user rejection gives the largest gate gains at 6.0 and 6.2 points, but rejection designs cost 1.60x to 1.76x baseline on Terminal-Bench and 1.48x to 1.56x on SWE-bench. The authors recommend avoiding completion-time self-checking.
 03 Independent review: A separate critic adds 5.9 to 9.5 points on Terminal-Bench at 1.13x to 1.25x baseline cost, and 3.9 to 6.6 points on SWE-bench at 1.00x to 1.04x. Extra review rounds or critics add up to 3.5 more points on Terminal-Bench.
 04 Why it matters: The critic designs also lead on VisualBench, with net preferences of 13.2 to 23.8 points over the baseline, and reviewing the changes beats reviewing the trajectory there. Teams adding one verification step to a harness have a measured case for a separate reviewer.
 Abstract Agent harnesses enable large language models to act through tools, observe the results, and iteratively work toward a task goal. Verification checks and revises agents' work to improve output quality. Yet there is limited guidance on how harnesses should organize this effort. We study verification as an axis of test-time scaling by examining when to intervene, who checks the work, and how checking leads to revision. Our framework spans four layers of the agent lifecycle: initial instructions, mid-turn reminders, completion gates, and independent review. We compare 17 designs across seven frontier coding models on Terminal-Bench (TB) and SWE-bench Verified (SWE), and introduce VisualBench for expert evaluation of visual and interactive artifacts. Initial instructions and execution-time reminders yield modest, model-dependent average gains on the coding benchmarks, without substantially increasing average output token use. Completion gates can yield larger gains but may substantially increase cost. Independent review by a critic offers a more favorable accuracy-cost tradeoff, improving average accuracy by 5.9-9.5 percentage points on TB and 3.9-6.6 on SWE at 1.13-1.25 and 1.00-1.04 times baseline cost, respectively. On VisualBench, independent review achieves net preferences of 13.2-23.8 points over the baseline across its four designs, exceeding the evaluated L0-L2 designs. Additional review rounds or independent critics further improve average TB accuracy by up to 3.5 percentage points. Together, these results provide empirical guidance for verification design in agent harnesses and support independent review as a practical axis of test-time scaling.
```

## System One models Are Not Better Rerankers
- arXiv: 2610.32318 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32318)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/system-one-models-are-not-better-rerankers-2610.32318
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 26 – Nov 1, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Michael Dinzinger, Kanishka Ghosh Dastidar and Michael Granitzer (University of Passau, Interdisciplinary Transformation University Austria) test whether decision fine-tuning, used to build System One models that answer with a calibrated probability, produces better rerankers than plain reranker fine-tuning of the same backbone.
   Key points 01 Setup. Six Qwen backbones from 0.6B to 27B, each compared as the untuned model asked zero-shot, as decision fine-tunes and as reranker fine-tunes (two trained by the authors), all reranking the same BM25 top-100 on TREC DL 2019 to 2023 and five BEIR collections.
 02 Ranking. Decision fine-tuning never gives the better reranker. From 0.6B to 9B, reranker fine-tuning wins on every backbone by 5 to 29 points nDCG@10, including on zero-shot collections; at 27B the two tie.
 03 No gain over zero-shot. Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over asking the untuned model the same yes or no question.
 04 Calibration. Asked whether a passage answers the query, decision models are better calibrated than published reranker fine-tunes from 4B up, but a slightly different question removes the advantage. Up to 9B, a reranker fine-tune still keeps better passages at a fixed threshold.
 Abstract System One models, or decision models, answer a question about an input with a calibrated probability instead of generated text. Within weeks of the first release, in September 2026, they were used as rerankers. We test whether the decision fine-tuning behind them makes a better reranker than plain reranker fine-tuning of the same model. For six Qwen backbones from 0.6B to 27B parameters and ten TREC DL and BEIR collections, we compare the untuned model, asked zero-shot, with its decision fine-tunes and its reranker fine-tunes, two of which we train ourselves. In our work, we draw the conclusion that decision fine-tuning never makes the better reranker. From 0.6B to 9B, reranker fine-tuning wins on every backbone by 5 to 29 points nDCG@10, also on zero-shot collections, and at 27B the two tie. Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over simply asking the untuned model. Moreover, we find that the calibration advantage of decision models depends on the question. Asked whether a passage answers the query, they are better calibrated than the published reranker fine-tunes from 4B on. Asked a slightly different question from prior work, they lose this advantage. Lastly, calibration does not make up for a worse ranking: up to 9B, a reranker fine-tune keeps the better passages at a fixed threshold. If you need a reranker, fine-tune a reranker.
```

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Yuhua Jiang, Chang Xu, Feifei Gao and Li Zhao (Tsinghua University, Microsoft Research Asia) present HarnessMM, which cuts harness token cost by reusing decisions that earlier tool feedback has already fixed instead of calling the model again.
```

- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/correction-or-manipulation-measuring-epistemic-vigilance-in-llm-agents-under-use-2610.36453
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 29, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Nardine Basta, Venus Haghighi, Dali Kaafar and Amin Beheshti (Macquarie University) use signal detection theory to separate how often seven LLMs concede to users from how well they tell a correction from a manipulation, and test both on answers and on customer-support actions.
   Key points 01 Setup. Simulated users push agents toward correct and incorrect answers with graded pressure. The pressure either appeals to the source, such as claimed authority, or offers unverifiable content such as a cited source.
 02 Citations. Citations made five of seven models more willing to concede and four of them worse at discriminating correction from manipulation. Stronger wording added little.
 03 Actions vs answers. As customer-support agents, five models never broke the verification policy, including models that readily abandoned correct answers. Ministral 3B broke it mainly under claimed authority, and Ministral 8B reported account changes it had not made.
 04 Instruction fix. Telling the agent to revise only on verifiable evidence mainly lowered its overall willingness to concede, not its discrimination. Concession rates alone are a misleading metric.
 Abstract LLM agents that answer questions and act on behalf of users must decide when to yield to a user who disagrees. Disagreement may correct a genuine error or may be an attempt at manipulation. Prior work shows that models frequently abandon correct answers when challenged by users, yet it remains unclear whether they distinguish correction from manipulation or merely differ in their propensity to concede. We examine this question across seven LLMs from four vendors in two studies, one on the answers agents give and one on the actions they take as customer-support agents whose policy permits account changes only for verified users. Simulated users push toward correct and incorrect answers with graded pressure. Following research on epistemic vigilance, the pressure either appeals to the source, such as claimed authority, or offers content the agent cannot verify, namely a cited source. Drawing on signal detection theory, we measure on a set of benchmark questions how much more readily each agent concedes to correction than to manipulation, separately from its overall propensity to concede. Citations made five of seven models readier to concede and four worse at discriminating, while stronger wording added little. In the agentic study, five models never violated the verification policy, including models that readily abandoned correct answers, so concession on answers did not predict violations of policy. Ministral 3B violated the policy mainly under claimed authority, and Ministral 8B reported changes it had not made. An instruction to revise only on verifiable evidence chiefly shifted propensity to concede rather than improving discrimination. Concession rates alone are therefore misleading, and agents should be evaluated on both their discrimination and the actions they take.
```

## The uneven rise of AI-generated web content
- arXiv: 2610.36631 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36631)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/the-uneven-rise-of-ai-generated-web-content-2610.36631
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 28, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Adam Visokay, Soumyakanti Pan, Tyler McCormick and colleagues (2026 Summer Institute in Computational Social Science at the University of Washington, UC Berkeley) estimate how much of the crawlable web contains AI-generated text, using more than 5 million pages from 2019 to 2026 and a Bayesian correction for detector errors.
   Key points 01 Prevalence. In August 2026 about 24% of crawlable webpages (95% credible interval 22.9 to 24.4) contained AI-generated or AI-edited text, up from near zero before 2022.
 02 Acceleration. The rate of increase nearly doubled, from 4.6 points per year between early 2023 and early 2025 to 8.1 points per year since.
 03 Where it grows. About four-fifths of the increase comes from domains on the periphery of the web graph. The most central tier is the least likely to contain AI text. Prevalence is higher on English pages than other languages and much lower on .gov and .edu than .com.
 04 Method. A Bayesian hierarchical model combines several AI detectors with labeled benchmarks and pre-2022 control crawls, estimating prevalence jointly with detector sensitivity and specificity and propagating that uncertainty.
 Abstract In less than five years, generative artificial intelligence (AI) has dramatically reshaped the public-facing web. Using a sample of more than 5 million crawlable webpages, we demonstrate that the share containing AI-generated text has increased precipitously, but unevenly. Our analyses reveal substantial differences in AI composition across language and domain categories (e.g., .edu, .com, or .gov). A higher fraction of English-language webpages contain AI-generated text compared to pages in other languages, and prevalence is substantially lower on .gov and .edu domains compared to .com. These distinctions persist even after accounting for compositional effects. We also find heterogeneity based on a page's position in the connectivity structure of the web. AI-generated text is most common and has increased most rapidly in pages that are the least central in the graph, which drives much of the overall growth in AI-generated text. A slower increase has taken place for more central pages, with the most central tier emerging as the least likely to contain AI-generated text. Methodologically, we use multiple AI detection tools, and generate estimates using a Bayesian hierarchical model that incorporates corrections for the detectors' statistical properties (both false positives and false negatives) and allows for different detector performance across groups, while propagating uncertainty in detector error rates into prevalence estimates. Our work suggests that AI is a prominent, but varied, contributor to public information and internet-based communication.
```

## FeatureArc: Benchmarking Coding Agents on Evolving Features
- arXiv: 2610.37434 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37434)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/featurearc-benchmarking-coding-agents-on-evolving-features-2610.37434
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 28, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Yunlong Wang, Alyssa Wu, Cuiyun Gao and colleagues (Tencent Hunyuan, CUHK) introduce FeatureArc, a benchmark where coding agents extend the same feature over 2 to 10 successive stages on their own earlier code, and find that success drops by more than half once the whole feature is tested at the end.
   Key points 01 Setup. 73 manually reviewed tasks across four languages are built from repository histories. Each stage has explicit requirements, interface contracts, fail-to-pass tests for new behavior and pass-to-pass tests for existing behavior. Agents work in one persistent session with no external test feedback and no rollback.
 02 Headline gap. Across 10 coding agents, pooled success is 62.7% on the first stage and 28.5% when the full feature is tested on the final code.
 03 Inherited defects. Defects carried over from earlier stages appear in 72.9% of failed later stages.
 04 Undetected errors. In stages following a failure, agents leave the earlier error unnoticed about 95% of the time, and their self-tests rarely revisit earlier stages.
 05 Use at Tencent. The construction pipeline is used internally to extend evaluations to multi-stage feature work for the Hunyuan model family.
 Abstract A software feature usually evolves over time: developers extend its first version as new use cases and requirements arrive, and each extension builds on code written earlier. Most feature benchmarks evaluate a single request on a fixed codebase. Feature evolution requires evaluating successive extensions on an agent's own earlier code and checking whether the feature remains correct. We introduce FeatureArc to evaluate coding agents on evolving features, together with a pipeline that constructs its tasks from repository histories. The benchmark combines dependencies between successive stages of the same feature, explicit requirements and interface contracts, and executable checks across four programming languages. The pipeline validates fail-to-pass (F2P) tests for new behavior and pass-to-pass (P2P) tests for existing behavior on the reference code before and after each stage. We evaluated 10 coding agents on 73 manually reviewed tasks of 2 to 10 stages. Each agent works in one persistent session and workspace without external test feedback or rollback. The evaluation reveals a gap between first-stage and whole-feature success: pooled success falls from 62.7% at the first stage to 28.5% when the whole feature is tested on the final code. Trajectory analysis identifies inherited defects as a common failure mechanism, present in 72.9% of failed later stages. These earlier errors often remain undetected: in stages following a failure, agents leave the earlier error unnoticed about 95% of the time, and their self-tests rarely revisit earlier stages. Evolving a feature autonomously, without human checks between stages, therefore remains a substantial challenge for current coding agents. At Tencent, the pipeline extends internal evaluations to this setting and guides the development of Hunyuan, Tencent's family of large language models.
```

## 3, 7, 14, 17, 42: Instruction Tuning Installs and Sharpens Number-Choice Heuristics in Language Models
- arXiv: 2610.36178 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36178)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/3-7-14-17-42-instruction-tuning-installs-and-sharpens-number-choice-heuristics-i-2610.36178
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 28, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Samar Ansari computes, without sampling, the exact probability that 18 open-weight model lines assign to each integer when asked for a random number, and shows that instruction tuning installs round-number avoidance and sharpens endpoint avoidance.
   Key points 01 Exact measurement. For a fixed prompt, the probability of each integer is computed by summing over its scored spellings, which removes the sampling noise in earlier studies. The study covers five ranges up to N = 100, 29 decoding configurations and two inference stacks.
 02 Less uniform after tuning. In all 15 matched base and instruction-tuned pairs, tuning makes the distribution less uniform.
 03 Two heuristics. Tuned checkpoints avoid round numbers, multiples of 10 almost completely, while base checkpoints do not once endpoints are excluded. Base checkpoints return 1 and N at 0.30 to 0.60 of the interior rate, and tuned checkpoints almost never return them.
 04 Comparison with humans. Endpoint avoidance and an odd-number preference at N = 100 match a classic account of human number choice. Round-number avoidance is not part of that account, and predicted midpoint avoidance is essentially absent.
 05 No shared favorite. Above N = 10 no modal integer is shared by more than 42% of tuned conditions. Nucleus sampling at top-p 0.95 adds a median of only 0.035 to the total variation distance, so the concentration is in the model's own distribution.
 Abstract We measure, without sampling error, the probability a language model assigns to each integer from 1 to N when asked for a random number. For a fixed prompt the model defines one probability vector, which we compute by summing over each integer's scored spellings rather than estimate by sampling, removing the noise that limits earlier studies [1, 4]. We evaluate 18 open-weight model lines at five ranges up to N = 100, under 29 decoding configurations, on two inference stacks; 15 of them have matched base and instruction-tuned checkpoints that both give usable answers. Instruction tuning makes the distribution less uniform in all 15 pairs. It installs one heuristic, round-number avoidance, and sharpens another, endpoint avoidance. Base checkpoints do not avoid round numbers once the endpoints are removed from those sets; tuned checkpoints avoid them, multiples of 10 almost completely. Base checkpoints return the endpoints 1 and N at 0.30 to 0.60 of the interior rate; tuned checkpoints almost never return them. A preference for odd numbers is consistent across tuned checkpoints only at N = 100, where base checkpoints also show it weakly. Endpoint avoidance and the odd preference fit the account Kubovy and Psotka [6] proposed for human number choice; round-number avoidance is not part of that account, and the midpoint avoidance it predicts is essentially absent, with one slight exception at N = 100. Tuning does not install a new favourite integer shared across models: above N = 10 no modal integer is shared by more than 42 percent of tuned conditions, and P (17) rises in 10 of 15 pairs and falls in 5. The concentration is present in the model's own distribution: at temperature 1.0, nucleus truncation at top-p 0.95 adds a median of 0.035 to the total variation distance.
```

## Beyond Update Counts: A Control Map for Evaluating Test-Time Model Updates
- arXiv: 2610.36109 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36109)
- DAIR page: https://academy.dair.ai/papers/beyond-update-counts-a-control-map-for-evaluating-test-time-model-updates-2610.36109
- source: AI Papers of the Week | DAIR date: Oct 28, 2026

DAIR curator's take and key points:

```text
Zehao Zhao, Shangjian Yin, Mark Ibrahim, Kavosh Asadi, Hamed Firooz and colleagues (Meta AI) argue that test-time update methods are compared on the wrong quantity, and propose the Control Map, which records the full history of committed updates before each scored answer and scores acquisition, retention, locality and transfer separately.
   Key points 01 Problem. Systems that update weights, adapters or memory at test time are usually compared by update count and by accuracy right after updating. Two systems with the same count can answer after different update histories.
 02 Framework and theory. The Control Map logs which updates preceded each answer. The authors prove when counts alone determine the final state and bound how differences in pre-answer histories limit differences in average scores.
 03 Scheduling. At a 760M-parameter checkpoint, 24 schedules with the same update count give accuracy from 19.7% to 34.2%.
 04 Acquisition is not retention. All six selected CounterFact LoRA settings learn the new fact. ROME retains edits at all 13 operating points but fails locality at every one of them.
 05 No single winner. No editor is best across models, benchmarks and tests, so the authors recommend reporting update histories and scoring acquisition, retention and locality separately.
 Abstract Test-time updating lets a language model change persistent state after training---such as model weights, adapter parameters, or a memory module---before or while answering queries. Existing evaluations often compare systems using update count or frequency and performance measured immediately afterward. These summaries omit what is updated, where, when, and in what order. The same total can therefore hide an important difference: one system may answer after one update while another answers after two. We introduce the Control Map, an evaluation framework that records the committed updates preceding each scored answer and separately evaluates behavioral changes, immediate learning (acquisition), later recall (retention), preservation of unrelated behavior (locality), and performance in new settings. We prove when counts alone determine final state and show, under stated assumptions, how differences between pre-answer histories limit differences in average scores. Using ControlMap, our experiments reveal three insights. First, update scheduling matters: at a 760M-parameter checkpoint, 24 schedules with the same update count yield 19.7%--34.2% accuracy. Second, learning an edit does not guarantee that it lasts or remains contained: all six selected CounterFact LoRA settings learn the new information, while ROME retains edits at all 13 operating points but fails locality at each. Third, no editor performs best consistently across models, benchmarks, and tests. Evaluations should therefore report update histories and assess acquisition, retention, and locality separately.
```
