- **xAI ships an open-source Grok CLI** ([@aksheyd](https://nitter.net/aksheyd/status/2081189103006298152#m), quoting [@elonmusk](https://nitter.net/elonmusk/status/2081174079969632347#m), [x.ai/cli](https://x.ai/cli)). The pitch is that trying new developer tools should feel like a video game, with a `/tutorial` command as the entry point, and the tool is open source. The attached screenshot is the substantive part. It shows a terminal UI with a git branch indicator and working directory on the top line, a token counter reading **9.2K / 500K** in the top right, a `session_start [hooks: 1]` event in the transcript, a slash-command autocomplete row surfacing `/tutorial`, a model and effort selector reading **Grok 4.5 (high)**, a permission mode reading **always-approve**, and a footer with `Enter:send`, `Shift+Tab:mode`, `Ctrl+.:shortcuts`. That is a feature-for-feature match with Claude Code's interaction model, including hooks and permission modes, at a 500K context window. The read: the terminal agent harness is converging on one design, and xAI just made its implementation free to fork.

- **Grok 4.5 usage surging in Augment's Cosmos on real production codebases** ([@MarioNawfal](https://nitter.net/MarioNawfal/status/2081235563924062517#m)). The claim is that the growth is coming from engineering teams working on large, messy production repos rather than demos, and that Augment's Context Engine already gives models unusually strong repo-wide understanding, so developers routing hard tasks there is a signal about multi-file reasoning and architectural awareness holding up as context grows. The attached image is just the Grok 4.5 wordmark on black, no data. Treat the framing as vendor-adjacent, but the underlying observation is the one that matters for routing: model selection inside a coding product is increasingly decided by behavior on large contexts rather than by benchmark position. Cursor's own board from two days ago put Grok 4.5 High at 66.7 on CursorBench 3.2 for $1.51 per task against Opus 5 High's identical 66.7 for $3.91, so a usage surge at that price point is exactly what the numbers predict.

- **Andrew Ng reframes the open-weights fight** ([@ns123abc](https://nitter.net/ns123abc/status/2081155977894650120#m), quoting [@AndrewYNg](https://nitter.net/AndrewYNg/status/2081103828859117908#m)). Ng's line is that the framing of the debate is a false equivalence: everyone has the right to keep their own code private, and the problem is only when someone tries to stop *others* from open-sourcing. This is a sharper version of the argument in NVIDIA's letter, because it separates the commercial question (should you open your weights) from the policy question (should anyone be permitted to), and only the second is what the proposed restrictions on Chinese open-weight models actually touch. See [the NVIDIA open-weights letter](../../ai-industry/2026-07-25-nvidia-open-weights-letter.md).

- **OpenAI has now signed the open-weights letter** ([@ns123abc](https://nitter.net/ns123abc/status/2081145123761459607#m), quoting [@thogge](https://nitter.net/thogge/status/2080828964625637385#m)). The quoted post calls it "a fascinating day of game theory," which it is. As of yesterday's digest, OpenAI and Anthropic were the two conspicuous non-signatories on a letter signed by NVIDIA, Meta, Microsoft, and a16z, and The Decoder's read was that the compute layer and the model layer had opposed commercial interests on openness. OpenAI signing collapses half of that story. Anthropic's position is now the isolated one, which is worth tracking given that Anthropic is also the lab publishing the strongest agent-security numbers this week.

- **Scoble on why signing letters does not matter** ([@Scobleizer](https://nitter.net/Scobleizer/status/2081066944724443581#m)). He recounts asking Zuckerberg for data portability in 2007 via a published bill of rights for the social web, being refused, and watching that refusal make billions. His conclusion applied to today: he is unexcited about everyone signing a letter supporting open source, and the question worth asking is who will turn down the money that comes from stabbing open source in the back. This is the cynical counterweight to the Ng post above and it is the right one to hold, because none of the signatories has committed to anything enforceable.
