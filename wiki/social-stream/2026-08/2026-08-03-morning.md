## Posts

- **The real divide at an AI hackathon is token budget, not model access** ([@Scobleizer](https://nitter.net/Scobleizer/status/2084168429821665574#m)). Scoble judged the Outside Lands AI Music Hackathon in San Francisco, hosted by @TravLauren, and asked a participant what they had learned. The answer he reports: "There's a difference between the people who show up with their own AI software team with ability to build custom harnesses, and people who never have had no limits to their token generation." Awkwardly phrased and worth reading twice, because the claim underneath is specific. A harness here means the scaffolding around a model, the retry loops, tool wiring, evaluation gates and orchestration that turn one model call into a working system, and building one is a software-engineering skill rather than a prompting skill. The observation is that at the applied end, capability now stratifies by who can afford to burn tokens freely and who can build the surrounding software, not by who has access to the better model, since everyone at the event had the same APIs. Also from the same post: of 47 entries, three were building a mesh network so festival-goers could still communicate when cell service drops, and Scoble's dry read is that those three are the ones who have actually been to a music festival.

- **A labour-cost test for Optimus, with no benchmark in it** ([@Scobleizer](https://nitter.net/Scobleizer/status/2084162475797467260#m), with a [follow-up](https://nitter.net/Scobleizer/status/2084165720636846279#m)). Scoble's stated criterion for whether Tesla's humanoid is "epic" is whether he could see millions of business people buying one, which reduces to two questions: can it do the job, and can I train it to do a new one. He assumes the basics (walking, jumping, dancing) and lists what he expects it to need: an extremely capable generalized world model, very dexterous hands, and the ability to lift a full suitcase into a Cybercab at an affordable price. The follow-up adds "if it does it smoothly and confidently." Logged not because the criteria are novel but because of what they leave out. There is no reference to any benchmark, success rate or evaluation suite, and the framing is entirely "business people look at labor as a cost." That is the commercial gate the robotics literature is not writing against, and it is a different target from the contact-rich manipulation success rates that today's vision-tactile-language-action results report.

- **Linux passes 10% of North American desktop share** ([@dhh](https://nitter.net/dhh/status/2084090303133163683#m), quoting [@vxdb](https://nitter.net/vxdb/status/2083964729496572268#m)). DHH's comment is a joke, that since there is no way to really know he will choose to believe his own Omarchy Arch Linux distribution did it. The attached image is the substance: a StatCounter GlobalStats panel for **Desktop Operating System Market Share in North America, July 2026**, reading Windows 57.54%, OS X 21.14%, **Linux 10.65%**, macOS 8.6%, Chrome OS 2.06%, OpenBSD 0%. Two caveats the tweet does not mention. The figure is North America, not global, while the quoted post says "the Desktop OS marketshare" without qualification. And StatCounter is reporting OS X and macOS as separate rows summing to 29.74%, which is a user-agent classification artifact rather than two distinct platforms, so the denominator these percentages divide is not as clean as the panel implies. The reason it matters here at all is developer environment: local model tooling, llama.cpp builds and CUDA work all assume Linux first, and a rising desktop Linux share on the machines developers actually sit at slowly changes what "runs locally" means by default.
