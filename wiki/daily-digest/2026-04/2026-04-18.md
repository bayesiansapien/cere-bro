## The Big Picture

Three papers dropped this week on the same core problem from different angles: how do you transfer knowledge from a larger model to a smaller one when they don't speak the same "language"? BLD (2026-04-17) handled tokenizer mismatch. TESSY handles generation-style mismatch. Switch-KD handles modality-space mismatch in VLMs. Each paper found a "neutral interface" — bytes, hybrid token sequences, shared text probabilities — and distilled through that. The convergence is striking. It suggests the main unsolved problem in distillation isn't "can we transfer knowledge" but "can we find the right representation space to transfer through."

LongAct adds another piece: even once you have the data, *how* you train on it matters. High-magnitude KV activations during long-context processing are the positions where the model is doing real reasoning work. Concentrating RL gradients there is the training-time analog of saliency-based KV cache eviction at inference time. This is the second paper in a week (after TIP) showing that uniform gradient updates are wasteful and selective training outperforms.

On the industry side, this week crystallized something that's been building: the AI market is fracturing. OpenAI is in internal disarray (three exec departures, shareholder doubts about Altman). Claude doubled its market share month-over-month. And Anthropic is making a bet that its most capable model will be more valuable as a government asset than a consumer product. Whether that's responsible safety practice or strategic repositioning depends on who you ask — but Algorithmic Bridge's piece is worth reading as the most pointed critique from inside the field.

### LongAct: Saliency-Guided Sparse RL for Long-Context Training

> High-magnitude KV activations are the positions where attention is actually working — so why are we training uniformly across all positions?

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers  
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14922) · [Wiki](../../inference-efficiency/2026-04-18-longact-saliency-sparse-rl.md)  
**Tier:** 1 — KV Cache / GPU Optimization

```
Long context input
        │
        ▼
  Attention mechanism
  Q/K tensors
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Low-magnitude positions  │  HIGH-MAG peaks │
  │  (background processing)  │  (active focus) │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        │                              │
  gradient: skip                 gradient: UPDATE
        │                              │
        └──────────────────────────────┘
                        │
                  LongAct training
```

When an LLM processes a long document, not all query and key positions are equally active. Some positions develop dramatically higher activation magnitudes — these are the same "outlier weights" that quantization research (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant) identified as numerically critical. The insight is that these high-magnitude positions are the model's natural attention anchors: they're where the long-context reasoning is actually happening.

Standard RL trains uniformly across all weights. This is expensive and noisy — most gradient signal in long-context settings comes from distant correlations that are hard to attribute. LongAct shifts to saliency-guided sparse updates: profile which positions have high-magnitude activations, restrict gradient updates to only those weights. The result is 8% improvement on LongBench v2 and better generalization on RULER, with no architecture changes. Critically, it works across both GRPO and DAPO — it's not algorithm-specific.

The deepest insight here is the *cross-paradigm transfer*: quantization research observed high-magnitude activations as a problem (hard to represent in low precision). LongAct flips this — high magnitude = high signal = where to train. The same empirical observation generates opposite implications depending on whether you're trying to compress inference or improve training.

**Why it matters:** Saliency-guided sparse RL could reduce long-context training costs by concentrating compute where it matters, while simultaneously improving quality. If the saliency pattern is predictable from architecture, this is a recipe for efficient long-context RL at scale.

**Research angle:** The key open question is whether saliency profiling can be done *during* training (online) rather than requiring a separate profiling pass. If yes, this becomes a practical training recipe. Also: does the high-magnitude activation pattern shift as context window grows, or is it architecturally stable? The answer determines whether LongAct generalizes to 128K+ context models.

### TESSY: Stop Giving Your Student Foreign-Language Homework

> Using a stronger teacher to generate training data often *hurts* the student — the teacher's reasoning is right, but the student's optimizer treats it as noise.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers  
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14164) · [Wiki](../../inference-efficiency/2026-04-18-tessy-teacher-student-sft.md)  
**Tier:** 1 — Distillation

```
Standard SFT pipeline:
  GPT-OSS-120B teacher ──generates──► full sequence ──trains──► Qwen3-8B student
  Result: -3.25% LiveCodeBench, -10.02% OJBench ❌

TESSY pipeline:
  GPT-OSS-120B ──generates──► reasoning tokens (hard thinking)
                                       │
  Qwen3-8B     ──generates──► style tokens (formatting, transitions)
                                       │
                          ┌────────────┴────────────┐
                          │  hybrid training sequence│
                          └─────────────────────────┘
  Result: +11.25% LiveCodeBench, +6.68% OJBench ✓
```

The finding is surprising: for reasoning models like Qwen3-8B, using a stronger teacher's data makes the model *worse*. This isn't because the teacher's reasoning is wrong — it's because the teacher's output style is different enough from the student's that the SFT signal gets corrupted. The student's optimizer treats the stylistic mismatch as a training target, not as noise to filter.

TESSY decouples style from reasoning. The teacher contributes the hard cognitive work (solution strategies, intermediate steps, key insights). The student contributes the formatting, transition language, and surface presentation. The resulting training sequence is something the student's optimizer can actually learn from, because the style envelope matches its own distribution — but the content is genuinely smarter than what the student would generate alone.

The 13-point swing (from -10% to +7% on OJBench) is large. It suggests that a significant fraction of "distillation doesn't work" cases in the literature may be stylistic divergence masquerading as capability transfer failure.

**Why it matters:** Every lab is trying to distill frontier models into smaller, cheaper deployable models. If naive SFT on frontier data degrades performance for reasoning-capable student models, TESSY offers a practical fix that doesn't require architectural changes.

**Research angle:** Does the style/content decomposition generalize beyond code generation to math reasoning or long-form writing? And can TESSY be iterated — use the improved student as the next round's style contributor?

### Switch-KD: VLM Distillation Needs a Single Language

> Visual and language modalities are supervised separately in VLM distillation — but inside the model, they're already fused. Switch-KD distills through the fusion point.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers  
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14629) · [Wiki](../../inference-efficiency/2026-04-18-switch-kd-vision-language-distillation.md)  
**Tier:** 1 — Distillation

Standard VLM distillation supervises vision and language outputs in separate modality silos. The problem: in late-fusion VLMs, visual representations are fused *into* the language space before generation anyway. The vision encoder outputs become language-space tokens. So separate supervision is creating an artificial split at exactly the point where the model has already unified the modalities.

Switch-KD's fix: route the student's visual outputs through the teacher's language pathway (the "switch") to build cross-modal reference distributions, then distill in the shared text probability space. The Dynamic Bi-directional Logits Difference (DBiLD) loss adds a second alignment direction (student→teacher, not just teacher→student) to prevent mode collapse.

A 0.5B TinyLLaVA distilling from a 3B teacher: +3.6 points averaged across 10 multimodal benchmarks. No architecture changes.

**Why it matters:** VLM deployment is bottlenecked by model size. A reliable method to compress a 3B VLM into a 0.5B model without quality loss is practical immediately.

### Corpus2Skill: The Model Should Know Where to Look

> RAG hands the model chunks. Corpus2Skill hands the model a map.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers  
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14572) · [Wiki](../../agents-tool-use/2026-04-18-corpus2skill-knowledge-navigation.md)  
**Tier:** 2 — Agents / RAG

```
Standard RAG:
  Query ──retriever──► [chunk A, chunk B, chunk C] ──► LLM generates answer
  (model never knows what it's missing)

Corpus2Skill:
  Offline: cluster docs ──► LLM-written summaries at each level ──► skill tree

  Query ──► agent sees root summary
              │
              ▼ (drill into branch)
          topic cluster summary
              │
              ▼ (drill into sub-branch)
          document summaries
              │
              ▼ (retrieve by ID)
          full document
              │
              ▼ (backtrack if unproductive)
          different branch
```

The RAG assumption is that dense retrieval surfaces the right context. It works well for single-hop factual questions. It breaks for multi-hop questions where evidence is spread across the knowledge space and you don't know what to search for until you've partially answered the question.

Corpus2Skill compiles the corpus into a hierarchical skill tree offline — an LLM recursively clusters and summarizes documents into progressively coarser representations. At serve time, the agent gets a bird's-eye view and navigates the tree like a filesystem. It can see *where* knowledge lives, backtrack when a branch doesn't pan out, and combine evidence from multiple branches.

The benchmark: WixQA (enterprise customer-support QA). Corpus2Skill beats dense retrieval, RAPTOR (tree-structured retrieval), and agentic RAG across all quality metrics.

**Why it matters:** Enterprise RAG is a large deployment target. The navigation paradigm is fundamentally different from retrieval and more robust for complex multi-hop queries.

### Model Capability Dominates: What AIMO 3 Proved About Inference-Time Optimization

> 23 experiments, 50 IMO-level problems, one H100 — and the conclusion is: just get a better model.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers  
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27844) · [Wiki](../../inference-efficiency/2026-04-17-model-capability-dominates-inference-time.md)  
**Tier:** 1 — Inference Optimization

Already covered in the 2026-04-17 digest, but the punchline is worth restating: Diverse Prompt Mixer ran 3 models × 23 prompt-level experiments on 50 IMO-level problems. Every prompt strategy failed. High-temperature sampling already decorrelates errors; weaker strategies reduce accuracy more than they reduce correlation. The gap between the best majority-vote score (42/50) and pass@20 (~45.5) is a *selection* gap, not a *prompt* gap. A verifier-based selector could close it. Prompt engineering cannot.

This is the clearest empirical statement to date that inference-time scaling via prompt diversity is a dead end. The open problem is now unambiguous: build better verifiers.

## Connecting the Dots

**The distillation convergence:**

```
Teacher-student gap types solved this week:

Tokenizer mismatch ──► BLD (04-17): byte-level interface
                              │
Style mismatch     ──► TESSY: interleaved token sequences
                              │
Modality mismatch  ──► Switch-KD: shared text probability space

All three: find a neutral representation layer, distill through that
```

Three papers, three different gap types, one meta-strategy: find the natural "common ground" between teacher and student and route the knowledge transfer through it. This is not a coincidence — it's a research community converging on a principle. The implication: if you're building a distillation pipeline and it's not working, the first diagnostic question should be "what kind of representation gap do I have?" before reaching for more data or longer training.

**Training-time vs. inference-time saliency:**

LongAct (training: concentrate gradients on high-magnitude activations) is the mirror image of KV cache eviction policies (inference: evict low-attention tokens). The same empirical observation — not all positions carry equal signal — is being exploited in both directions. A clean synthesis paper that unifies saliency-guided training and saliency-guided cache management would be high-impact.

**OpenAI instability + Claude surge:**

The market share data and the exec departures are not independent. OpenAI's product lineup is restructuring *because* Claude is growing fast on developer and enterprise accounts. The bet that ChatGPT's consumer brand would carry all verticals is being tested, and the test is not going well. GPT-Rosalind (life sciences vertical) and Codex-as-coding-agent are moves to defend enterprise accounts specifically — the same segment Claude is winning.
