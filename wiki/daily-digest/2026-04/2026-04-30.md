## The Big Picture

Today's batch makes one structural argument: **the cost of *running the model during training* is now the dominant frontier compute problem.** NVIDIA's paper opens with the number — generation eats 65–72% of every RL step at 8B+. That is not a deployment statistic. That is the cost of trying to do the next post-training run. PrFaaS (04-22) attacked the prefill side of this; speculative decoding now closes the decode side. Combined, these two papers are a recipe: prefill across datacenters, decode with a draft. The 2.5× end-to-end projection at 235B is what changes which frontier experiments are runnable inside a fixed GPU budget.

The second thread is quieter but stronger in its convergence. Tide is the *fourth* paper this month to solve a teacher/student mismatch problem by engineering a neutral exchange representation rather than forcing token alignment: BLD used bytes (04-17), TESSY used cooperative interleaving (04-18), Switch-KD used a shared text-probability space (04-18), and Tide uses bounded-gradient chunk-likelihood matching (04-30). Four papers, four mechanisms, one principle. The community is converging — without saying so explicitly — on the idea that *the channel between mismatched models is the design surface*. That is what the next round of distillation work will be optimizing.

A third thread, smaller but worth flagging: GLM-5V-Turbo's MMTP and Nemotron 3 Super's MTP heads (04-21) suggest Multi-Token Prediction is becoming a general-purpose architectural primitive. MTP started as a speculative-decoding helper. It is now showing up as a multimodal training primitive (GLM-5V) and as the speculation interface itself (NeMo-RL). The same head, three different roles.

### Speculative Decoding for RL Post-Training Rollouts (NVIDIA)

> Speculative decoding has been a deployment trick for two years. NVIDIA just showed it survives policy drift inside the RL training loop — and the speedup numbers are in *frontier-scale-changing* territory.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26779) · [Wiki](../../inference-efficiency/2026-04-30-speculative-decoding-rl-rollouts.md)
**Tier:** 1 — Inference efficiency / GPU optimization / RL training

```
                    ┌───────────────────┐
   prompt ────────► │  Draft model      │ ── k=3 tokens ──┐
                    │  (DAPO-init)      │                 │
                    └───────────────────┘                 ▼
                                                  ┌─────────────────┐
                                                  │  Target policy  │ verify
                                                  │  (verifier)     │ ─────► accepted
                                                  └─────────────────┘
                                                          │
                                            log-probs / loss computed
                                            against TARGET, not draft
                                            ──────────────────────────
                                            (this is the "lossless" guarantee)
```

The single most important number in the paper: rollout generation accounts for 65–72% of every RL step. That number reframes the bottleneck. RL post-training compute is no longer a *gradient* problem — it is an *inference* problem that happens during training. Once you accept that framing, every speculative-decoding trick from the deployment world (EAGLE-3, MTP heads, draft trees) is a candidate training accelerator.

The lossless guarantee is what makes this safe to use. The target policy verifies every draft proposal via exact rejection sampling, and log-probs and policy gradients are computed against the target, not the draft. That is what separates this work from off-policy / replay tricks that change the optimization regime — those papers got speedups by *trading* RL fidelity. NeMo-RL gets speedups *for free*, in the sense that the policy gradient sees the same trajectory distribution.

Three findings deserve attention. First, draft alignment dominates the speedup: a DAPO-initialized draft hits 1.77× at k=3, while an UltraChat chat-aligned draft only gets 1.51× at the same k. The draft must match the rollout distribution, not generic chat. Second, k=3 is the sweet spot — k=5 and k=7 are *slower* than autoregressive on RL-Think rollouts despite higher acceptance lengths, because more speculative work means more wasted draft tokens. Third, n-gram drafting fails despite a 2.47-token acceptance rate. Positive acceptance is necessary, not sufficient: the draft must be cheap enough that wasted work is forgiven.

The 235B projection (2.5× end-to-end on 2048 GB200s) was generated by a high-fidelity simulator, not measured. That should be flagged. But the simulator has been validated against measured 8B numbers, and the projection sits comfortably in the regime where async RL is already in use — so the failure mode is "speedup is 2.0× instead of 2.5×," not "doesn't work at scale."

**Why it matters:** RL post-training is now the dominant frontier compute cost, and inference-side acceleration is the only lever big enough to move it. This paper provides the first lossless integration that survives policy drift.

**Research angle:** The k=3 ceiling is the most interesting open thread. A *content-adaptive k* — varying speculation depth per-token based on draft confidence or rollout phase — would beat the fixed-k regime. A second open thread: this paper preserves losslessness, but a *biased speculation* scheme that drafts near high-reward regions (rather than approximating the current policy) could trade losslessness for sample efficiency. The natural follow-up.

### Tide: Cross-Architecture Distillation for Diffusion LLMs

> Diffusion LLMs are the most credible non-AR frontier — bidirectional context, parallel decoding — but they were stuck at billions of parameters for competitive performance. Tide is the bridge that lets dLLM capabilities flow into 0.6B students.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26951) · [Wiki](../../inference-efficiency/2026-04-30-tide-cross-arch-diffusion-distillation.md)
**Tier:** 1 — Distillation / compression / inference efficiency

```
Teacher (16B MoE LLaDA2 / 8B dense WeDLM)
                ├── Tidal      ← schedule strength: noise-aware
                │              (heavy mask = teacher unreliable, down-weight)
                ├── CompDemo   ← split mask into complementary halves;
                │              teacher predicts each given the other
                └── Reverse Calm ← invert chunk-likelihood matching;
                                bounded gradients, dual-end noise filter
                          ↓
              Student (0.6B BD3LM, different tokenizer)

Result: +1.53 avg / 8 benchmarks; HumanEval 32.3 → 48.78
        22× memory reduction, 5× faster inference
```

The three components map to the three failure modes of cross-architecture diffusion distillation. Tidal handles *temporal* unreliability — the teacher is more accurate at low mask rates than high, and Tidal weights the distillation signal accordingly. CompDemo handles *informational* collapse under heavy masking — when too much is hidden, the teacher's prediction degenerates, and CompDemo restores teacher predictive power by giving it a complementary mask half as context. Reverse Calm handles *cross-tokenizer* gradient instability — the standard chunk-likelihood matching loss is unbounded, so divergent teacher/student tokenizers cause gradient blow-up; inverting the matching direction yields bounded gradients.

What makes this Tier 1 is not the average +1.53 — that is a respectable distillation number. It is the HumanEval result: 32.3 → 48.78. Code generation has been the weakest dLLM area, and Tide closes the gap to AR baselines. If this generalizes, dLLMs become deployment-relevant for code, which is the workload most sensitive to inference speed and bidirectional context (read both ways into a function).

**Why it matters:** dLLMs need a compression story before broad deployment. Tide is the first end-to-end recipe that handles the realistic case — different architecture, different attention, different tokenizer — without requiring a shared interface vocabulary.

**Research angle:** The hardest open regime is *AR teacher → dLLM student*. Tide handles dLLM → dLLM. Using a strong AR teacher (GPT-5.5, Opus 4.6) to distill into a dLLM student requires inventing a bridge between AR's autoregressive supervision and dLLM's diffusion-timestep supervision. This is the natural follow-up — and a head-to-head between Tide's Reverse Calm and BLD's byte-level decoder on the same cross-tokenizer task would clarify whether bounded gradients beat byte alignment, or whether they compose.

### GLM-5V-Turbo: Native Multimodal Agent Foundation Model

> Most VLMs are text-LLMs with a vision adapter. Z.ai trained perception, reasoning, and tool-use jointly, end-to-end. The 94.8 Design2Code result (above Claude Opus 4.6) is the headline; the Multimodal MTP design is the durable contribution.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26752) · [Wiki](../../multimodal/2026-04-30-glm-5v-turbo-multimodal-agent.md)
**Tier:** 2 — Multimodal agents (Tier 1 intersection: MTP-as-primitive)

The interesting architectural detail is **Multimodal Multi-Token Prediction (MMTP)**. Standard MTP heads predict multiple future tokens at once and use that for speculative decoding (Nemotron 3 Super, 04-21). MMTP extends this to multimodal inputs by routing visual content through a shared `<|image|>` special token at the MTP head, rather than pushing raw visual embeddings through. This cuts cross-device communication overhead (visual embeddings are heavy) and stabilizes training. It is the multimodal analog of what made Nemotron's MTP heads cheap.

The other distinguishing claim is **joint RL across 30+ task categories with weaker cross-domain interference than SFT**. That is a strong claim and it is under-explained: is the win because the RL reward shape suppresses task-specific overfitting that SFT amplifies, or because joint perception training creates representations robust enough to share across tasks? The follow-up that disentangles reward effects from representation effects would be high-leverage. This connects to the broader "joint training reduces interference" literature — but most prior work was about SFT-vs-multitask-SFT, not RL-vs-SFT.

**Why it matters:** Native multimodal agents are now competitive with the best frontier closed models on Design2Code. The architectural recipe — CogViT + MMTP + joint multi-domain RL — is open and reproducible.

**Research angle:** MMTP currently uses *one* shared `<|image|>` token. A learned token-set (multiple shared tokens, each routing different visual properties — texture, structure, color) would further reduce communication while preserving signal. This is, in effect, *internal AI routing through learned tokens* — which is the routing thesis applied inside a single forward pass. Worth tracking whether anyone tries this.

### ClawGym: Full-Lifecycle Framework for Claw-Style Personal Agents

> Personal agents that own a workspace have been chronically under-served by training infrastructure. ClawGym is the first end-to-end open framework — synthesis, sandbox-parallel RL, calibrated benchmark.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26904) · [Wiki](../../agents-tool-use/2026-04-30-clawgym-agent-framework.md)
**Tier:** 2 — Agents / agent training infrastructure

The synthesis design is the part worth keeping. **Dual-route synthesis**: persona-driven top-down (start from a user persona, generate plausible workflows — covers realistic distributions) plus skill-grounded bottom-up (start from skills, compose workflows — covers compositional space). Hybrid verification combines code-based checks (deterministic sub-steps) with rubric-based judging (open-ended steps). The two routes have complementary failure modes: persona-driven is bounded by persona diversity, skill-grounded can produce unrealistic workflows. The mixture is currently hand-tuned.

The other operational answer is **per-task sandboxes for parallel RL rollouts**. Persistent-state agents are the operational nightmare of RL training — naive parallel rollouts contaminate each other's state. ClawGym isolates each task in its own workspace, so rollouts run in parallel without cross-contamination. This is the answer to the question Persistent Agent Infrastructure (04-23) raised: how do you do RL on agents that mutate persistent state?

The Qwen3-8B (+43.46%) vs Qwen3-30B-A3B (+25.96%) gap is interesting and inverts naive expectations — the smaller model gains more. This is consistent with the TEMPO observation (04-22) that test-time training and RL fine-tuning reward smaller models proportionally more, because larger models already encode the relevant priors. Worth tracking whether this is a consistent regularity across agent training corpora.

**Why it matters:** Closes a gap in the open agent training stack. Together with Persistent Agent Infrastructure (04-23) and Reward-Free Self-Evolution Agents (04-21), the recipe for training personal agents at scale is now publicly documented.

### Zig's Anti-LLM Contribution Policy and "Contributor Poker"

> The clearest articulation yet of an anti-LLM open-source position — and crucially, it's a project-economics argument, not an ethics argument. The bite holds even if you fully accept LLM-generated code.

**Source:** Simon Willison covering Loris Cro / Zig Software Foundation
**Links:** [Simon Willison post](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Apr/30/zig-anti-ai/) · [Loris Cro original](https://kristoff.it/blog/contributor-poker-and-ai/) · [Wiki](../../ai-industry/2026-04-30-zig-anti-ai-policy.md)
**Tier:** 2 — AI industry / open source / engineering practice

The argument has a simple shape: **PR review is an investment in people, not just code.** A successful OSS project's review time is justified because each new contributor becomes a long-term asset. Loris calls this "contributor poker" — you bet on the person, not the cards. LLM-authored PRs short-circuit this: the time the maintainer spends reviewing doesn't grow a contributor. The cutting line: *"if a PR was mostly written by an LLM, why should a project maintainer spend time reviewing and discussing that PR as opposed to firing up their own LLM to solve the same problem?"*

The Bun divergence is the deployment consequence. Bun (Anthropic-acquired in December 2025) runs a fork of Zig and recently shipped a 4× compile speedup with parallel semantic analysis and multiple codegen units in the LLVM backend. They explicitly will *not* upstream because of Zig's policy. So the policy is now affecting which performance work flows back into the upstream tree. That is the policy's first concrete cost — and its first concrete validation, depending on how you read it.

This is the OSS-side companion to Building Pi (Pragmatic Engineer, 04-29) and the Claude Code postmortem (04-23-24). Mario Zechner (Pi) and Loris Cro (Zig) diagnose the same underlying fact — AI-assisted code shifts the cost structure of software development — and propose different mitigations. Zechner builds a stable harness; Cro builds a wall.

**Why it matters:** This is the first articulation of an anti-LLM stance that other OSS projects can copy without invoking ethics. The argument lands on contribution economics. Expect adoption pressure on at least one major project (CPython, LLVM, Linux subsystem) within 90 days.

### Ken Huang Chapter 12: The Skill System Pattern

> Claude Code's CLAUDE.md is a static memory attachment. Hermes has a full skill lifecycle: versioned frontmatter, progressive disclosure tiers, atomic agent-managed creation, 80+ pattern security scanning, and a GitHub-sourced hub marketplace. These are not the same abstraction wearing the same name.

**Source:** Ken Huang / Agentic AI Substack  
**Links:** [Post](https://kenhuangus.substack.com/p/chapter-12-the-skill-system-pattern) · [Wiki](../../agents-tool-use/2026-04-25-claude-code-memory-systems-chapter8.md)  
**Tier:** 2 — Agents / agent harness architecture

Chapter 8 (04-25) covered Claude Code's QueryEngine memory system and LRU cache behavior. Chapter 12 turns to skills — how agents accumulate and reuse procedural knowledge across sessions. The gap between the two implementations is larger than any previous chapter revealed.

Claude Code's model is minimal by design. Any directory can contain a `CLAUDE.md`; the harness discovers and injects all of them at session start via `startRelevantMemoryPrefetch()`. A dedup set (`loadedNestedMemoryPaths`) prevents re-injection. Slash commands come from `.claude/skills/`. That's the full implementation — static files, full injection, no versioning, no security scanning, no creation, no hub.

Hermes treats skills as a first-class subsystem with five components worth naming:

```
Tier 0: skills_categories()  → category names + counts only
Tier 1: skills_list()        → name + description (no body)
Tier 2: skill_view(name)     → full SKILL.md + linked file list
Tier 3: skill_view(name, file_path="references/ioc-formats.md")
                             → supporting file on demand
```

The four-tier progressive disclosure is the token-cost design: the agent never loads full skill content until it has already decided this skill is the right one. Claude Code loads everything at session start unconditionally.

Agent-managed creation is the self-improvement loop. When Hermes solves a novel problem, it can create a skill from the solution: `_create_skill()` validates frontmatter, writes atomically via `tempfile + os.replace()` (no partial-write state possible), then immediately runs a security scan. If the scan blocks the skill, the entire directory is rolled back via `shutil.rmtree`. The same rollback pattern applies to edits. The agent cannot leave the skill library partially written.

The security scanner covers 9 threat categories with 80+ patterns: exfiltration (curl with `$SECRET` env vars), prompt injection, destructive commands (`rm -rf /`), persistence (SSH authorized_keys), reverse shells, obfuscation (base64 decode pipes), supply chain (curl piped to sh), privilege escalation (NOPASSWD), and credential exposure (embedded private keys). It also detects invisible Unicode (zero-width spaces, RTL overrides). The trust model maps trust level × scan verdict to allow/ask/block — builtin skills can pass anything, community skills are blocked on anything above "caution."

**Why it matters:** The skill lifecycle difference is the clearest structural argument so far for why the two systems make different safety bets at scale. Claude Code delegates safety to human review of static files. Hermes delegates it to a scanner that must clear every agent-created or hub-installed skill before it becomes reusable. Both bets are coherent; they're just optimized for different threat models.

## Connecting the Dots

```
PrFaaS (04-22)         ──┐
   prefill across DCs    │
                          ├── RL-rollout cost stack
NeMo-RL spec dec (04-30) ┘   prefill + decode both attacked
   decode via draft           total: ~3-5x at frontier scale

Nemotron 3 Super MTP (04-21) ──┐
   MTP heads as drafter         │
                                 ├── MTP as general-purpose primitive
GLM-5V MMTP (04-30)           ──┤   inference, training, multimodal
   MTP for multimodal training   │
                                 │
NeMo-RL spec dec (04-30)      ──┘
   MTP / EAGLE / external draft
   all interchangeable

BLD (04-17, bytes)              ──┐
TESSY (04-18, cooperative)        │
Switch-KD (04-18, shared text)    ├── "Engineer the channel,
Tide (04-30, bounded chunks)      │    don't force token alignment"
                                  │   FOUR papers, ONE principle
```

**The RL-rollout cost stack is now coherent.** PrFaaS (04-22) attacked prefill via cross-datacenter disaggregation in hybrid-attention models. NeMo-RL (04-30) attacks decode via speculative decoding inside the training loop. Together: prefill offloaded across DCs, decode accelerated via draft. The 2.5× end-to-end projection at 235B starts to look conservative when stacked with PrFaaS's 50% lower TTFT — though no paper has measured the combined effect yet.

**MTP has become a general-purpose architectural primitive.** Three roles in three weeks: speculative-decoding drafter (Nemotron 3 Super, 04-21), multimodal-training primitive (GLM-5V MMTP, 04-30), and the speculation interface itself (NeMo-RL, 04-30). Whether explicitly designed or accidentally rediscovered, MTP is now the place where "predict more than the next token" lives architecturally. The follow-up question — whether MTP heads can be repurposed for *anything else* (long-context attention, mixture routing) — is the open frontier.

**Four papers, one principle on cross-architecture distillation.** BLD (04-17) used bytes as the channel. TESSY (04-18) used cooperative interleaving as the channel. Switch-KD (04-18) used a shared text-probability space as the channel. Tide (04-30) uses bounded-gradient chunk-likelihood matching as the channel. Different mechanisms, same structural choice: *engineer a neutral exchange representation rather than force token alignment*. Four papers in two weeks is no longer coincidence; the community has converged on the frame. The next round of distillation work will be optimizing *which* channel to use under which mismatch profile.

**Bun-vs-Zig is the first structural cost of an anti-LLM policy.** A 4× compile speedup that won't reach upstream is exactly the kind of concrete loss that decides whether the policy stays. If Bun's fork stays a permanent infrastructure tool, other OSS projects will adopt similar rules. If Anthropic eventually pressures Bun back to upstream, the policy starts to look unenforceable in practice.
