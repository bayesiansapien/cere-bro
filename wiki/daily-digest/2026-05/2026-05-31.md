## TL;DR

The day's center of gravity is research, not news. Parallax upgrades the core attention read from a local constant fit (a kernel-weighted average, which is what softmax attention is) to a local linear fit, makes it trainable at scale by deleting the per-query solver, and matches FlashAttention 2/3 on its decode kernel; its standout secondary result is that the Muon optimizer unlocks the architecture where AdamW does not. Why Larger Models Learn More gives a data-centric mechanism for scaling: small models starve rare-and-complex tasks because common-task gradients overwrite them, and bigger models win by *reduced interference*, validated on OLMo from 4M to 4B. How LoRA Remembers derives a Parametric Memory Law plus a sharp p > 0.5 verbatim-recall threshold and spends the training budget on sub-threshold tokens. On the reward side, CorVer extends RLVR to factual QA using Wikipedia co-occurrence instead of a neural verifier (4.8 to 8.4x faster training), while RUBRIC-ARROW does the subjective-domain half with learned rubrics. When Cloud Agents Meet Device Agents maps a three-way accuracy/cost/edge-energy frontier and finds more frontier compute is not monotonically better, and RePoT recovers failed Program-of-Thought plans from a verified checkpoint at one extra LLM call. On the thin industry side, SoftBank floated a 75 billion euro French data-center buildout, The Decoder reported that AI search agents mostly confirm training-time memory rather than truly browse, and NVIDIA is staging the GTC Taipei keynote (Jensen Huang, June 1) with the first Dell + Vera Rubin NVL72 systems at CoreWeave.

## The Big Picture

Two of today's papers, written by different groups about different things, land on the same uncomfortable conclusion: the optimizer is not a training detail you set and forget, it is part of the architecture. Parallax finds that its local-linear attention only realizes its capacity under Muon, not AdamW. Eight days ago the "Optimizer-Induced Spectral Scaling Laws" paper found that Muon scales the representation rank of rare tokens where AdamW flatlines. Read together, the architecture side and the scaling-law side are making the same claim from opposite ends: the right experimental unit is the (architecture, optimizer) pair, and the field's habit of ablating one while holding the other fixed is quietly mis-measuring both. If that holds up, a lot of published architecture comparisons run under AdamW are testing the optimizer as much as the idea.

The second thread is about where capability actually comes from, and it is getting mechanistic. "Why Larger Models Learn More" argues that the reason bigger models pick up rare, complex skills is not extra representational room but *reduced gradient interference*: once a large model has enough neurons for the common tasks, those tasks' gradients go weak and stop overwriting the slowly-accumulating features of the rare ones. That is the training-dynamics complement to the superposition account of scaling from May 3rd, which explained the *geometry* that lets many features coexist. And it rhymes with a pattern the wiki has been compounding for two months: the useful signal is sparse and fragile. TIP showed only about 10% of distillation tokens carry signal; LongAct showed long-context gradient signal concentrates in a few high-magnitude activations; today's How-LoRA-Remembers shows memorization is gated at a hard p > 0.5 threshold and that most tokens are already above it and waste budget. Every one of these papers ends at the same instruction: find the sparse, fragile, sub-threshold minority and spend there.

The third thread is cheaper supervision and cheaper routing. CorVer and RUBRIC-ARROW between them carve up the post-math-and-code reward problem into "checkable against a corpus" and "judged against rubrics," both deliberately avoiding an expensive neural verifier in the RL loop. When Cloud Agents Meet Device Agents and RePoT both reach a conclusion the routing pages have been accumulating all month: throwing the biggest model at every step is a worse policy than a trajectory-aware one. Cloud/device says more frontier compute is not monotonically better and adds edge *energy* as a routing constraint nobody had formalized; RePoT shows that the right *recovery* move is capability-dependent, and that picking it wrong can actively lower accuracy. The frontier is getting cheaper to supervise and smarter about when not to spend.

## Deep Dives

### Parallax: a better attention read, unlocked by the optimizer

> Softmax attention is a local *average*. Parallax makes it a local *line*, deletes the solver that made that idea unscalable, matches FlashAttention 2/3 on its kernel, and then finds the gain only appears under Muon.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29157) · [Wiki summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-05-31-parallax-local-linear-attention.md)

```
Softmax attention   : value ≈ local CONSTANT fit   (kernel-weighted mean of values)
Local Linear Attn   : value ≈ local LINEAR  fit     (better bias-variance for recall)

Exact LLA (too slow):  query ─► solve Σx = μ per query (conjugate-gradient loop) ─► estimate
Parallax (no solver):  query ─► softmax read ─┐
                       learned probe(q) ──────┼─► affine combine ─► estimate
                       + kernel raises arithmetic intensity (compute-bound, beats FlashAttn 2/3)
```

**What is it about?**
Attention, the operation where each token reads a weighted mix of other tokens' values, has stayed structurally the same for years. Viewed through the test-time-regression lens, softmax attention is a local *constant* estimator: it returns a kernel-weighted average of nearby values. Local Linear Attention (LLA) upgrades that to a local *linear* estimator, fitting a line instead of a point, which nonparametric statistics says gives a strictly better bias-variance tradeoff for associative memory. Parallax is the version of LLA that can actually be pretrained at LLM scale.

**What problem does it solve?**
Exact LLA needs a linear-system solve for every query (an iterative conjugate-gradient loop), which is both slow, roughly `T·L·d` memory traffic versus `2·L·d` for one normal attention pass, and numerically unstable. That is why nobody had scaled a provably-more-expressive attention read before. Most efficient-attention work instead chases *cheaper* attention (linear attention, Mamba-style state-space models) and accepts a quality hit on in-context retrieval. Parallax goes the other way: a more expressive read, made affordable.

**What is the core novelty?**
It deletes the solver. Instead of solving `Σx = μ` per query, Parallax learns an extra query-like projector that probes the key-value covariance directly, turning an iterative numerical routine into one learned forward operation. That also defines a whole family of attention mechanisms indexed by bandwidth, probe construction, and affine structure, with plain softmax sitting at one corner. The hardware-aware kernel deliberately raises arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved) so attention becomes compute-bound rather than memory-bound, which is how it keeps up with FlashAttention despite doing more math.

**Key takeaways**
- Decode kernel **matches or beats FlashAttention 2/3** across batch sizes and context lengths.
- Pretrained at 0.6B and 1.7B: perplexity gains throughout pretraining that **survive both parameter-matched and compute-matched controls** (a genuine Pareto win, not a parameter-count artifact).
- **Muon unlocks Parallax; AdamW does not**, the first clean architecture-optimizer codesign result for attention.
- Reframes softmax attention as the constant-fit corner of a larger estimator family.

**Gaps in the study**
Largest scale is 1.7B, far below where long-context retrieval degradation actually bites, and no needle-in-haystack or RULER numbers are reported, which is the exact axis where linear-attention substitutes usually collapse. The Muon-unlocks-Parallax effect is reported empirically with no mechanistic explanation of why this optimizer matters for this architecture.

**Industrial implication**
If the (architecture, optimizer) pairing is real, every attention-variant comparison run under AdamW is suspect, and the near-term practical move is to re-benchmark candidate efficient-attention designs under Muon before declaring a winner. A FlashAttention-competitive kernel that also improves quality at matched compute is the rare efficiency result that does not ask for a quality tradeoff, but it has to clear the long-context retrieval bar at 7B+ before anyone ships it.

### Why Larger Models Learn More: it is interference, not just room

> Bigger models do not learn rare skills because they have more space. They learn them because, once the common tasks are handled, those tasks stop overwriting the rare ones.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29548) · [Wiki summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-05-31-why-larger-models-capacity-interference.md)

```
Finite neurons, tasks compete:
  SMALL  common task (frequent) ─► grabs neurons, strong gradient persists
         rare task   (infrequent) ─► overwritten each step ─► NOT learned (even if expressible)
  LARGE  common task ─► enough neurons ─► gradient goes WEAK once learned
         rare task   ─► residual capacity ─► features accumulate, not overwritten ─► learned
  Lever: gradient interference between tasks (larger ⇒ lower ⇒ rare tasks survive)
```

**What is it about?**
It is a data-centric theory of why scaling works, aimed at the specific puzzle that larger models acquire skills smaller ones never do even with unlimited data. The argument is that neurons are a finite resource tasks compete for, and the competition, not raw capacity, decides what gets learned.

**What problem does it solve?**
Scaling laws tell you loss falls as a power of size but say nothing about *what* the extra size buys, which is exactly what you need to make model-sizing and data-mixture decisions. The paper reframes the question from "how much does loss drop" to "which parts of the data distribution become learnable at all," and gives a measurable cause.

**What is the core novelty?**
Identifying *reduced gradient interference* as the operative mechanism, and isolating it. The synthetic setup is built so every task is individually expressible at small scale, which proves the small model fails not from lack of capacity but because high-frequency tasks' gradients keep overwriting the rare features. The OLMo 4M-to-4B sweep then reproduces the synthetic story in a real pretraining run, with direct measurement of gradient interference and feature embedding.

**Key takeaways**
- Power-law scaling implies a larger model learns distribution slices a smaller one cannot, **even at infinite data**.
- Small models fail on rare-and-complex tasks **even when those tasks fit in their capacity**, because common-task gradients overwrite them.
- The escape is **reduced interference**: large-model common-task gradients weaken once learned, sparing rare-task features.
- Confirmed on OLMo 4M→4B: only larger models learn the infrequent/complex tasks and show measurably less inter-task interference.

**Gaps in the study**
The synthetic mixture is hand-built with clean frequency × complexity cells; real corpora do not decompose that way, so the jump from "rare synthetic task" to a real capability is asserted, not shown. The runs stop at 4B, well short of the frontier where the interesting emergent skills appear, and the mechanism does not predict *which* real capability sits above *which* size threshold.

**Industrial implication**
If rare-task learning is interference-limited rather than capacity-limited, then "just make it bigger" is the expensive solution to a problem that may have cheaper fixes: a data re-weighting or optimizer that reduces common-task gradient dominance could let a smaller model acquire tail skills it otherwise misses. For anyone paying for tail capabilities, that is the difference between buying a bigger model and re-tuning a training recipe.

### How LoRA Remembers: a memory law with a hard recall threshold

> Memorization in a fine-tuned adapter is not gradual. A token crosses a sharp p > 0.5 line into verbatim recall, and most of your training budget is spent on tokens that already crossed it.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30260) · [Wiki summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-05-31-parametric-memory-law-lora.md)

```
Parametric Memory Law:  ΔL ∝ (effective params)^a · (sequence length)^b   ← smooth power law
Token-level transition: p(token) < 0.5 → not reliably recalled
                        p(token) > 0.5 → verbatim recall under greedy decoding ✓
MemFT:  p already > 0.5 → stop spending budget here
        p still   < 0.5 → redistribute budget here  ⇒ higher fidelity, lower cost
```

**What is it about?**
LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, the standard cheap way to fine-tune an LLM by training a small low-rank weight delta) is used everywhere to inject new knowledge, but its memory behavior has only ever been studied through downstream task scores. This paper uses LoRA as a controlled capacity probe and measures exact parametric memory directly.

**What problem does it solve?**
There has been no quantitative answer to "how much can a given LoRA rank and training length memorize" or "when does a fact cross from approximately-learned to reliably-reproduced." Without that, training budget gets spread uniformly across tokens, over-training easy ones and under-training the rare hard ones that actually fail.

**What is the core novelty?**
Two results at two scales. A smooth Parametric Memory Law linking loss reduction to effective parameters and sequence length. And a sharp token-level phase transition: a prediction probability above 0.5 is a *sufficient* condition for verbatim greedy recall. The threshold is the actionable part, and MemFT acts on it by redistributing the training budget toward sub-threshold tokens instead of wasting it on tokens already above the line.

**Key takeaways**
- **Parametric Memory Law**: loss reduction follows a power law in effective parameters and sequence length.
- **p > 0.5 phase transition**: memorization is gated, not gradual, and the threshold is sufficient for verbatim greedy recall.
- **MemFT** redistributes budget toward sub-threshold tokens, raising memory fidelity and efficiency over uniform training.
- LoRA is used as a clean probe of *exact* parametric memory rather than judged by downstream proxies.

**Gaps in the study**
Verbatim greedy recall is the easiest possible memory target; whether the threshold survives paraphrastic recall, sampling, or multi-fact composition is untested. Catastrophic forgetting of the base model's prior knowledge under MemFT's reallocated budget is not measured, which matters because the whole point of LoRA memory updates is to add without destroying.

**Industrial implication**
This turns LoRA memory budgeting from guesswork into a measurable engineering quantity: you can predict how much an adapter will memorize and target the budget at the tokens that have not yet locked in. For RAG-versus-fine-tune decisions and for knowledge-editing pipelines, a quantitative recall threshold is exactly the missing instrument.

### CorVer: verifiable rewards for facts, without a neural verifier

> RLVR works on math and code because correctness is checkable. CorVer makes factual QA checkable too, using Wikipedia co-occurrence statistics instead of an expensive judge model, and trains 4.8 to 8.4x faster.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29648) · [Wiki summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-05-31-corver-corpus-grounded-process-rewards.md)

```
Prior fine-grained reward: trace ─► [NLI / LLM judge / KB pipeline] ─► sentence rewards
                                     └ slow, unreliable on rare entities, costly at RL scale ┘
CorVer: trace ─► split sentences ─► 0.5B extractor pulls facts ─► Wikipedia co-occurrence lookup
        ─► sentence credit ─► simple alignment ─► token-level advantages ─► RL update
        (one corpus lookup per sentence, no neural verifier in the loop)
```

**What is it about?**
RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, the dominant recipe for reasoning models) needs a mechanical correctness check. Math and code have one; factual question answering does not. CorVer (Corpus Verify) builds a cheap process reward for factual QA by scoring each generated sentence against Wikipedia co-occurrence statistics.

**What problem does it solve?**
The fine-grained reward options for facts, NLI models, LLM judges, knowledge-base pipelines, are too expensive to run on every sentence of every rollout and are least reliable exactly on rare-entity facts, where the signal matters most. CorVer takes the neural verifier out of the hot loop entirely.

**What is the core novelty?**
The reward is a corpus statistic, not a learned model. Co-occurrence in Wikipedia stands in for "this combination of claims is consistent with recorded facts," and it is one lookup. The whole pipeline is a 0.5B extractor plus the lookup, and a simple alignment maps sentence-level credit to the token-level advantages standard policy optimization needs.

**Key takeaways**
- Replaces neural verifiers with **Wikipedia co-occurrence**; only a 0.5B extractor plus one lookup per sentence.
- Improves over the raw baseline in **all 30 (model, benchmark) cells**, average **+4.1 pp on TriviaQA**.
- Beats four neural-verifier baselines in **18 of 20** feasible cells while training **4.8–8.4x faster**.
- Targets the rare-entity regime where learned verifiers are weakest.

**Gaps in the study**
Co-occurrence is a popularity-and-coverage signal, so it is weakest on the long tail and on very recent facts, the same blind spot The Decoder's LiveBrowseComp coverage today flagged in search agents. And co-occurrence rewards correlation, not truth, so a fluent claim pairing commonly-co-occurring entities could be over-rewarded, a reward-hacking surface the paper does not stress-test.

**Industrial implication**
A cheap, deterministic factual reward makes RLVR-on-facts affordable at scale, which is the unlock for training models to be reliably factual rather than merely fluent. The likely production shape is hybrid: use the cheap co-occurrence prior on most sentences and spend an expensive verifier only where the prior is ambiguous, which is itself a routing decision.

### When Cloud Agents Meet Device Agents: routing gets a power bill

> Splitting agent work between a phone and the cloud is not a binary choice. It is a three-way tradeoff of accuracy, dollars, and battery, and pouring in more frontier compute does not reliably help.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30102) · [Wiki summary](../../ai-routing/2026-05-31-hybrid-cloud-device-agents.md)

```
        accuracy ▲   ✦ cloud-heavy (high $, off-device energy)
                 │  ╱
   Pareto        │ ╱  operating point set by: agent placement (device/cloud),
   frontier      │╱   escalation triggers, division of labor
   device-heavy ✦┴────────────► cost / edge-energy
   Finding: more frontier compute ↛ monotonic accuracy; best point is TASK-DEPENDENT.
```

**What is it about?**
A systematic study of hybrid multi-agent systems that mix on-device small language models with cloud frontier models. It retrofits two representative multi-agent architectures for hybrid inference and measures how each design choice moves the operating point.

**What problem does it solve?**
On-device agents are real now, and the obvious pattern is keep cheap work local, call the cloud when needed. But "when needed" has been hand-tuned per app, with no principles for trading accuracy against dollars against the edge device's battery and thermal budget. The paper turns that folk practice into a measured frontier.

**What is the core novelty?**
Treating hybrid design as a three-objective optimization, accuracy × monetary cost × edge *energy*, rather than a cloud-versus-device binary. Edge energy as a first-class routing constraint is the under-appreciated part: a call that is cheap in dollars can be expensive in battery and heat, and those are different budgets.

**Key takeaways**
- Small on-device models **do** benefit from selective cloud assistance, the hybrid premise holds.
- The optimal topology is **highly task-dependent**; no single architecture dominates.
- **More frontier compute does not consistently improve performance.**
- Accuracy, cost, and edge energy are tightly coupled; optimizing one alone mis-sizes the rest.

**Gaps in the study**
Only two MAS architectures, a thin base for "general design principles," and the task-dependence finding undercuts generalization by its own logic. Energy numbers are device- and model-specific. Crucially there is no learned router: the study maps the frontier but decides placement by ablation rather than a policy that *finds* the optimum.

**Industrial implication**
For anyone shipping on-device agents (phones, wearables, edge boxes), this says the naive "escalate to the big model" policy leaves performance and battery on the table, and that the routing policy has to be re-derived per task class. Energy-aware routing is the missing primitive, and edge deployment is where it stops being optional.

### RePoT: recover a failed plan from the last verified step

> When a Program-of-Thought plan hits one bad action, the usual move is to throw the whole trajectory away. RePoT keeps the verified prefix and fixes only the tail, for at most one extra LLM call.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30052) · [Wiki summary](../../agentic-systems/2026-05-31-repot-recoverable-program-of-thought.md)

```
PoT (brittle):  LLM ─► plan ─► run ─► first invalid action ─► whole trajectory dead ✗
RePoT:          LLM ─► plan ─► deterministic replay finds verified prefix [step1..K ✓ | K+1 ✗]
                          ─► ONE LLM call resumes from verified prefix ─► success
                          (cost ≤ 1 extra call, only on the ~14% where PoT fails)
Adaptive RePoT: long verified prefix ─► SUFFIX REPAIR ; short prefix ─► FRESH RETRY
```

**What is it about?**
Program-of-Thought (PoT) has an LLM emit a Python program that prints a plan of primitive actions. One invalid action silently kills the trajectory. RePoT adds a deterministic environment replay that finds the first invalid step, then one LLM call that resumes from the verified prefix.

**What problem does it solve?**
One-shot PoT has no graceful failure: a single bad action discards every correct step before it, and naive retries re-pay for the whole trajectory. The field's default recovery signal, an error string ("that failed, try again"), turns out to be nearly useless.

**What is the core novelty?**
Separating *deterministic verified replay* (a non-LLM environment walk that localizes the exact first failure) from *one LLM repair call* (resume from the verified prefix). On the controlled Derail-550 benchmark, conditions with access to checkpoint *state* clear far higher than error-only feedback, proving the checkpoint state, not the specific suffix text, is the load-bearing recovery signal. Adaptive RePoT then routes between suffix-repair and fresh-retry based on verified-prefix length, because the right move is capability-dependent.

**Key takeaways**
- **+3 to +11 pp over PoT** across four closed-model configs on PuzzleZoo-775; peak **96.9% vs 86.3%** on gpt-5.4-mini-medium.
- Cost is **at most one extra LLM call**, only on the ~14% of problems where PoT fails.
- vs matched-budget retry: wins decisively on Gemini, ties GPT-medium/Claude, **loses on GPT-mini**, a capability-scaling pattern.
- Derail-550: checkpoint-information conditions clear ≥30% (GPT-medium) / ≥70% (Gemini) vs ≤3.1% for error-only feedback.

**Gaps in the study**
The capability-dependence is reported but not solved: Adaptive RePoT is preliminary and rule-based, and because RePoT *hurts* the weakest model, the dispatcher is required, not optional. Evaluation is on planning/puzzle environments with crisply-defined valid actions; transfer to open-ended tool-use agents, where transitions are not cleanly verifiable, is untested.

**Industrial implication**
For agent stacks that emit executable plans, deterministic checkpoint replay is a cheap, near-free reliability layer, one repair call on the small fraction of runs that fail. But it has to be gated by a capability-aware dispatcher, because applying repair to a weak model can make things worse, which makes "which recovery strategy" a real routing decision rather than an always-on feature.

## Connecting the Dots

The sharpest connection today is between Parallax and a paper from eight days ago. Parallax (the local-linear attention that needs Muon to realize its gains) and "Same Architecture, Optimizer-Induced Spectral Scaling Laws" (05-23, which found Muon scales rare-token representation rank at β=1.02 where AdamW stalls at β=0.44) are independently arguing the same thing: the optimizer determines what capacity an architecture actually delivers. The 05-23 paper made the claim from the scaling-law side using FFN eigenspectra; Parallax makes it from the architecture side by showing its attention variant is inert under AdamW and alive under Muon. Two papers, two methods, one conclusion, that the (architecture, optimizer) pair is the real unit of study. This now reads as a building pattern rather than a one-off, and it puts a question mark over any attention-variant comparison run under AdamW alone.

A second thread runs from "Why Larger Models Learn More" straight into "How LoRA Remembers," and back through two months of the wiki. The capacity paper says rare features are slowly-accumulating and easily overwritten by dominant common-task gradients; the LoRA paper says memorization is gated at a hard p > 0.5 threshold and that budget should be redistributed to the sub-threshold minority. One diagnoses the fragility of rare signal, the other prescribes where to spend to protect it. Both are the latest members of the wiki's strongest running pattern: TIP (04-16, only ~10% of distillation tokens carry signal), LongAct (04-18, long-context gradient signal concentrates in a few high-magnitude activations), and the Extrapolation Cliff (05-14, a sharp closed-form threshold past which on-policy distillation collapses). The field keeps rediscovering that the useful signal is sparse, locatable, and worth targeting, now from the angles of pretraining capacity and adapter memory.

The reward and routing papers compose into a third thread about spending less. CorVer (factual reward from corpus co-occurrence) and RUBRIC-ARROW (subjective reward from learned rubrics) split the post-math-and-code reward problem in two, and both deliberately keep an expensive neural verifier out of the RL loop, the same cost-cutting instinct NeMo-RL's speculative decoding (04-30) applied to rollout generation. Meanwhile When Cloud Agents Meet Device Agents and RePoT both arrive at the routing pages' accumulating verdict that more compute is not monotonically better: the cloud/device study says escalating to the frontier model everywhere leaves the accuracy/cost/energy frontier, and RePoT shows the right *recovery* move is capability-dependent and can backfire if mis-chosen. That is the same "routing is the policy" claim the May cluster (Conductor, CaRE, MISA, then MobileMoE and MiniMax-M2) has been building, now extended with two axes those papers lacked: edge energy as a constraint, and recovery strategy as a routed decision with real downside risk.
