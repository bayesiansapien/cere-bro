## Deep Dives

### Meta AI's Perfect Physics Olympiad Score

> A Meta model scored 30/30 on the Asian Physics Olympiad theoretical exam, tying the top three human contestants. The result is striking. The refusal to say which model is the story underneath it.

**Source:** Twitter/X (AI at Meta)
**Links:** [AI at Meta](https://x.com/AIatMeta)

**What is it about?**
Meta submitted a model to the Asian Physics Olympiad's theoretical exam and reported a perfect score of 30/30, tying the top three student contestants. The Asian Physics Olympiad is a serious competition, and the theoretical exam rewards multi-step derivation and physical reasoning, not recall. A perfect score is a genuine reasoning result. The catch, flagged immediately by observers including HuggingFace's eliebakouch: Meta did not say whether this was Muse Spark 1.1 (their public model) or an unreleased internal model.

**What problem does it solve?**
Physics olympiad problems are a clean test of multi-step scientific reasoning under a rubric that humans also take, so the human-contestant comparison is meaningful. A perfect score demonstrates that frontier models can now match elite human physics students on formal theoretical problems. This extends the pattern from math olympiad results into physics, which requires modeling physical systems, not just symbolic manipulation.

**What is the core novelty?**
The result itself (perfect physics olympiad score) is the headline, but the methodological gap is the substance. Without knowing the model, the community cannot calibrate: a 30/30 from a public model is a shipped capability, while a 30/30 from an internal research model is a lab demo. The wiki's rule is to trust the abstract over the press release; here there is no abstract, only a tweet, so the correct posture is skepticism about generalization until Meta names the model and releases the transcripts.

**Key takeaways**
- Meta model scored 30/30 on the Asian Physics Olympiad theoretical exam, tying top three humans.
- Meta did not disclose which model (Muse Spark 1.1 or internal), which caps how much can be concluded.
- Physics olympiad reasoning is a step beyond math olympiad: it requires physical-system modeling, not just symbolic derivation.
- Fits the pattern of frontier models reaching elite-human level on formal reasoning competitions.

**Gaps in the study**
No model disclosed, no transcripts, no methodology. Whether this was a single best-of-N run, an ensemble, or a clean single-shot is unknown. Competition results without released solutions are the weakest form of capability evidence, because they cannot be independently audited. The APhO committee allowing participation is noted, but the evaluation conditions (tools, time, retries) are not.

**Industrial implication**
If Meta has a model that scores 30/30 on physics olympiad problems and it is an internal model, then the public-model gap (Muse Spark 1.1) understates Meta's actual frontier position, which matters for the competitive read on Meta after a turbulent year. If it is Muse Spark 1.1, then Meta's public model is stronger on scientific reasoning than its market position suggests. Either way, the physics-reasoning capability is real, and it strengthens the case that frontier models are now scientific-reasoning tools, not just coding and chat tools.

**Research angle**
The interesting research question is what physics olympiad problems test that math olympiad problems do not: the ability to set up a physical model (identify forces, conserved quantities, approximations) before the math starts. That setup step is a form of problem-framing that is closer to real scientific work than symbolic derivation. If Meta's model genuinely does the framing rather than pattern-matching to memorized problem types, that is evidence for the kind of scientific reasoning that the Kurate "AI scientists produce results without reasoning scientifically" paper (cs.AI #7, rating 8.5) says is still missing. The two claims are in direct tension and worth tracking together.

### Grok 4.5 Ranks #2 on FrontierSWE

> A week after reaching the intelligence frontier, Grok 4.5 gets a second coding data point: second on FrontierSWE, behind only Fable 5.

**Source:** Twitter/X (Proximal HQ)
**Links:** [Proximal](https://x.com/ProximalHQ)

**What is it about?**
FrontierSWE is a software-engineering agent benchmark. Grok 4.5 ranked second, behind only Claude Fable 5 and ahead of Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, and GLM-5.2. This is a second independent-ish coding data point after the July 9 Artificial Analysis coding-parity claim, and it is a stronger result: ahead of GPT-5.5, not merely on par.

**Key takeaways**
- Grok 4.5 second on FrontierSWE, behind only Fable 5.
- Ahead of Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, and GLM-5.2 on this benchmark.
- Second coding data point in a week, strengthening the July 9 frontier-arrival claim.
- Still needs harness normalization: FrontierSWE results depend on the agent scaffold, per The Harness Effect (July 11 weekly).

**Gaps in the study**
FrontierSWE ranking is from Proximal, a single source, and the harness used is not specified. Per The Harness Effect finding that orchestration moves cost more than the model menu, a coding benchmark without harness disclosure cannot cleanly separate Grok 4.5's model capability from its scaffold. That said, two coding data points in a week is a stronger signal than one.

**Industrial implication**
If Grok 4.5 is genuinely the second-best coding agent, the SpaceXAI-Cursor partnership becomes a real competitive threat to Anthropic's Claude Code franchise. The July 8 SemiAnalysis analysis identified coding as Anthropic's profit engine. A credible #2 coding model at lower cost is the most direct pressure on that engine.

## Global View

**The capability-vs-permission gap widened on a single day.** Meta AI scored a perfect 30/30 on physics olympiad reasoning while New York froze new AI data center construction statewide. That juxtaposition is the story: models are reaching elite-human scientific reasoning at the exact moment that the physical infrastructure to run them is hitting political limits. The July 12 Interconnects piece ("6 months to live for open models") argued regulation is coming for frontier open-weights; New York's data-center freeze is the infrastructure-side version of the same clamp. The compute buildout (trillion-won semiconductor checks, July 6) assumed unlimited permission to build. The permission is now contested.

**The coding frontier is consolidating into a three-model race, and Grok 4.5 is holding its position.** Grok 4.5's #2 on FrontierSWE (today) is the second coding data point in a week, after the July 9 Artificial Analysis coding-parity claim. The ranking (behind Fable 5, ahead of Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, GLM-5.2) is a stronger result than parity. But both data points share the same weakness that The Harness Effect (July 11) exposed: coding benchmarks measure model-plus-harness, and neither Grok result disclosed its harness. The consistent caveat across every coding claim this month is that until benchmarks normalize the harness, "best coding model" is an untestable claim. NVIDIA's Nemotron open-model push and Kilo's OS-level sandbox are the same week's reminder that the deployment layer (open weights, sandboxing) is where the practical action is, regardless of which model tops the leaderboard.
