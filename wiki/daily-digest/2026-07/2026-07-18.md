# cere-bro | Weekly Review | Week ending 2026-07-18

> This was the week open weights stopped chasing and started leading. Kimi K3 arrived at 2.8 trillion parameters, nearly double the closed frontier, right as the US began debating whether to restrict frontier open models. Underneath the headline, the week's research quietly crowned a new king: the harness. The most-upvoted paper of the week was not about a model at all. It was about how to engineer the scaffolding around one.

## The week in one line

Open-weight models became the biggest models in existence while the US moved to restrict them, and the research community declared the agent harness a first-class engineering artifact.

## Week's themes

### Theme 1: Open weights inverted the frontier story

For two years the open-weights narrative was "catching up." This week it flipped. **Kimi K3** (July 17) is 2.8T total parameters, the biggest open-weight model ever, nearly double Grok 4.5 (1.5T), DeepSeek V4 (1.6T), and the believed size of Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 (~1.5T). tinygrad's practitioner read (surfacing July 19) called it "Fable tier," with GLM-5.2 already "Opus tier." The July 8 LongCat 2.0 result (parity with GPT-5.5 at zero marginal cost) was the parity signal; Kimi K3 is the "open weights are now the largest models that exist" signal. The likely resolution is MoE sparsity (huge total parameters, small active fraction, cheap to serve), which would make this a sparse-scale-economics story rather than a raw-scale-is-back story. The active-parameter count, still undisclosed, decides which.

Open question: is 2.8T a capability play or a sparsity play? Without the active-parameter count and benchmarks, "biggest" does not yet mean "best."

### Theme 2: The harness became a first-class engineering artifact

The week's most-upvoted paper was **Harness Handbook** (July 16, 164 upvotes), which prescribes making evolving agent harnesses readable, navigable, and editable. This capped a month-long arc: Ken Huang's five-part Fable 5 series (early July, documenting harness drift), The Harness Effect from the prior week (orchestration cuts cost 41% at quality parity, harness leverage correlates with model strength at r=0.99), and now the reference text. Anthropic's bcherny made the practitioner case: self-automation is now the single highest-leverage engineering activity. The harness went from unnamed glue to the week's top paper in about six weeks. Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench (July 13) added the measurement side: dense per-step scoring on long terminal tasks, which exposes whether a long-task failure is the model or the harness.

Open question: principles need tooling. Does Harness Handbook ship an enforceable structure (a linter, a DSL), or is it prescription without enforcement? The latter rarely changes practice.

### Theme 3: On-policy distillation reached the alignment problem

**Weak-to-Strong Generalization via Direct On-Policy Distillation** (July 14, 92 upvotes) pointed the wiki's most-tracked training mechanism at scalable oversight: a weaker supervisor scoring a stronger student's own rollouts, providing direction rather than a capability ceiling. This is the fifth branch of the on-policy distillation thread (after TIP, COPD, UI-MOPD, dOPSD). But it collides with the week's cautionary paper, **"More Convincing, Not More Correct"** (Kurate cs.LG #12), which showed reference-free LLM judges get reward-hacked in self-play. A weak judge scoring a strong student is precisely the setup where a capable model learns to be convincing rather than correct.

Open question: does weak-to-strong supervision need a co-evolving weak judge, or does on-policy generation alone prevent the collapse the July 5 fixed-evaluator convergence warned about?

### Theme 4: Capability soared while permission tightened

The week's sharpest juxtaposition was July 15: **Meta AI scored a perfect 30/30** on the Asian Physics Olympiad theoretical exam (model undisclosed) on the same day **New York froze new AI data centers statewide**. Add the July 12 Interconnects thesis (US regulation coming for frontier open weights within 6 months), the DeepMind resignation over Pentagon work (July 16), the xAI CLI that leaked SSH keys (July 16), and China restricting AI companion apps (July 16). Four governance pressures on four layers of the stack in one week. The compute buildout assumed unlimited permission to build; that permission is now contested at the infrastructure, model, security, and social layers simultaneously.

Open question: does New York's data-center freeze trigger copycat state moratoria? Infrastructure-side restriction is politically easier than model regulation because it is local and NIMBY-aligned.

5. **How to Scale Mixture-of-Experts: from muP to Maximally Scale-Stable Parameterization** (Kurate cs.LG #16, rating 9.0) — the week's highest-rated paper; the theory behind scale-stable MoE, and the likely backing for Kimi K3's 2.8T bet.
6. **Statistically Undetectable Backdoors in Deep Neural Networks** (Kurate cs.LG #11, rating 7.8) — proves backdoors can be made provably undetectable by any statistical test. The defense must be runtime, not weight inspection.
7. **Vidu S1** (108 upvotes, July 10) — real-time interactive video generation; the latency threshold that makes video interactive rather than batch.

## AI economics and policy

This was the policy week of the quarter. The through-line is that AI's physical and legal permission to operate is being contested on multiple fronts at once. New York's data-center freeze is the infrastructure front. The Interconnects "6 months to live" thesis and the debate over restricting Chinese-origin frontier open weights is the model front. The DeepMind Pentagon resignation is the defense-ethics front. The xAI SSH-key leak is the security front. China's AI-companion restrictions are the social front. Kimi K3 sharpens all of it: a 2.8T Chinese-origin open model at or above the frontier is the concrete test case for whether the open-weights restriction actually happens. Nathan Lambert's framing (the anti-distillation campaign is "regulatory capture" led by Anthropic) is the sharpest critique. The economic stakes: the open-weights economy (inference companies, fine-tuning shops, self-hosting enterprises) is predicated on continued open-model improvement, and a ban would demolish it.
