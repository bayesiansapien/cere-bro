## The Big Picture

Yesterday the wiki named the dominant design move of the moment: stop treating everything equally, find the sparse load-bearing part, and spend only there. Four papers did it at four layers (experts, attention, distillation tokens, RL rollouts). Today that instinct did not just repeat, it matured in two specific ways. First, it reached parts of the stack it had not touched. VideoMLA goes below every recent video-cache paper and rewrites the per-head key-value layout itself. Draft-OPD reaches into speculative decoding and fixes the draft model's training objective rather than its architecture. LongAttnComp moves the same "keep only what carries the query" logic to the prefill input. The efficiency frontier is no longer about discovering the principle, it is about finding the last places the principle has not been applied.

Second, the principle grew a new dimension: time. The sharpest single paper today is "Not Only Where, But When," which points out that all the credit-allocation work answers *where* to put the learning signal but uses a rule frozen for all of training. It schedules that rule over training instead, and shows the payoff in preserved policy entropy. That is the same idea the wiki has been tracking, now with a clock attached. Two of today's reasoning papers rhyme with it from the inference side: Geometric Latent Reasoning and the chunk-level process-scorer work both produce substantially shorter outputs, which is the inference-time version of not wasting effort uniformly. Where to spend, when to spend, how long to spend, the field is optimizing all three axes of compute allocation at once.

The third thread is quieter but worth flagging: speculative decoding is leaving adolescence. The 2026 story so far was architectural, better draft heads (EAGLE3, DFlash, embedded MTP). Today both speculative papers ignore architecture. Draft-OPD changes the training objective, SPD changes the hardware scheduling. When a technique's gains migrate from "design a better module" to "train it right and schedule it well," that is the signature of a maturing subfield, and it usually means the remaining wins are larger and harder.

## Deep Dives

### VideoMLA: collapse the KV layout, not just its contents

> Every recent video-diffusion cache paper changed which tokens sit in the window. VideoMLA changes the thing underneath all of them, the per-head key-value layout, cutting KV memory 92.7% per layer, and it shows MLA works for a reason the field had wrong.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30351) · [Wiki summary](../../inference-efficiency/2026-06-02-videomla-low-rank-latent-kv-cache.md)

```
Per-head KV (baseline):  each head stores its own K, V  ─► huge streaming memory, latency-bound
VideoMLA (MLA):          shared LOW-RANK content latent  +  shared decoupled 3D-RoPE positional key
                         ─► 92.7% less per-token KV per layer ─► 1.23x throughput (B200)
Surprise:  pretrained video attention is NOT low-rank (99%-energy rank >> latent dim)
           the MLA BOTTLENECK, not the spectrum, sets effective rank
```

**What is it about?**
Long-rollout causal video diffusion (generating minute-scale video frame by frame) runs on a fixed-size sliding-window KV cache (the memory store that holds past attention computations so they are not recomputed). VideoMLA is the first study of Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA, compressing each attention head's keys and values into a shared low-rank latent) in video diffusion.

**What problem does it solve?**
The per-head KV layout is a dominant contributor to streaming memory and latency, and recent work left it untouched, innovating only in which tokens occupy the window. VideoMLA attacks the layout directly: it replaces per-head keys and values with one shared low-rank content latent plus a shared decoupled 3D-RoPE positional key, reducing per-token KV memory 92.7% at every cached layer.

**What is the core novelty?**
A genuine correction to MLA folklore. In language models MLA is justified by a low-rank spectral assumption, but VideoMLA shows pretrained video attention is *not* low-rank (its 99%-energy effective rank sits far above any practical latent dimension), and MLA still works. The reason: the MLA bottleneck dimension, not the pretrained spectrum, determines the effective rank. Both spectral and random initialization fill nearly the full rank budget from step zero, and training adapts within that budget rather than approximating the spectrum.

**Key takeaways**
- 92.7% per-token KV memory reduction at every cached layer; 1.23x throughput on a single B200.
- Matches short-horizon streaming baselines on VBench and scores best overall at long horizons among evaluated methods.
- Retains quality at compression ratios where direct spectral approximation would predict large reconstruction error.
- The bottleneck-not-spectrum finding decouples MLA's success from the low-rank story it is usually sold with.

**Gaps in the study**
Evaluated on video diffusion only; the claim that MLA transfers to other non-low-rank modalities is implied, not demonstrated. VBench has known blind spots for long-video failure modes (loopy or frozen output), and the paper does not report where a fixed latent budget breaks at extreme horizons.

**Industrial implication**
If the bottleneck dimension, not the attention spectrum, sets the achievable compression, then MLA-style KV reduction should generalize far beyond the language models it grew up in, including to modalities everyone assumed were too high-rank to compress. For anyone serving minute-scale video on Blackwell, a 92.7% KV cut at 1.23x throughput is the difference between one stream and several on the same card.

### Draft-OPD: fix the drafter's training, not its architecture

> The 2026 speculative-decoding playbook was "design a better draft head." Draft-OPD ignores architecture and fixes the training objective instead: the drafter was learning from the wrong distribution. Result, over 5x lossless speedup.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29343) · [Wiki summary](../../inference-efficiency/2026-06-02-draft-opd-speculative-draft-distillation.md)

```
SFT drafter:  learn from FIXED target trajectories ─► plateau (judged on its OWN blocks at decode)
Draft-OPD:    target-assisted rollout for stable continuation
              + replay drafting FROM verification-exposed error positions
              ─► learn from feedback on accepted AND rejected proposals
              ─► 5x lossless · +23% vs EAGLE-3 · +13% vs DFlash
```

**What is it about?**
Speculative decoding speeds up LLM inference by pairing a slow target model with a small draft model whose proposed tokens the target verifies in parallel, committing the verified prefix losslessly. Draft models are usually built by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the target's generated trajectories. Draft-OPD changes how the drafter is trained.

**What problem does it solve?**
SFT plateaus: the drafter's acceptance length (how many proposed tokens get accepted per round) stops improving. The cause is an offline-to-inference mismatch. In SFT the drafter learns from fixed target trajectories, but at decode time it is judged on blocks it proposed under its own policy. This is the exact covariate-shift problem the wiki has logged for student models, now showing up in the drafter.

**What is the core novelty?**
Making on-policy distillation (training the model on its own rollouts under teacher supervision) actually work for a draft model. Drafters cannot roll out full sequences reliably alone, and if the target assists the rollout the sequence follows the target distribution and the on-policy signal vanishes. Draft-OPD uses target-assisted rollout for stable continuations but replays drafting specifically from the positions where verification exposed an error, so the drafter learns from target feedback on both accepted and rejected proposals, focusing training on the draft-induced errors that cap acceptance.

**Key takeaways**
- Over 5x lossless acceleration for thinking (reasoning) models across diverse tasks.
- +23% over EAGLE-3 and +13% over DFlash, the two SFT-built baselines.
- Learns from rejected proposals, not just accepted tokens, by replaying from error positions.
- Confirms the offline-to-inference mismatch is the real ceiling on SFT-built drafters.

**Gaps in the study**
Reported on thinking models; whether the 5x holds for non-reasoning workloads with shorter, less branchy outputs is untested. The cost of the target-assisted rollout plus error-position replay during training is not fully characterized against plain SFT's cheaper pipeline.

**Industrial implication**
Speculative decoding is the default serving acceleration for low-concurrency reasoning inference, and a 23% acceptance-length gain over EAGLE-3 with no architecture change is a drop-in upgrade for anyone already running a draft model. The deeper signal: the next speedups in this area come from training and scheduling, not new draft modules.

### Speculative Pipeline Decoding: zero idle stages

> Multi-token prediction makes drafting harder the further ahead it guesses, and leaves the pipeline half-idle. SPD predicts one token per pipeline stage from aggregated features, in parallel with the target, so no stage ever waits.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30852) · [Wiki summary](../../inference-efficiency/2026-06-02-spd-speculative-pipeline-decoding.md)

```
Multi-token-prediction SD:  draft k ahead serially ─► difficulty grows with depth, BUBBLES in pipeline
SPD:  split target into n pipeline stages ─► n tokens in flight
      speculation module aggregates intermediate features ACROSS depths, runs in PARALLEL with target step
      ─► bounded difficulty · higher acceptance · ZERO bubbles
```

**What is it about?**
Speculative Pipeline Decoding (SPD) is a speculative-decoding framework built around pipeline parallelism (splitting a model across stages so different stages process different tokens at once). It partitions the target LLM into n pipeline stages and processes n tokens in parallel.

**What problem does it solve?**
Mainstream speculative decoding relies on multi-token prediction, where one module guesses several future tokens. The further ahead it predicts, the harder the guess and the more serial latency the drafting adds. In single-sequence decoding, pipeline-parallel hardware also sits half-idle (the "bubble" problem).

**What is the core novelty?**
A speculation module that aggregates intermediate features across different pipeline depths to predict the next token, executing strictly in parallel with the target model's pipeline step. Because each prediction is short-range (one token per stage) and runs concurrently with the target, SPD gets bounded prediction difficulty, higher acceptance rates, and zero latency bubbles at once.

**Key takeaways**
- Reframes speculation as filling pipeline stages rather than predicting many tokens ahead.
- Bounded difficulty plus higher acceptance from cross-depth feature aggregation.
- Zero pipeline bubbles in single-sequence decode, the classic pipeline-parallel inefficiency.
- Claims significantly higher theoretical speedup than mainstream baselines; code released.

**Gaps in the study**
The headline is theoretical speedup; real wall-clock gains depend on stage-balancing and inter-stage communication, which the abstract does not quantify. Pipeline parallelism helps most at low concurrency, so the benefit at high batch sizes is unclear.

**Industrial implication**
Draft-OPD and SPD are complementary: one raises how many proposed tokens get accepted, the other removes the serial cost and idle hardware of producing them. A serving stack could adopt both. For multi-GPU low-concurrency reasoning serving, zero-bubble speculation is a direct utilization win.

### LongAttnComp: a trained scorer beats training-free compression

> Training-free context compressors fall apart on code reasoning. LongAttnComp fine-tunes a small cross-attention scorer instead and matches full-context accuracy on 100k-token code-debug while transferring across four models from three families.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01336) · [Wiki summary](../../inference-efficiency/2026-06-02-longattncomp-context-compression.md)

```
Full context (100k+):    prefill ALL tokens ─► quadratic prefill, the bottleneck
Training-free attn comp: drop low-attention tokens ─► big gaps on code reasoning
LongAttnComp:  fine-tuned cross-attention SCORER + token chunking + token-budget top-p
               + positional reordering + format-agnostic query parser
   Stage 1: NIAH retrieval foundation ─► Stage 2: + multi-hop & reasoning
   ─► matches/exceeds FULL-context on InfiniteBench Code-Debug · transfers across 4 models / 3 families
```

**What is it about?**
As applications push past 100k-token inputs, the cost of prefill (processing the whole input before the model can answer) becomes the binding constraint. Context compression drops input tokens to cut that cost while preserving accuracy. LongAttnComp is a long-context adaptation of attention-based compression.

**What problem does it solve?**
Existing training-free attention-based compressors leave substantial gaps on demanding tasks, especially code reasoning, where naive attention heuristics discard tokens that matter. LongAttnComp closes that gap.

**What is the core novelty?**
Replacing the training-free heuristic with a lightweight, fine-tuned cross-attention scoring layer, plus a recipe: token-level chunking, a token-budget top-p selection algorithm (keep the highest-scoring tokens up to a budget), positional reordering, and a format-agnostic query parser. A two-stage fine-tuning recipe builds a retrieval foundation from needle-in-a-haystack data, then extends it with multi-hop and reasoning data.

**Key takeaways**
- Matches or exceeds full-context accuracy on InfiniteBench Code-Debug, where training-free baselines fall short.
- Transfers across four target models from three families, so it is not glued to one base.
- Stage 2 largely closes the multi-document-reasoning gap while preserving code-debug performance.
- Argues a trained scorer is necessary where attention heuristics fail.

**Gaps in the study**
The compressor itself needs training, undercutting the plug-and-play appeal of training-free methods, and the fine-tuning cost is not weighed against the prefill savings. Strongest results are on code-debug; how far the recipe generalizes to other hard long-context task types is partly open.

**Industrial implication**
For long-context code agents and retrieval pipelines, a transferable compressor that hits full-context accuracy at lower prefill cost is immediately useful. The broader signal is a reversal: after a year of training-free compression being the selling point, the hard tasks now seem to require a small trained component.

### κ-SwiGLU: let the gate sharpen when the router is confident

> SwiGLU's gate sharpness has been a fixed constant since it became standard. κ-SwiGLU ties it to routing confidence, so a mixture-of-experts model gates sharply when it is sure and broadly when it is not, nearly for free.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00761) · [Wiki summary](../../inference-efficiency/2026-06-02-kappa-swiglu-confidence-adaptive-moe.md)

```
Standard SwiGLU MoE:  gate sharpness κ = FIXED for every token, all training
κ-SwiGLU:             κ = learnable f(router logit)
   low routing confidence ─► smoother, broadly-active gate
   high routing confidence ─► sharper, selective gate
   ─► +mean CORE on 8–28-layer MoE (FineWeb-Edu), ~negligible params
```

**What is it about?**
SwiGLU is the standard gated activation in modern Transformer feed-forward layers. Its gate sharpness (how smooth or selective the gating function is) is normally fixed. κ-SwiGLU is a variant for mixture-of-experts models (MoE, where each token routes through a small subset of specialized sub-networks) that makes that sharpness adapt to each token's routing confidence.

**What problem does it solve?**
A fixed gate sharpness treats a token the router is highly confident about the same as one it is unsure about. That is the uniform-treatment default the efficiency field is now systematically attacking, here inside the expert gate.

**What is the core novelty?**
Parameterizing the SiLU gate's sharpness coefficient as a learnable function of the router logit, so each expert gate unit can interpolate between smooth/broadly-active and sharp/selective gating depending on token-level routing confidence.

**Key takeaways**
- Improves mean CORE performance across MoE Transformers from 8 to 28 layers on FineWeb-Edu.
- Negligible added parameters, small compute overhead.
- Confidence becomes the control signal for gate selectivity, per token.
- Code released.

**Gaps in the study**
Tested at modest depth on one dataset (FineWeb-Edu); no frontier-scale validation and no throughput numbers, so the inference cost of the learnable sharpness is unquantified. "Mean CORE improvement" is aggregate, without a per-task breakdown.

**Industrial implication**
This is the second MoE paper in two days arguing the machinery should adapt to routing confidence rather than treat tokens identically (after dMoE's block-coherent routing on 06-01). If confidence-adaptive gating holds at scale, it is a nearly free addition to any MoE training recipe. It also extends a pattern worth naming: a model's own confidence is becoming a generic allocation signal, used for KV-cache budget (Conf-KV, 05-30) and now gate sharpness.

### Geometric Latent Reasoning: reason in embedding space, answer in fewer steps

> Chain-of-thought makes models reason by emitting long token strings. GLR makes them walk a continuous path through embedding space instead, and the answers come out substantially shorter with no length penalty applied.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02248) · [Wiki summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-06-02-glr-geometric-latent-reasoning.md)

```
Explicit chain-of-thought:  emit discrete reasoning tokens step by step ─► long, expensive
GLR:  reasoning = a PATH through the pretrained token-embedding space
      lightweight transition head predicts iterative DIRECTION updates
      text CoT traces = anchors; continuous deviations allowed
      ─► reach correct answer in FEWER total steps (emergent, no length objective)
```

**What is it about?**
LLMs solve hard problems by generating long chains of explicit reasoning tokens, which is expensive, length-sensitive, and confined to discrete natural language. Latent reasoning (reasoning in continuous vectors instead of tokens) is an alternative, but no one knows what the intermediate latent states should be. GLR formulates latent reasoning as a geometric path-approximation problem inside the model's pretrained token-embedding space.

**What problem does it solve?**
It gives latent reasoning a concrete structure: a lightweight transition head predicts iterative direction updates in embedding space, using textual chain-of-thought traces as anchors while allowing continuous deviations from exact token embeddings. That answers the open "what should the latent states look like" question with "points along a path through embedding space."

**What is the core novelty?**
Treating reasoning as a trajectory in embedding space and learning to approximate it, then showing an emergent effect: replacing early explicit reasoning with continuous latent steps makes models reach correct answers in substantially fewer total generation steps, with no explicit length objective.

**Key takeaways**
- Latent reasoning formalized as geometric path approximation in the pretrained embedding space.
- Substantially shorter generations emerge with no length penalty applied.
- Exposes a new tradeoff between latent computation budget, output length, and accuracy.
- Evaluated on math reasoning with Qwen3 models.

**Gaps in the study**
Math reasoning only; whether continuous trajectories stay information-dense on open-ended reasoning is the key untested question. The transition head adds inference machinery whose overhead versus the token savings is not fully reported.

**Industrial implication**
Most inference-time-scaling work pushes reasoning *longer*; GLR pushes it shorter while keeping accuracy, which is the more deployment-friendly direction if it generalizes. Today it pairs with a second shorter-reasoning result (the chunk-level process-scorer paper below), making "shorter correct reasoning" a small but real same-day cluster.

### Not Only Where, But When: a clock for credit assignment

> Every RLVR credit-allocation method decides which tokens get the learning signal, then keeps that rule frozen for all of training. This paper schedules the rule over time, and the payoff shows up as preserved policy entropy.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25381) · [Wiki summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-06-02-temporal-scheduling-rlvr.md)

```
Standard RLVR:        one scalar reward to ALL tokens ─► heterogeneous behaviors blurred, entropy burned
Credit allocation:    reweight/select tokens — but criterion STAGNANT all training
Temporal scheduling:  WHEN you allocate credit matters as much as WHERE
   early:  prioritize targeted tokens w/ specific policy behaviors (trajectory percentiles)
   later:  gradually attenuate ─► general optimization
   ─► healthier policy-entropy evolution, more stable learning
```

**What is it about?**
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR, post-training driven by a checkable reward like whether a math answer verifies) broadcasts one scalar reward across all sampled tokens. Prior work addresses the resulting bluntness with credit allocation: token-level advantage reweighting and selective token optimization. This paper adds a temporal dimension to that allocation.

**What problem does it solve?**
Existing credit-allocation criteria are fixed throughout training, which limits how the policy can evolve. The authors argue *when* learning signals are scheduled is as important as *where* across tokens.

**What is the core novelty?**
Scheduling the credit-allocation criterion over the course of RLVR optimization: prioritize targeted tokens tied to specific policy behaviors early, then gradually attenuate toward general optimization. Trajectory percentiles provide a cheap, natural way to distinguish policy behaviors and pair well with the schedule. The analysis shows standard optimization sacrifices a lot of policy entropy when accommodating heterogeneous behaviors at once, while temporal scheduling keeps entropy healthier.

**Key takeaways**
- Adds a time axis to credit assignment: the allocation rule evolves across training.
- Trajectory percentiles distinguish policy behaviors with no extra model.
- Preserved policy entropy is the mechanism behind the stability gain.
- Consistent improvements on mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks.

**Gaps in the study**
The schedule shape (how fast to attenuate) appears hand-designed; sensitivity to that choice is not mapped. Gains are on reasoning benchmarks; transfer to alignment-style RLVR is untested.

**Industrial implication**
This is a cheap modification to existing RLVR pipelines (it rides on top of GRPO-style training via trajectory percentiles) that directly targets the entropy-collapse failure mode practitioners keep hitting. If it holds, the default credit-allocation rule in post-training stacks gains a schedule rather than staying a constant.

### Multi-Agent Computer Use: a DAG of agents that rewrites itself

> Computer-use agents are deployed as single serial workers and get stuck on long tasks. MACU has a manager decompose the task into a self-revising dependency graph and run parallel sub-agents, beating single agents by up to 25% and finishing 1.5x faster.

**Source:** HuggingFace Daily Papers
**Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01533) · [Wiki summary](../../agentic-systems/2026-06-02-multi-agent-computer-use.md)

```
Single CUA:  one serial agent ─► stuck on long-horizon tasks, slow
MACU:  Manager ─► decompose into DAG (nodes + dependencies)
              ─► dispatch PARALLEL sub-agents on ready frontier
              ◄─ findings (esp. non-re-observable info) returned & forwarded
              ─► continuously REVISE DAG (add / cancel / rewrite)
   ─► +3.4–25.5% on OSWorld / Online-Mind2Web / WebTailBench / Odysseys · ~1.5x faster (Odysseys)
```

**What is it about?**
Computer-use agents (CUAs, agents that operate a desktop or browser by clicking and typing) are usually run as a single serial agent. MACU argues for multi-agent computer use: a manager model decomposes the task into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of sub-tasks with dependencies, dispatches parallel CUA sub-agents on the graph's ready frontier, and continuously revises the graph as findings arrive.

**What problem does it solve?**
Single serial CUAs are bad at long-horizon tasks that benefit from decomposition, parallel execution, and re-planning, and they lose information they cannot re-observe (a transient popup, a one-time state). MACU treats partial observability as first-class, retaining and forwarding non-re-observable information through the manager and DAG.

**What is the core novelty?**
The self-revising DAG: the manager does not commit to a fixed plan, it adds, cancels, and rewrites nodes as sub-agents report back, while passing forward information downstream agents could not otherwise recover.

**Key takeaways**
- 3.4 to 25.5% improvement over strong single-agent baselines on OSWorld, Online-Mind2Web, WebTailBench, and Odysseys.
- About 1.5x faster average wall-clock on the long-horizon Odysseys benchmark.
- More favorable test-time scaling than single agents, and solves long-horizon tasks where single CUAs get stuck.
- Explicitly handles partial observability via forwarded findings.

**Gaps in the study**
Running parallel sub-agents multiplies token and compute cost; the accuracy gains are reported but the cost-per-task tradeoff is not foregrounded. The manager's DAG-revision quality is itself model-dependent and may not transfer to weaker managers.

**Industrial implication**
Computer-use agents are heading into production (NVIDIA and OpenAI both shipped Windows agent control this week), and MACU says the path to longer, more reliable automation is orchestration, not just a stronger single model. The caution comes from a same-day paper (below): coordinating agents helps at inference but is fragile to train end-to-end.

## Connecting the Dots

The clearest cross-day thread is that yesterday's "kill equal treatment" principle deepened along a new axis. On 06-01 the wiki named four papers (dMoE on experts, StateKV on attention, TA-OPD on distillation tokens, DRIFT on rollouts) all locating the sparse load-bearing part and spending only there. Today "Not Only Where, But When" (the RLVR paper that schedules credit allocation over training instead of fixing it) explicitly adds the time dimension that thread had left implicit: TIP (04-16, the finding that under 10% of distillation tokens carry signal) and TA-OPD (06-01, keep only teacher corrections the student can reach) answered *where* to spend signal; this paper answers *when*. That the title literally reads "not only where, but when" is the field articulating the wiki's own framing back at it.

Speculative decoding produced its own two-paper convergence, and both papers pointedly avoid the architectural moves that defined the area. Draft-OPD (on-policy distillation for the draft model, 5x lossless, +23% over EAGLE-3) fixes the drafter's *training*; SPD (pipeline-parallel speculation with zero idle stages) fixes its *scheduling*. The wiki's speculative-decoding page had tracked the field crossing architectural axes through April (embedded MTP heads in Nemotron 3 Super, cross-modal in SDVG). Today marks the shift from "build a better draft head" to "train it right and run it well," which is the same maturation signal the distillation pages went through weeks earlier.

Draft-OPD also ties the speculative-decoding thread back into the distillation thread directly. Its diagnosis (the drafter trains on a fixed offline distribution but is judged on its own on-policy blocks) is the identical covariate-shift problem the wiki logged in TA-OPD (06-01), DRIFT (06-01, exploiting that KL-regularized RL equals importance-weighted SFT), and DAgger-for-LLM-agents (05-14, on-policy distribution with dense teacher labels). On-policy distillation has now been applied to student models, multi-turn agents, and as of today the speculative-decoding drafter. Same lesson, three different consumers, which is exactly the ≥3-papers threshold for calling it a settled pattern: offline training distribution is the wrong distribution, fix it on-policy.

A smaller cluster formed around shorter reasoning. Geometric Latent Reasoning (reason as a path through embedding space) and the chunk-level off-the-shelf process-scorer paper (use a big model's likelihoods to steer a small model's chunks, no trained reward model) both report substantially shorter reasoning traces as a side effect, by completely different mechanisms. That is notable because the dominant inference-time-scaling literature pushes reasoning *longer*; two same-day papers cutting length while holding accuracy suggests the pendulum on test-time compute may be starting to swing toward density over volume.

Today's three agentic papers triangulate when multi-agent structure actually pays. Multi-Agent Computer Use (DAG of self-revising sub-agents) shows orchestration helps at inference, +3.4 to 25.5% and 1.5x faster. OpenWebRL (online multi-turn RL trains a single 4B web agent to near-proprietary quality) shows a well-trained single agent is a very strong baseline. And "When Does Multi-Agent RL Improve LLM Workflows?" is the cautionary middle: end-to-end multi-agent RL gains are conditional on workflow, task, and scale, and jointly training roles can fall off a terminal accuracy cliff. Read together: coordinate at inference, but be careful training the coordination, and do not assume it beats a strong single agent for free.

## Also today

- **NITP (Next Implicit Token Prediction)**: adds dense continuous supervision in representation space to pretraining, predicting the next token's implicit semantics from the model's own shallow layers; +5.7% MMLU-Pro on a 9B MoE at ~2% extra FLOPs and zero inference cost ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24956) · [summary](../../llms-foundation-models/2026-06-02-nitp-next-implicit-token-prediction.md)).
- **OpenWebRL**: open framework for online multi-turn RL on live websites; OpenWebRL-4B hits 67.0% on Online-Mind2Web with only 0.4K init trajectories, competitive with OpenAI and Gemini CUA ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02031)).
- **When Does Multi-Agent RL Improve LLM Workflows?**: end-to-end multi-agent RL gains depend jointly on workflow, task, and scale; isolated-policy training reaches higher peaks but more often falls off a terminal accuracy cliff ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24202)).
- **FineVerify**: decomposes a question into checkable sub-questions and verifies each candidate per-check; with 12 samples it lets GPT-5-mini surpass GPT-5 on BrowseComp-Plus ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00660)).
- **Chunk-Level Guided Generation (off-the-shelf process scorers)**: a big model scores a small model's fixed-length chunks by likelihood, no trained reward model; contrastive selection beats majority voting by up to 28 points and matches a 72B PRM, with shorter traces ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01682)).
- **LVSA**: training-free block-sparse attention for long-video diffusion, up to 3.17–3.33x compute reduction and enabling 2x-horizon generation that is otherwise out-of-memory, with a new loopy-video-aware evaluator ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31057)).
- **JAMEL**: trains agent memory and exploration jointly via deterministic novelty signals (code coverage in GUI), rivaling a closed-source model's exploration depth at lower token cost ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01533)).
