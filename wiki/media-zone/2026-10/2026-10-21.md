<h4>DeepInfer: the engine behind DeepSeek's API</h4>
<p>DeepSeek released the inference framework that runs its production service, with the stated goal of lowering compute and energy cost for everyone. ### PyTorchCon day one: the serving stack grows up

<div class="mz-feed">

<div class="mz-card">
<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge">IBM Research · PyTorch Conference</span>
<h4>llm-d 1.0: a stable API for day-two inference</h4>
<p>llm-d is the Kubernetes-native layer that turns vLLM or SGLang servers into a production service. Version 1.0 does not add new tricks. It makes the existing five work together under real traffic: prefix-cache-aware routing, KV cache offload across GPU, CPU and storage, batch optimization, prefill and decode disaggregation, and wide expert parallelism. The new pieces are flow control and fairness, so an agent's burst of requests cannot starve a human's chat, and multi-turn pinning that keeps a request on the replica holding its cache. Two numbers to keep: the prefix-aware router captured 97% of available cache reuse on an agentic coding workload, and IBM and Red Hat ran 3,000 concurrent software agents self-hosted for 5 to 10 times less than the API price of the same open model.</p>

### Consumer-GPU inference: the stack, not the card, sets the ceiling

<div class="mz-feed">

<div class="mz-card">
<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge">X thread · quietly high-signal off a small account</span>
<h4>Qwen3.8-27B at 381 tokens/sec on one RTX 3090</h4>
<p>A developer took a 27B model from 82 to 381 tokens a second on a 24 GB RTX 3090 without touching the hardware. The steps: multi-token prediction drafting (114 tok/s), DFlash2 speculative decoding plus context lookup (138 tok/s), then optimized lookup verification (381 tok/s). Context lookup is the key idea. When the expected output already exists in the prompt, as in extraction, document transformation or code edits, the engine proposes matching spans from the context as draft tokens and the model only verifies them. Verification blocks grew from the usual 7 tokens to 16, with about 15 of 16 accepted. The honest qualifier: ordinary chat runs closer to 133 tok/s. Prefix caching separately cut time to first token on a repeated 25K-token document from 22.4 seconds to 0.56.</p>

## LLMs, agents, safety

### Is the harness dead? The same fight, with better evidence on both sides

This is the reader's most-saved theme, and it flared again on the US day. The viral claim is that a paper titled "The End of Loop Engineering" shows pre-authored caps, budgets and exit conditions are all the same mistake. The rebuttal is that the claim rests on code benchmarks that models have already saturated.

<div class="dg-title">The "observed exit" loop the viral thread describes</div>
<div class="dg-sub">Nothing is pre-authored except the check. The run decides when it is done.</div>

- **The claim (contested).** Across 2,400 runs, the verifier-plus-three-state rule reportedly ended at the wrong moment 4% as often as a fixed cap, with 2,391 self-stops and 9 escalations. The thread links no paper and sells the author's own playbook. Treat the numbers as unverified until the arXiv id surfaces.
- **The rebuttal (influence).** Elvis Saravia: like "prompt engineering is dead" and "RAG is dead," this comes from a few papers with weak setups on benchmaxxed SWE tasks. Outside coding, harnesses are getting more specialized, not less: security, authorization, memory, cost, and the new wave of proactive personal agents (Muse, Grok Bot, dots, Instinct). DAIR is polling the question.
- **The practitioner answer (cross-source, YouTube).** Coinbase's Evan Kormos says the same thing from inside a regulated workflow: model processes as DAGs and loops, and a loop is only as trustworthy as its check. Agents graduate from fully supervised, to sampled, to humans handling only exceptions. That is the viral thread's verifier, with a compliance department attached.
- **The tooling answer.** Pi Durable (Hugging Face video) checkpoints every task and tool call to SQLite so an agent resumes after a crash, even running Qwen3.5-0.8B locally. Google's open Ambient quality agent reads live traces and points to the failing line. Hugging Face's Agent Manager orchestrates dozens of agents across Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode. All three are harness, not model.

### What to scale: agentic RL data, and agents from many developers

<div class="mz-feed">

<div class="mz-card">
<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge paper">arXiv 2610.23946 · NeurIPS 2026 · via DAIR</span>
<h4>Scaling laws for agentic RL data: environments beat tasks beat trajectories</h4>
<p>Pretraining treats data as one number, a token count. Agentic RL does not get that luxury, because synthesis pipelines generate tasks, environments and trajectories separately and at very different costs. This paper fits per-axis scaling laws across model sizes, families, four domains (math and code, SWE, web, UI and tools) and both PPO and GRPO, and the exponents order the same way every time: environment above task above trajectory. The environment advantage comes mainly from world diversity, meaning genuinely different settings. Varying the structure of one world helps about as much as adding tasks. The laws also price each synthesis method with a discount factor and a synthetic-to-real ratio at which training collapses, and those ratios differ by an order of magnitude across axes. A Chinchilla-style budget recipe gives up to 1.7x compute efficiency over the default of generating more trajectories.</p>

<div class="mz-card">
<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge paper">arXiv 2610.20518 · Princeton</span>
<h4>The Wealth of Agents: specialists from many developers beat one general agent</h4>
<p>Most agentic systems are built by one developer who controls every agent, so every team rebuilds the same skills. This paper simulates populations of developers, each building agents under a fixed budget, and compares lone systems with a multi-developer system that splits each task and routes the parts to different developers' agents. On GAIA, specialists scoring at most 35% alone reached 70% together, 12 points above the median general agent, at the same total development budget. On the search-only SealQA benchmark the combined system did worse and cost more. The practical lesson is to build agents that fill gaps in shared systems: one scoring 19% alone lifted the combined system from 40% to 53%.</p>
