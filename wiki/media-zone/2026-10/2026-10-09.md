### Decision models go open: routing becomes one forward pass

<div class="dg-title">A decision model scores options instead of writing an answer</div>
<div class="dg-sub">Why it is fast: no token-by-token decoding, and the output plugs straight into an if-statement.</div>

<span class="mz-card-badge">Repo · the day's most-shared routing artifact</span>
<h4>Laya: an Apache-2.0, local alternative to Jev</h4>
<p>Laya answers typed questions over unstructured state by scoring options, not generating text. It first picks an English or multilingual checkpoint in plain Python (a router before the model). Each answer option gets a [MASK] marker, a bidirectional encoder plus small head scores them, and softmax gives a distribution. All questions batch into one call. One test put it near 35 ms on a local GPU against roughly 380 ms for hosted Jev. The catch: Jev is zero-shot, Laya needs fine-tuning and calibration on your answer set, with uncertain cases sent to a bigger model.</p>

<h4>Unsloth: turn any small LLM into a decision model</h4>
<p>Unsloth swaps the language-model head for a classification ("Clef") head and fine-tunes with LoRA (r=64) for one epoch. Qwen3.5 0.8B went from about 21% to 74% aggregate accuracy across three decision benchmarks, on 4 GB of VRAM. Towards Data Science's most-read piece this week describes the same head-swap trick on Qwen. The cost angle: a sub-1B classifier replaces a frontier-model call for every routing or labeling fork.</p>

<h4>The largest agentic LLM inference trace dataset</h4>
<p>Shared by Hugging Face: 206B tokens across about 12,000 agent sessions, 1.19M LLM requests and 1.21M tool calls. Agent traffic is long shared prefixes, bursty tool waits and many short turns, which is exactly what KV cache (stored attention state) reuse, prefix caching and scheduling research needs and rarely has. Real traces at this scale let you test eviction and routing policies against actual agent workloads instead of chat benchmarks.</p>

<h4>Haiku 5.5: the playbook is caching and batching</h4>
<p>Anthropic's Haiku 5.5 lists at $0.10 / $0.50 per million tokens under 100K context, about 75% below Haiku 4.5. The practitioner thread's useful part is the cache arithmetic: cache reads cost $0.01 per million against $0.10 uncached, and prompts above 100K enter a pricier tier. So retrieve before you send, cache stable instructions, and batch anything that can wait. Pattern: Haiku as the bounded-job subagent feeding Sonnet or Opus.</p>

## LLMs, agents, safety

### Harnesses vs training: who improves the agent?

<div class="mz-feed">

<div class="mz-card">
<div class="mz-card-body">
<span class="mz-card-badge paper">Paper + code · automated AI research</span>
<h4>RSIGym: fix the harness before you train</h4>
<p>Evolvent's RSIGym gives an agent CPU-only containers plus budgeted remote services (LoRA fine-tuning, serving, benchmarking, sandboxes) and asks it to improve another model. Starting from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base with $500 of services per benchmark, Claude Opus 5 nearly tripled its SWE-bench Verified score. The practical lesson: heavier training scored lower in 8 of 10 small tests, and reading failure logs to fix the harness paid more. Cost angle: budget is a first-class axis of the benchmark.</p>

<h4>DeLM: drop the coordinator, share a task queue</h4>
<p>A Stanford paper replaces the central orchestrator agent with a shared context and task queue that agents pull from, posting discoveries and dead ends as they go. Built on Claude Code and Codex, it reports up to 2.49x faster runs and up to +19 points accuracy on Terminal-Bench 4.0, DeepSWE and ProgramBench within a 120-minute budget. Replies push back on the baselines (vanilla single agents), so treat the margin as an upper bound. Code and 720 trajectories are public.</p>
