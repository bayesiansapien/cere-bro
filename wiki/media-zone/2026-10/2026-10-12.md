<h4>THX-01: a 322M decision model with calibrated outputs</h4>
<p>HAL-X released THX-01, a non-generative model built on an mmBERT encoder with a small decision head. You give it a state (a ticket, email, document or agent trace) and typed questions. In one pass of about 10 ms it returns a choice, a yes/no probability, a score, an extracted number or a verbatim quote with citation. It is the smallest member yet of the decision-model category that formed on 10-11 (Microsoft-Decision-1, Liquid d1, Vega). Apache 2.0. The "beats Jev" claim is the authors' own.</p>

<h4>Speculative decoding, explained right</h4>
<p>A widely shared explainer walks through why speculative decoding works. Plain decoding reads all weights from memory for every token, so compute sits idle. A small draft model guesses several tokens ahead, and the big target model checks all of them in one pass. Each guess is accepted with probability min(1, p/q), where p is the target's probability and q the draft's. On the first rejection, the token is resampled from the leftover difference between the two distributions. That rule is why the output distribution matches the target model exactly, so the speedup is free in quality. The post points to Uzu, the Apple Silicon engine that hit 92 tokens/s on a 9B model (10-10). The Sunday newsletter also ran a speculative decoding explainer, so the topic is going mainstream.</p>

<h4>GitSwarm: Decentralized Compounding Inference (Meta Superintelligence Labs)</h4>
<p>Most extra inference compute goes into separate attempts, and when an attempt ends its partial work and dead ends are lost. GitSwarm gives hundreds of identical asynchronous agents one branchable Git repo. Each contribution is an atomic commit with an <code>informed_by=</code> field naming the earlier commits it builds on. With GPT-5.5 Codex workers it solved all 30 IMOProofBench-Advanced problems in one run on under 1M output tokens. On ProgramBench it scored 79.4% vs 65.1% for one Codex agent told to keep going at a similar budget, and it improves with more workers (73.1% at 100, 79.4% at 500). The key number for cost: 94.7% of contributions get built on, and 70.1% of failed research commits are cited by later accepted work, so failures are not wasted spend. The open question is the wall-clock and dollar cost of 500 frontier workers compared with a better-routed single agent.</p>

<h4>Language Models as Research World Models (Amazon)</h4>
<p>Research agents can propose experiments much faster than anyone can pay to run them. This paper asks an LLM to predict an experiment's gain before it runs. It is tested on 2,600+ real experiment records from nine setups (pretraining, post-training, inference) representing 171K H100-hours. Feeding in past records from the same setup raised ranking correlation from 0.506 to 0.774. Pretraining experience from OLMo3, Marin and Nanochat cut selection regret on Qwen3 by 78%. Across 13 models, adding experimental history helped more than switching to a stronger model or raising reasoning effort. The takeaway: log every run, failures included, because the log is what makes the next GPU hour pay off. It pairs with GitSwarm and with Meta FAIR's research-preference-model line (2608.13940).</p>

<h4>MASS: a model that trains on its own best team runs (Sakana)</h4>
<p>Sakana's Multi-Agent Self-Supervision (with UC Berkeley) had the day's most-shared research post.
