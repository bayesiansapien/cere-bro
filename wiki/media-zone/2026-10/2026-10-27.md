<h4>HarnessMM: stop paying the model to re-decide</h4>
<p>Agent harnesses call the model again after every tool result, even when an earlier call already fixed the next step. HarnessMM (Tsinghua and Microsoft Research Asia) stores those settled steps in an Executable Decision Graph: the exact action, its literal arguments, what it depends on, and a guard condition. When the guard holds, a controller runs the stored action without a model call; anything new goes back to the model. A self-improvement loop tunes the harness configuration from training feedback while model weights stay frozen. On a DeepSeek harness, tokens per task fall from 96.3k to 11.6k (an 88% cut) while success rises from 71% to 86%; Codex on HumanEval keeps 90.8% success with 30% fewer tokens. This is decision caching, a sibling of KV and prompt caching one layer up. Picked by DAIR Academy and pushed by Elvis Saravia, the day's clearest cost result.</p>

<h4>Why temperature 0 still gives different answers</h4>
<p>Avi Chawla walks through a known result: Thinking Machines sent one prompt to Qwen3-235B 1,000 times at temperature 0 and got 80 different completions. Greedy decoding is deterministic; the logits are not. Floating-point addition depends on order, and batched inference changes the reduction order in kernels with batch size. So the same prompt can get slightly different logits depending on what else shares the GPU. The fix is batch-invariant kernels, at some throughput cost. Worth knowing before trusting any temperature-0 eval to repeat.</p>

### Simple beats elaborate in agent plumbing

<h4>MCP faults: a 120B model is no tougher than a 20B one</h4>
<p>MCP-Injector is a proxy that sits on the tool-call path and injects realistic failures. Its fault mix comes from 57,240 issue and PR threads across 1,774 open-source MCP servers, so it mirrors how tools really break (mostly permanent faults). Across 1,350 runs with gpt-oss-20B and 120B and three agent stacks, the task score falls 60%, from 8.08 to 3.23 out of 10. The 120B keeps no larger share of its fault-free score (p = 0.96), and its completion rate under faults is slightly lower. The stack mattered more than the model size. Lesson: put fault injection in your evals and spend on error handling, not a bigger model.</p>

<h4>Raw-RAG: plain retrieval over chat history beats agent memory</h4>
<p>Raw-RAG does the simple thing: dense retrieval plus reranking over the raw conversation, then hand the passages to the reader model. It ranks first in six of seven benchmark-model settings across LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MEME and MemoryAgentBench, beating the best agentic memory by 0.1 to 5.5 points. The reason: an answer often depends on links between facts (what changed, when, what it affects). Turning chats into separate memory notes breaks those links. Even full access to the transformed stores leaves a gap against raw evidence. The 10-24 digest covered the paper; today it trended, alongside yesterday's Jev-Mem (a fast scorer handles memory writes). Both say the same thing: keep the expensive model out of the memory bookkeeping.</p>
